ESTA vs B-1/B-2 Visa: Which US Entry Route Do You Need?

Your passport decides the answer more than your preference does. This comparison lays ESTA and the B-1/B-2 visitor visa side by side — eligibility, cost, permitted stay, work rules and how to apply — so you pick the right route the first time.

Quick Answer

If your passport is from one of the 42 Visa Waiver Program countries (including the UK), you need an approved ESTA — $40.27, up to 90 days, no extension. If it is not (India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Philippines, Bangladesh and most other nationalities), you need a B-1/B-2 visitor visa — $185, DS-160 form, an embassy interview, and commonly up to 6 months per admission. You do not get to choose between them: eligibility is decided by nationality.

How to use this comparison

Start with the only question that matters: what passport do you hold? If it is from a Visa Waiver Program country, ESTA is your route — a B-1/B-2 visa application would be unnecessary and slower. If it is not, the B-1/B-2 visa is your route — ESTA is simply unavailable to you, whatever a blog or agent suggests. The detailed comparison below matters most for VWP-country travellers weighing whether ESTA's 90-day, no-extension terms are enough for their plans, and for everyone who wants to understand exactly what each route authorizes.

What ESTA actually is (and isn't)

ESTA — the Electronic System for Travel Authorization — is not a visa. It is a pre-travel authorization for citizens of the 42 countries in the Visa Waiver Program (VWP), including the United Kingdom. It costs $40.27 total as of 2026 ($10.27 application charge plus $30 payable on approval), is applied for only at esta.cbp.dhs.gov, and should be obtained at least 72 hours before departure — carriers check it before boarding.

An approved ESTA permits tourism or business visits of up to 90 days. The two hard limits: no extension and no change to another immigration status while in the US. If your plans might need more than 90 days, or might turn into study or work, ESTA is the wrong tool even when you qualify for it. Every traveller needs their own ESTA, children included — there is no family authorization. VWP nationals may also transit the US on an approved ESTA, since the country has no sterile international transit.

What the B-1/B-2 visa is

The B-1/B-2 is a genuine visitor visa issued by a US embassy or consulate: B-1 covers business visits, B-2 covers tourism, and the two are very often issued as a combined B-1/B-2. It is the route for travellers from India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Philippines, Bangladesh and all other non-VWP nationalities. The application means completing Form DS-160, paying the $185 machine-readable-visa fee (as of 2026), and attending an in-person interview — the step that drives the real timeline.

The visa is often issued for up to 10 years with multiple entries, but that is the visa's validity, not your stay: each admission is decided separately by the CBP officer at the port of entry, commonly up to 6 months and recorded on the electronic I-94 (check it at i94.cbp.dhs.gov after arrival). Interview waits are the binding constraint — the State Department's July 2026 table showed roughly 8.5–10.5 months at Indian posts, 7.5–8 months in Pakistan, and about 2 months in London — so high-demand applicants should plan 6–12 months ahead.

When ESTA is not enough

Even VWP-country citizens should apply for a B-1/B-2 visa instead of ESTA when: the trip may run longer than 90 days; there is any chance of needing to extend or change status inside the US; the purpose shades toward work or study rather than a genuine visit; or the traveller is ineligible for the VWP (for example, certain dual nationals or prior immigration violations — check CBP's eligibility rules). A B-1/B-2 visa also gives a cleaner paper trail for frequent travellers: each admission is documented on the I-94, and the visa's multi-year validity removes the per-trip authorization step.

The honest test: if any part of your plan contains the words "maybe I'll stay longer" or "I might look for work while I'm there," ESTA's absolute 90-day cap and no-change-of-status rule make it the wrong choice. Apply for the visa.

When B-1/B-2 is the only route

For non-VWP nationalities there is no decision to make — the B-1/B-2 is the only visitor route, and the work is about timing and evidence, not choice. Book the interview as early as the travel window allows (6–12 months ahead at high-demand posts), prepare the file around the question officers actually ask — INA 214(b), will this person return home? — and keep the story consistent across the DS-160, the interview answers and the documents: stable employment with approved leave, property or lease, family ties, prior compliant travel, and bank statements showing the trip is funded without US work.

Note what the $185 fee does not buy: it covers the application, not approval, and most refusals cite 214(b) — a judgment about ties abroad, not a paperwork error. Vague itineraries ("two months, visiting friends, not sure where") read as risk; a dated plan with bookings and a return ticket reads as a genuine visit. Costly mistakes to avoid

  • Paying a copycat site for ESTA. The official application is $40.27 at esta.cbp.dhs.gov; third-party sites charge large markups for submitting the same form.
  • Assuming you can "choose" ESTA. Travellers from India, Pakistan, Nigeria and other non-VWP countries cannot use ESTA at any price — applying on a lookalike site buys nothing but a lighter wallet.
  • Reading a 10-year B-1/B-2 visa as a 10-year stay. Visa validity and admitted stay are different things — the I-94 date governs, commonly up to 6 months.
  • Entering on ESTA to job-hunt or "sort things out." VWP entrants cannot extend or change status; the 90 days are absolute, and misrepresentation at entry is a separate ground of inadmissibility.
  • Booking non-refundable travel before the interview when your post's wait is 8+ months.
  • Working remotely "because the employer is abroad." On either route, physical presence while working is what counts — a B-1/B-2 or ESTA never authorizes employment.

What to verify before paying or booking

Confirm your nationality's route on travel.state.gov — VWP list for ESTA, visitor-visa pages for B-1/B-2. Check the Global Visa Wait Times tool for your consulate before booking anything non-refundable. Verify the $185 MRV / $40.27 ESTA figures on the live fee pages, since fees change by regulation. Keep six months' passport validity beyond your stay (unless your country is exempt — the UK is). And if your plans later shift toward study, that is a different process entirely: F-1 status needs SEVIS registration ($350 I-901, as of 2026) and a Form I-20 from the school — see our US student visa guide. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I choose between ESTA and a B-1/B-2 visa?

Only if your passport qualifies for the Visa Waiver Program. VWP-country citizens may use ESTA or apply for a B-1/B-2 visa instead (useful for longer stays or complex plans). Travellers from non-VWP countries — India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Philippines, Bangladesh and most others — cannot use ESTA at all; the B-1/B-2 visa is their only visitor route.

Is ESTA a visa?

No. ESTA is a travel authorization under the Visa Waiver Program, not a visa. It lets citizens of 42 countries board a carrier to the US for visits up to 90 days. Admission itself is still decided by the CBP officer at the port of entry, exactly as with a visa.

How long can I stay on ESTA versus a B-1/B-2 visa?

ESTA admissions are capped at 90 days with no extension and no change of status. B-1/B-2 admissions are set per visit by the CBP officer — commonly up to 6 months — and recorded on the electronic I-94, which is the legal limit regardless of the visa's multi-year validity.

Can I work in the US on ESTA or a B-1/B-2 visa?

No — neither route authorizes employment in any form, including remote work for a foreign employer while physically in the United States, or unpaid work at a family business. Working requires a work-authorized immigration category.

What does ESTA cost compared with the B-1/B-2 visa?

ESTA costs $40.27 total as of 2026 ($10.27 application charge plus $30 on approval), applied for online with no interview. The B-1/B-2 visa costs $185 (MRV fee, as of 2026), plus the time cost of the DS-160 and an embassy interview — with waits from weeks to over a year depending on the post.

Can I extend my stay if I entered on ESTA?

No. VWP/ESTA admissions cannot be extended and cannot be changed to another status — you must leave within 90 days. Overstaying can void your VWP privileges and trigger 3- or 10-year re-entry bars. B-1/B-2 holders, by contrast, may apply to USCIS for an extension before their I-94 expires.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

Check your passport first: VWP country means ESTA ($40.27, 90 days, no extension, no interview); anything else means the B-1/B-2 visa ($185, DS-160, interview, commonly up to 6 months per admission). Pick ESTA for short, simple visits and the visa for longer stays, complex plans, or any hint your trip might outgrow 90 days. Neither route permits work — and on both, the CBP officer, not the document, sets your stay.
