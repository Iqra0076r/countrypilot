Portugal Startup Visa 2026: IAPMEI & the Incubator Gate

Portugal outsourced its startup judgment to the market: it is not the immigration authority that decides whether your idea is good enough, it is a certified incubator that has to bet its own reputation on you. IAPMEI writes the rules and checks the paperwork, but the signature that unlocks the visa belongs to an incubator. Everything in this guide flows from that one design choice.

Portugal's StartUP Visa (Despacho Normativo n.º 4/2018, under Portaria n.º 344/2017) hosts non-Schengen entrepreneurs who sign an incubation contract with an IAPMEI-certified incubator. There is no minimum investment; instead each founder needs 12× the 2026 IAS (€6,445.56) in own funds, and the project must be able to reach €325,000/year in turnover or assets within five years. IAPMEI decides within about 30 working days, the consulate issues the residence visa, and AIMA grants a 2-year permit renewable in 3-year blocks. The incubator's acceptance — not the state's — is the real test.

The incubator is the gatekeeper, not IAPMEI

Read the legal text and you might think Portugal runs a founder visa like any other: the entrepreneur applies to IAPMEI, the agency for competitiveness and innovation, which validates the file. But the rules — Legislative Order No. 4/2018 of 2 February, which IAPMEI still applies in its revised 2021 form — make one requirement the load-bearing wall of the entire programme: the founder must sign an incubation contract with a certified incubator, and the certified incubator must first declare its interest in hosting the project.

This reverses the usual power dynamic. In Spain's entrepreneur route, the state (ENISA) judges the innovation. In Portugal, the judgment is made by people who see hundreds of pitches — incubators and accelerators certified by IAPMEI under Regulation 344/2017. Their assessment runs in two stages per the programme's own framework: first an evaluation of interest in hosting the project, judged on the innovative nature of the project, its growth potential, its scalability in the market, and the founders' plans to stay in Portugal after the programme; then the formal contract.

The practical consequence is brutal and simple: you can have a perfect file and still have no application, because no incubator has chosen you. Founders who treat the incubator as a stamp — "I'll pay the desk fee, get the declaration, and work from home" — misunderstand the instrument. The contract is usually a real, paid relationship with desk space, mentoring, and programme obligations, and consulates and AIMA can ask for evidence of that relationship at renewal. Choose the incubator like a business partner, because that is what the programme thinks it is.

Who the rules actually let in

The eligibility requirements are written in Article 5 of the legislative order, and they read less like a visa checklist and more like a venture thesis. On the applicant side: no permanent residence in the Schengen area, compliance with tax and social-security obligations, no criminal record, at least 18 years old, and the means test we will calculate below. On the project side: a real interest in developing an innovative, technology-and-knowledge-based company in Portugal; production of goods or services aimed at internationalisation; the potential to create qualified employment beyond the founders themselves; and the potential to reach the €325,000 threshold we will get to in a moment.

Up to five entrepreneurs can apply jointly for one project, and the order adds an important phrase: the individual contribution of each candidate must be essential to the development of the project. Decorative co-founders — the friend added to look like a team — fail this test by definition.

The rules also cover founders who already have a business abroad (established less than four years ago) and want to deploy it in Portugal, as well as founders who have not created any company yet. Both must meet the same innovation and scale tests, and both must go through the same incubator.

The €6,445.56 means test: 2026 IAS math

Portugal does not set a "show us €50,000" founder wealth test. Instead the order requires own financial and livelihood resources equivalent to twelve times the Social Support Index — the IAS. The IAS is set annually by government portaria; for 2026 it is €537.13, fixed by Portaria n.º 480-A/2025/1 of 30 December (Diário da República), up 2.8% from the €522.50 of 2025. Twelve times €537.13 comes to €6,445.56 per founder, evidenced by a bank document.

Note three things about this figure. First, it applies per founder — a team of three needs to show three times that amount between them, in each founder's name. Second, it is personal subsistence money, not business capital; it sits in your account and proves you will not become a burden on the state while the startup earns nothing. Third, it is an annual benchmark that moves with the IAS, so older guides quoting €6,270 or other figures from earlier years are simply out of date.

Note: this means test is separate from the consulate's general means-of-subsistence scale (currently around €920/month for the main applicant) and from any funds the incubator itself may require. Budget for all three, not just the headline figure.

The €325,000 target: what five years really tests

The number founders argue about most is in Article 5(2)(f): the project must have the potential to reach a turnover of more than €325,000 per year and/or an asset value of more than €325,000, within five years of the start of the incubation contract. It is not a deposit. It is not an investment. It is a forecast horizon — and it tells you exactly what Portugal is buying with this visa: companies that might matter.

Evaluated honestly, this is the scale filter. A freelancing collective, a local-services business, or a lifestyle consultancy will not credibly project €325,000 a year in five years, and the incubators know it. A deep-tech product, a platform with network effects, a tradable-goods manufacturer — those can make the case. The order explicitly lists the assessment dimensions: degree of innovation, scalability of the business, market potential, capacity of the management team, potential for creating qualified employment in Portugal, and the relevance of each applicant in the team.

Write your projection to survive that scrutiny. Vague "we will capture 1% of a €30 billion market" slides are the Portuguese equivalent of Spain's "innovative platform" copy — the incubators have seen thousands of them. What persuades is unit economics: customers, pricing, channels, and a hiring plan that explains how the company creates qualified Portuguese jobs on the way to the target.

The two-stage application: declaration first, consulate second

Stage one happens on IAPMEI's StartUP Visa online platform, in Portuguese or English. You register the project and apply to one or more certified incubators. If an incubator declares interest, you sign the incubation contract — and then IAPMEI decides, in about 30 working days, whether the file complies. If the decision is positive, IAPMEI issues the declaration certifying the incubation contract: the document that makes you a "StartUP Visa entrepreneur" in the eyes of the state.

Stage two happens at the Portuguese consulate in your country of residence. The consular file is a standard residence-visa file with one extraordinary document inside it: the IAPMEI declaration. Alongside the form, photos, passport, travel insurance, criminal-record certificate, and means-of-subsistence evidence, that declaration replaces the business evidence an ordinary entrepreneur-visa (D2) applicant would have to argue from scratch. The consulate has its own decision deadline — around 60 days — but it is judging your identity and admissibility, not your business. The business was already judged by the incubator.

After entry, the route continues at AIMA, the agency for integration, migration and asylum, which formalises the residence permit. The realistic end-to-end calendar: one to three months for the incubator stage, about a month for IAPMEI, two to four months for the consulate, then AIMA after arrival. Six to eight months, start to finish, if nothing stalls.

The permit after arrival — and the quarterly reports nobody warns you about

The residence permit issued after arrival is initially valid for 2 years, renewable for further 3-year periods under article 89(4) of the foreigners act (Lei n.º 23/2007) — the provision that creates this residence basis for entrepreneurial projects hosted in certified incubators. Five years of legal permits puts you in range of permanent residence under the ordinary rules.

The part founders are never warned about is the monitoring. Entrepreneurs and incubators must submit quarterly reports to IAPMEI assessing the project's progress. This is not a formality — it is the mechanism by which the programme checks that the company you promised is the company you are building. Renewal at AIMA can involve evidence of the ongoing incubator relationship: that you are still working from the incubator, still developing the project, still in the country.

And the failure mode is structural: the programme's validity is the duration of the incubation contract. If the project collapses and the incubator relationship ends, the immigration basis ends with it. This is a visa for a project, not a visa for a person — unlike a passive-income permit, it does not survive the death of its own premise. Founders choosing between Portugal's Startup Visa and its ordinary entrepreneur route (the D2) should weigh this honestly: the D2 judges your business once and then your permit is yours; the Startup Visa buys you ecosystem support and a smoother consular path, but the project must stay alive to keep the permit alive.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do I need personally for Portugal's Startup Visa in 2026?

Each founder must show own financial and livelihood resources of 12 times the 2026 Social Support Index (IAS) — €6,445.56 per person, evidenced by a bank document. This is personal means of subsistence, separate from any business capital, and it scales with each founder on the application.

Is there a minimum investment for the Portugal Startup Visa?

No capital minimum exists in the rules. Instead the project must show the potential to reach €325,000 per year in turnover and/or asset value within five years of the incubation contract — the target is scale, not money invested today.

Who actually approves my project — IAPMEI or the incubator?

Both, in sequence. A certified incubator must first declare interest and sign an incubation contract with you — without that, there is no application. IAPMEI then validates the file and issues the declaration you take to the consulate. The incubator's acceptance is the real competitive hurdle; IAPMEI's review is largely a compliance check.

How long does the Portugal Startup Visa process take?

Expect one to three months for the incubator stage (pitching, declaration of interest, signing the contract), about 30 working days for IAPMEI's decision, and then the consular residence-visa phase. Six to eight months end to end is realistic.

What residence permit do I get after arriving in Portugal?

After entering on the residence visa, AIMA issues the residence permit — initially for 2 years, renewable for further 3-year periods under article 89(4) of the foreigners act (Lei n.º 23/2007), as long as you remain in the incubation programme and meet the conditions.

Can I apply with co-founders?

Yes — up to five entrepreneurs can apply jointly for one project. Each founder must individually meet the eligibility requirements, including the €6,445.56 means test, and the official rules require that each founder's contribution be essential to the project.

What happens if my startup fails during the programme?

The programme runs on the incubation contract, and incubators must submit quarterly progress reports to IAPMEI. If the project collapses and the incubation relationship ends, the immigration basis falls away too — the permit is tied to the programme, not to you personally.

Bottom Line

Portugal's Startup Visa is a venture-capital-shaped immigration product: no capital minimum, but the incubator plays the investor and IAPMEI plays the lawyer. Your job is to be fundable — €325,000 in five years, credible team, real technology — and to show €6,445.56 per founder in 2026 means. Win the incubator's interest and the state machinery (IAPMEI's declaration, the consulate's visa, AIMA's 2-year permit) mostly follows. Lose it, and there is no application at all.
