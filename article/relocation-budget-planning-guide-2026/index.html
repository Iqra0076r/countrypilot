Relocation Budget Planning 2026: Cost Your International Move

Most relocation budgets fail in the same place: they price the dream — the flight, the apartment, the new life — and forget the machinery that gets you there. This is a planner's method for costing the whole move, line by line, before the dream spends your money for you.

The short version: A serious relocation budget has seven blocks: (1) government-set costs — visa fees and service charges, which are non-refundable; (2) the physical move — flights, freight, first-night accommodation; (3) housing — deposits and overlap rent; (4) proof-of-funds reserves you must hold but not spend; (5) insurance you must buy before you arrive; (6) the fees on moving your own money across borders; and (7) a 10–15% contingency. Price them in that order and the total stops surprising you.

Ask ten people what their international move cost and you will get ten numbers that disagree with each other by a factor of two. The disagreement is not about destinations — it is about what got counted. The flight got counted; the visa service-centre fee did not. The rent got counted; the overlapping month of rent back home did not. The deposit got counted; the six weeks it took to get it back did not.

This guide fixes that with a method rather than a number. Nobody can tell you what your move costs without knowing your route, your family size, and your visa — but everyone can build the budget the same way, from the costs you cannot negotiate down to the buffer that absorbs everything else. Where figures below are official — visa fees, settlement-fund thresholds, insurance minimums — they are dated and sourced. Where the figure depends on you, the guide tells you how to find it instead of inventing one.

Start with the costs governments set for you

Begin where you have no leverage: the fees set by states. These are the cheapest lines in most budgets and the most commonly undercounted, because there are always two of them — the consular fee and the service charge — and people budget one.

The consular fee is the price of the decision itself. A Schengen short-stay visa costs €90 for adults under the European Commission's fee schedule, in force since 11 June 2024. Japan raised its standard visa issuance fees from 1 July 2026 — Japanese embassies announced single-entry visas moving from ¥3,000 to ¥15,000, the first revision since 1978. Both fees share one brutal property: they are not refunded if the application is refused. Budget every visa fee as spent the moment you apply, and if anyone in the family might need to reapply, budget it twice.

Then add the service layer. Most consulates outsource collection to centres like VFS Global or BLS, which charge their own handling fee per applicant — separate from the consular fee, paid regardless of outcome. Add document costs: translations, notarizations, police clearances, medical exams where required. None of these are large individually; together, for a family of four, they routinely clear several hundred euros or dollars before a single bag is packed. List them per person, not per household, because that is how they are charged.

The move itself: flights, freight, and the first-night problem

The physical move has three sub-budgets, and the middle one is where money quietly dies. Flights are the easy part: price them early, price one-way versus return (some visas and airlines effectively require onward travel — check before you save money the wrong way), and remember that moving season — June to September — prices like moving season.

Freight is the decision that deserves a spreadsheet of its own. Get a real shipping quote — door-to-door, including customs clearance and destination handling — then price the same inventory new at your destination. The honest rule: for moves of a year or two, shipping almost never wins unless you own high-value items that are genuinely expensive to replace. Sentimental value is real but it is not a financial argument; put it in the budget as what it is, a choice, not a necessity. Most successful movers land in the middle: sell the replaceable, ship the irreplaceable and the costly-to-rebuy, and budget the rebuying as a line item instead of discovering it in month two.

Then the first-night problem: you will pay for accommodation before you have an address. Budget two to four weeks of temporary housing — a serviced apartment, an Airbnb, a hotel — at tourist prices, not resident prices. This is also the window where deposits, SIM cards, and basic furnishing all land at once, which is why the first month abroad routinely costs double the second. Anyone whose budget shows month one equal to month six has not finished the budget.

Housing math: deposits, overlap months, and the double-rent trap

Housing is the largest line in most relocation budgets and the one people price most optimistically. The rent figure you found online is the beginning of the housing cost, not the end of it. Deposits commonly run one to three months' rent depending on the market, and in some countries the move-in cost stacks a deposit, the first month, and various key or agency fees into a single payment worth several months of rent. Research your specific market's conventions; the variance between countries is enormous.

The double-rent trap is the silent killer: the month (or two) where you are paying for the old place and the new one simultaneously. Lease end dates and arrival dates rarely align neatly, notice periods run longer than people remember, and deposits from the old place take weeks to come back. Budget at least one full month of overlap rent, and treat any deposit refund as a pleasant surprise rather than planned income. If you are breaking a lease early, price the penalty too — it is a real cost of the move, not a separate misfortune.

Furnishing deserves its own line even if the flat comes "furnished," because furnished rarely means equipped: kitchenware, bedding, cleaning supplies, and the hundred small things that make a flat livable routinely cost a month's rent in the first weeks. Price a starter kit honestly and the number stops being a shock.

The proof-of-funds trap: money you need but cannot spend

Here is the budget line that breaks the most plans: the money you must have but must not spend. Many visa and residence routes require you to demonstrate settlement funds — money available to support yourself on arrival — and that money has to still be there when the officer looks. Spend your proof of funds on the move itself and you can afford the relocation but fail the visa. It is the cruelest arithmetic in immigration: the poorer your budget looks after paying for everything, the more money you need to show.

Canada's Express Entry system publishes the clearest version of this. Its official settlement-funds table — updated 7 July 2025 on the IRCC website — asks a single applicant to show at least CAD $15,263 and a family of four at least CAD $28,362, available and unencumbered at application and on landing. That money is a floor, not a budget: it sits underneath your spending, untouched, while you pay for everything in this guide from funds above it. Other countries set the bar differently — some by income thresholds, some by consular discretion — but the structural point is universal. Identify your route's funds requirement early, ring-fence it mentally, and build the entire move budget from what remains.

Note: Proof-of-funds figures move with exchange rates and annual updates. Check the current official table for your route in the month you apply — last year's number is not this year's number.

Insurance: the budget line people add last and need first

Health cover is routinely the last line added to a relocation budget and one of the first expenses incurred — and in several routes it is a visa condition, not an option. Japan's digital nomad visa requires private medical travel insurance with at least ¥10 million in medical-treatment coverage before you apply, per the Immigration Services Agency. Schengen visa applicants need travel medical insurance of at least €30,000 covering the whole zone. These are not suggestions; applications fail without them.

Beyond the visa requirement, there is the coverage gap: the weeks or months before you qualify for any public system at your destination. Price international private medical insurance for the transition period — for a family, the premium can rival a month of rent — and do not assume your home country's cover follows you. It usually does not, or does so with exclusions that make it useless. The insurance line belongs near the top of the budget, next to the visa fees, because like the visa fees, it gates everything after it.

Moving the money costs money: the transfer-fee bleed

A cost almost nobody budgets: the price of moving your own money across borders. Deposits, first months' rent, and setup costs at the destination are usually paid from funds that start in your home country, and every transfer method takes a cut — through the exchange-rate margin, the flat fee, or both. On a single large transfer the bleed is an annoyance; across a dozen transfers during a move — deposit here, rent there, a car, school fees — it becomes a line item worth hundreds.

The defence is boring and effective: compare the total cost (fee plus exchange margin) of two or three transfer providers on your actual amounts before the move, not during it, and batch transfers where the timing allows rather than dripping money across weekly. Large one-off sums deserve the most comparison — that is where a one-percent margin difference is real money.

The 10–15% buffer rule

Every relocation budget ever written has been wrong, and the direction is always the same. The working standard among professional relocation planners is a contingency of 10–15% on top of the total — not as pessimism, but as recognition that moves contain unknowns by definition: the delayed shipment, the extra week of temporary housing, the document that needs re-issuing, the exchange rate that moves against you between budgeting and paying.

The buffer has a second job that matters for visas: it keeps you above proof-of-funds thresholds when currencies move. If your required funds are denominated in a different currency from your savings, a thin margin can evaporate in a bad month of exchange rates. Budget to the threshold plus a cushion, not to the threshold itself. A budget with no buffer is not a budget; it is a wish with arithmetic.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do I need to relocate abroad in 2026?

There is no single figure — it depends on destination, family size, and visa type. As reference points: Canada's immigration authority asks Express Entry applicants to show at least CAD $15,263 for a single person and CAD $28,362 for a family of four as available settlement funds, and that money must remain unspent. Build your own budget from visa fees, travel, housing deposits, insurance, setup costs, and a 10–15% buffer.

What are the most commonly forgotten relocation costs?

Visa application service-centre fees on top of consular fees, overlapping rent during the transition month, housing deposits that take weeks to recover, international money-transfer fees on large sums, and the first month of private health insurance before any public coverage begins.

Are visa fees refunded if my application is refused?

No. Consular visa fees — such as the €90 Schengen fee or Japan's visa issuance fees — are not refunded on refusal, and service-centre handling charges are separate and also non-refundable. Budget the fee as spent the moment you apply.

Should I ship my belongings or sell everything and rebuy?

Get a shipping quote first, then price the same items new at your destination. For moves under a year or two, shipping rarely wins unless you own high-value items that are expensive to replace. Sell, ship only what is irreplaceable or costly to rebuy, and budget the rebuying as a real line item rather than an afterthought.

How much buffer should I add to a relocation budget?

A 10–15% contingency on top of the total is the working standard among relocation planners. Exchange-rate movement alone can erase a thinner margin: if your proof-of-funds threshold is in another currency, keep a cushion above the minimum rather than budgeting to the exact line.

Do I need health insurance before I arrive?

Usually yes, and sometimes it is a visa condition. Japan's digital nomad visa requires at least ¥10 million in medical coverage before you apply, and Schengen visas require travel medical insurance of at least €30,000. Price the policy before you commit to the move — for a family it can rival a month of rent.

Bottom Line

Price the move in the order the money actually leaves: government fees first, the physical move second, housing third, the untouchable proof-of-funds reserve fourth, insurance fifth, transfer costs sixth — and then add 10–15% for everything you cannot foresee. Do that honestly and the total will be larger than you hoped and smaller than the surprise would have been.
