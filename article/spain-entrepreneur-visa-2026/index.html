Spain's Entrepreneur Visa 2026: The ENISA Startup Route

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished 15 October 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026

Spain's startup route is the odd one out among European founder visas: the law sets no investment floor at all, but hands a single state innovation agency the power to make or break your case. If you can pass ENISA's innovation test, the paperwork is fast and the first card lasts three years. If you can't, no amount of money saves you.

Spain's entrepreneur visa (articles 68–70 of Law 14/2013) lets non-EU founders live and work in Spain on an innovative project approved by a favourable ENISA report. There is no minimum investment, the first permit runs 3 years, the UGE-CE must decide within 20 days (silence counts as approval), and family members can join under the same law. The ENISA report — not your capital — is the real gate.

Why Spain judges the idea, not the bank account

Most "startup visas" hide a capital test behind the word "startup." Spain does the opposite. The Ministry of Inclusion's entrepreneur page states, plainly and in bold, that no existen mínimos de inversión ni de creación de empleo — there are no minimum investment or job-creation figures for this route. That sentence is the whole philosophy of the Spanish system, and it explains both its generosity and its difficulty.

The law (Law 14/2013, the entrepreneurs and internationalisation law) targets one thing: an entrepreneurial activity that is innovative or of special economic interest to Spain. A €60,000 SaaS tool with a genuinely new technical approach can qualify. A €2 million restaurant chain cannot — not because the money is wrong, but because the innovation test is binary. You either have it or you don't, and no wallet changes the answer.

This is also what separates the entrepreneur visa from its neighbours. Spain abolished the investor — the "golden visa" — figure of the same law effective 3 April 2025, so there is no longer a buy-your-way-in alternative. The digital nomad visa, meanwhile, is for people paid from outside Spain; the entrepreneur visa is for people building inside Spain. And the self-employed route (cuenta propia, the general regime) exists for conventional businesses — cafés, consultancies, shops — that would never pass ENISA but are perfectly legitimate economic activity. Picking the wrong one of these is the single most common error we see, and it is decided at the application stage, not fixed later.

One more consequence of the no-minimum design: your financing still gets examined. ENISA's official assessment criteria include the required investment and its financing sources — not to check that the number is big enough, but to check that the money you describe can credibly deliver the project you describe. A plan that needs €400,000 of hardware backed by "personal savings, details to follow" fails the coherence test even though no legal minimum exists.

The ENISA report: three questions that decide everything

ENISA — Empresa Nacional de Innovación, the state innovation company — does not grant your visa. It issues one document: a favourable report on your entrepreneurial activity. But that document is mandatory (de carácter preceptivo), the UGE-CE requests it automatically when you file, and without it the authorization cannot be granted. Treat the report as the application and everything else as administration.

On its official entrepreneur page, the ministry lists exactly what ENISA evaluates, and it comes down to three things:

1. Your professional profile and your real involvement in the project. ENISA looks at who you are and what you personally bring. If there are several partners, it evaluates each one's participation — including partners who are not applying for a visa themselves. A famous co-founder who appears in the pitch deck but nowhere in the operational plan weakens the file rather than strengthening it.

2. The business plan — project, product or service, and financing. The plan must describe what you are building and how it is funded, including the required investment and its possible financing sources. This is where most reports are won or lost: thin descriptions of "an innovative platform" with no architecture, no differentiation, and no economics are read as advertising copy, not innovation evidence.

3. The value added to the Spanish economy. The project must generate innovation or investment opportunities that benefit Spain specifically. A project that would be identical anywhere on earth, merely hosted in Spain for lifestyle reasons, scores poorly here. Something in the project — the technology, the market, the jobs, the ecosystem linkages — has to belong to Spain.

When it files, the UGE requests the ENISA report ex officio, and the law gives ENISA ten working days to produce it. Practically, this is the fastest leg of the process — and it means your case is effectively judged within two weeks of filing, before any consular appointment is booked.

The 20-day clock — and the silence that works in your favour

Applications are filed electronically with the UGE-CE, the national Large Companies and Strategic Groups unit, and granted by the Directorate General for Migration. From the moment the complete electronic application is submitted, the clock runs: Article 76 of Law 14/2013 sets a maximum decision period of twenty days.

Here is the part that matters: if the administration does not resolve within those twenty days, the authorization is deemed granted by positive administrative silence. Spain wrote this deliberately — the fast-track mobility chapter of the law is designed to compete for international talent, and silence-as-approval is the sharpest tool in that design. In practice, complete files get explicit resolutions; incomplete files get requirements. The silence rule exists so that an overwhelmed office cannot quietly stall your future.

Filing the application also protects your current status: requesting a residence authorization under this chapter extends the validity of whatever residence or stay situation you already hold until the procedure is resolved. If you are in Spain on a student stay or a visa that is about to expire, filing does not leave you exposed.

Once the authorization is granted, two paths diverge. If you are already legally in Spain, you can begin operating. If you are abroad, there is one more step: obtaining the national visa in person at a consulate. Visa applications under this route are resolved and notified within ten working days, except where the Schengen consultation procedure applies.

The 3-year card and the two-year renewal

The first authorization is generous by European standards: three years, valid across all of Spain, and it authorises both residence and work from the start. After three years you can renew for two-year periods — each renewal granted electronically as long as you still meet the conditions that generated the right — and after five years of legal residence you can move to long-term (permanent) residence.

Family members are folded into the same framework rather than pushed to a slower family-reunification procedure. Your spouse or registered partner, your minor or economically dependent children, and dependent first-degree ascendants can apply jointly or afterwards under the Law 14/2013 route — a meaningful advantage over the general-regime permits where reunification means a separate, slower file.

There is a maintenance obligation worth knowing now, not at renewal: Article 76 and the seventh additional provision of the law require you to keep the qualifying conditions throughout the authorization's life, and any change affecting admission must be communicated to the UGE within thirty days. The Directorate General for Migration can request whatever reports it needs to verify that the project is still alive. Founders who get the card and then let the project quietly die — the famous "visa first, startup later" strategy — discover at renewal that the administration checked.

Why good projects still get refused

With no capital floor and a 20-day clock, refusals almost never come from the money or the timeline. They come from the ENISA report, and they follow a handful of patterns worth naming because each is avoidable:

The innovation is asserted, not shown. The most common refusal pattern is a business plan that uses the word "innovative" twenty times without ever specifying what is new — the technology, the process, the model, the intellectual property. ENISA's evaluators read hundreds of these; a project that could be described as "Uber for X" without further technical substance rarely survives.

The founder is decorative. When the technical substance of the plan clearly belongs to a partner or an employee and the applicant's profile is purely managerial, the involvement test fails. If you are the applicant, the file must make you load-bearing.

The project has no Spanish specificity. A plan that could be executed identically in Lisbon, Berlin, or remotely from a laptop invites the question the law requires ENISA to ask: what is the special economic interest for Spain? Market entry strategy, local partnerships, Spanish supply chains or customer bases — something must anchor the project here.

The financing is incoherent. Not too small — incoherent. A hardware project funded by "future revenue," a deep-tech build with no runway for salaries, a plan whose own numbers don't add up. ENISA does not need a minimum; it needs arithmetic that works.

Note one asymmetry in your favour: the ENISA report is requested by the UGE ex officio and costs you nothing but preparation time. There is no application fee to the agency itself for the assessment. The money you spend — and most serious applicants do spend it — goes into the business plan and the file, not into buying the decision.

Entrepreneur visa or the self-employed route?

This is the fork where readers most often go wrong, so let us settle it. The entrepreneur visa (Law 14/2013) is for innovative projects, decided centrally by the UGE in weeks, with a 3-year first card and family included from day one. The self-employed route (autorización de residencia y trabajo por cuenta propia, the general regime) is for conventional businesses: it goes through the local immigration office, takes months, and is judged on the viability of a normal business rather than its innovation.

The rule of thumb: if your project would make a venture capitalist's shortlist, you probably belong in the entrepreneur lane. If it would make a bank manager's shortlist, you belong in the self-employed lane. A good local business filed as an entrepreneur case dies at ENISA; a genuine startup filed as a self-employed case wastes months in a queue designed for restaurants.

There is one more option worth naming for completeness: the highly-qualified professional route, also under Law 14/2013, for founders who are being hired or sponsored rather than building independently. It runs through the same UGE window and shares the 20-day clock. But if you are the founder, driving the project yourself, the entrepreneur authorization is the cleanest instrument Spain offers — and in 2026, with the golden visa gone, it is the only Law 14/2013 route that rewards the quality of the idea itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a minimum investment for Spain's entrepreneur visa in 2026?

No. Spain's Unidad de Grandes Empresas (UGE-CE) states explicitly that there are no minimum investment or job-creation figures for the entrepreneur route. Your financing only has to be coherent with the project you describe in the business plan — a convincing €80,000 software startup passes where an unconvincing €500,000 project fails.

How long does the Spain entrepreneur visa take to process?

The law sets a maximum of 20 days from the electronic filing of a complete application with the UGE-CE, and if no decision arrives in that time the authorization is deemed granted by administrative silence. Before that, ENISA has 10 working days to issue its mandatory innovation report, and consular visa issuance afterwards takes around 10 working days.

How long is the first entrepreneur residence permit valid?

The initial authorization is valid for 3 years nationwide. It can then be renewed for 2-year periods, and after 5 years of legal residence you can apply for long-term (permanent) residence.

Can I bring my family on Spain's entrepreneur visa?

Yes. Your spouse or registered partner, minor or economically dependent children, and dependent first-degree ascendants can apply jointly or afterwards under the same Law 14/2013 route.

Do I have to invest before getting the ENISA report?

No. You do not need a finished product, incorporated company, or committed capital to get the favourable report. What ENISA judges is the documented innovation case — the idea, the team's profile, the financing plan, and the value it would add to the Spanish economy.

What changed for Spain's entrepreneur route after the golden visa ended?

Spain abolished the investor (golden visa) figure of Law 14/2013 effective 3 April 2025. The entrepreneur authorization — articles 68 to 70 of the same law — was untouched and remains the route for founders who cannot buy residency and must earn it with an innovative project.

Bottom Line

Spain's entrepreneur visa is the best founder route in Europe for people with a genuinely innovative idea and no pile of capital — and the worst for people with capital and no innovation. Everything hangs on the ENISA report, so write the business plan for the evaluator, not the investor: show the innovation, show your role in it, anchor it in Spain, and make the financing arithmetic work. Get that right and the 3-year card, the 20-day clock, and positive silence do the rest.
