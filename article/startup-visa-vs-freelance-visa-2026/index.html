Startup vs Freelance Visa 2026: 6-country guide

Founders relocating to Europe face two different auditions. One route asks a panel of experts to judge your idea; the other asks your bank statements to prove your income. Across Estonia, Spain, Portugal, France, Germany, and the Netherlands, the choice between the startup door and the freelance door changes the money you need, the risk you carry, and how renewals work. This guide compares the two doors country by country, then gives you a three-question framework to pick.

Startup visas trade money tests for expert judgment: a committee, incubator, or innovation agency decides your idea is worth admitting. Freelance and nomad visas trade judgment for arithmetic: hit the income threshold and document it. Estonia, Spain, Portugal, France, and the Netherlands all run both doors side by side; Germany runs two self-employment doors and no dedicated startup visa. The right choice is the route whose gatekeeper you can actually satisfy.

The real choice: who judges you — an expert or a formula

Strip away the marketing and every founder visa is one of two auditions. In the endorsement audition, a human panel — Estonia's Startup Committee, Spain's ENISA, a certified Portuguese incubator, a Dutch facilitator vetted by the RVO — reads your business and decides whether it deserves a place. Your bank balance is secondary. In the income audition, nobody reads your business plan at all: the state sets a number, you prove you earn it, and the permit follows.

That distinction drives three structural differences that matter more than any single threshold. First, capital at risk: endorsement routes let cash-poor founders with strong products through, while income routes quietly favour established earners. Second, evidence style: endorsement files are narratives (decks, traction, team), income files are arithmetic (contracts, statements, averages). Third, renewal math: endorsement routes front-load the hard test and then mostly check the business is alive; income routes re-test the money at every renewal, so a bad earnings year can end your residence.

With that frame in place, here is how the two doors actually work in six countries, checked against official sources in October 2026.

Estonia: the committee versus the payslip

The startup door. Estonia's Startup Committee — startup community experts — assesses your company against the Aliens Act definition of a startup: innovative, technology-based, scalable, with global growth potential. Decisions usually land within 10 working days, and a positive one yields a verification letter plus a unique application code. That unlocks a long-stay startup visa of up to 12 months, after which founders convert to a temporary residence permit for startup entrepreneurship. There is no fixed investment minimum; the committee's judgment replaces the money test that the ordinary business permit (with its €65,000 capital expectation) would otherwise demand.

The income door. Estonia's closest income-tested alternative is the digital nomad visa: work remotely for a foreign employer, your own foreign company, or freelance clients mostly outside Estonia, and prove gross income of at least €4,500 a month over the six months before applying, per the official Work in Estonia guidance. It runs up to 12 months. Nobody judges your business idea — but nobody cares how brilliant it is either. The number is the number.

The trade-off. Estonia is the purest illustration of the two auditions. If your product is your strongest asset and your savings are thin, the committee is your door. If your income is already documented and you would rather not pitch anyone, the payslip is. Note the asymmetry: the startup route leads to a multi-year residence permit; the nomad route is a one-year instrument you cannot simply stack forever.

Spain: ENISA's innovation report versus the minimum wage

The startup door. Spain's entrepreneur residence runs through ENISA, the state innovation lender, which issues a favourable report on your project's innovation and scalability. There is no minimum investment or job-creation figure in the law — the statute is explicit on that point — which makes Spain one of Europe's cheapest endorsement routes on paper. File from inside Spain through the UGE-CE (the large-companies unit) and the first permit runs three years, renewing for two; the consular route abroad starts with a one-year visa. Your means must be sufficient with reference to the SMI, Spain's minimum wage, but no fixed statutory amount applies.

The income door. Spain's digital nomad visa sets the arithmetic at 200% of the SMI in remote income, with at least 80% of your work for clients or an employer outside Spain. The same 3-years-inland / 1-year-consular structure applies. No one at ENISA reads your deck; the consulate reads your contracts and statements.

The trade-off. Spain is where the two doors sit closest together, which makes the choice genuinely tactical. The ENISA report is free to request but takes months and demands real innovation evidence — a thin project dies there. The nomad route is faster and purely documentary, but the income bar is high and re-tested at renewal. Founders with a defensible innovation story should price the ENISA wait against the nomad route's income floor; everyone else should take the arithmetic.

Portugal: IAPMEI's incubator versus four minimum wages

The startup door. Portugal's StartUP Visa is administered by IAPMEI through certified incubators. The sequence matters: you win a certified incubator's acceptance first, sign the incubation contract, and IAPMEI issues the declaration that anchors your residence visa. The agency assesses innovation, scalability, international orientation, and qualified-employment potential — including a credible path to over €325,000 in annual turnover or assets within five years. Personal means are set at 12 times the Social Support Index (IAS), and IAPMEI decides within 30 working days. Up to five founders can ride one project.

The income door. Portugal's remote-work residence route (the D8 in common parlance) asks for average monthly remote income of at least four times the guaranteed minimum monthly wage, evidenced over the previous three months. With the 2026 minimum wage at €920 a month under Decreto-Lei n.º 139/2025, that bar sits at €3,680 a month — the highest headline income figure among the six countries here. The residence track produces a two-year permit renewable in three-year blocks.

The trade-off. Portugal is the steepest income audition in this comparison and one of the more demanding endorsement auditions. The incubator is the real hurdle — founders consistently report that winning a certified incubator's backing is harder than satisfying IAPMEI afterward. If you can clear €3,680 a month in documented remote income, the arithmetic door is dramatically simpler; if you cannot, the incubator door is your only Portuguese option, and you should start courting incubators months before you need the visa.

France: a 4-year talent passport versus the 1-year entrepreneur card

The startup door. The French Tech Visa for founders is a Talent Passport: four years of residence from day one, renewable, for founders whose innovative project is recognised by a French public body or a partner incubator/accelerator. Family members get matching permits. It is diploma-agnostic and carries no fixed investment minimum — the recognition does the work that capital thresholds do elsewhere.

The income door. France's entrepreneur / profession libérale card is the freelance workhorse: a temporary card of at most one year, renewable, with the possibility of a four-year multi-year card afterward. The means test is explicit in the official service-public guidance — the activity must be capable of generating at least €1,867.02 a month — and issuance costs €350 in stamp duty and tax. An opinion from the foreign-labour platform is part of the file.

The trade-off. France offers the starkest duration contrast in this guide: four years of certainty for the endorsed founder versus one-year increments for the independent professional. If your project can win institutional recognition, the French Tech route is the single most renewal-proof option among the six countries. If it cannot, budget for annual renewals and keep your revenue documentation immaculate — the one-year card is re-examined every year.

Germany: two doors into self-employment, zero startup visas

Germany is the outlier: it has no dedicated startup visa at all. Instead it offers two self-employment doors, and founders must pick the one that fits their legal form.

Door one: the freelancer. The residence permit for freelance employment (Freiberufler) covers the liberal professions — developers, designers, consultants, journalists, and similar. The federal portal's conditions are refreshingly plain: prove you can finance the undertaking, prove you can make a living, and hold any professional permission your field requires; over-45s must show adequate pension provision. The initial permit runs up to three years and is extendable; settlement is possible after five. The unwritten rule, enforced at Ausländerbehörde discretion, is local economic interest — letters of intent from German clients carry enormous weight.

Door two: the entrepreneur. The §21 route for self-employed business owners (Gewerbe) demands more: an economic interest or regional need, a full business concept, and financing to match. It is the heavier file, and the permit logic is closer to an investment review.

The trade-off. In Germany the choice is not endorsement versus income — it is profession versus enterprise. Liberal professionals get the lighter, faster freelancer door; anyone incorporating a product company faces the full §21 assessment. Founders sometimes incorporate elsewhere in the EU and enter Germany later; that is a legitimate strategy, but it concedes the German market as your launch base.

Netherlands: the facilitator you must have versus the points you must score

The startup door. The Dutch startup residence permit lasts one year and has a non-negotiable precondition: a signed agreement with a facilitator recognised by the RVO (the Netherlands Enterprise Agency). The RVO then advises the IND on both the innovativeness of your product and the reliability of your facilitator — a positive recommendation is effectively required. You and the facilitator must both be registered with the Chamber of Commerce, and you must show lasting and sufficient means of support. The facilitator cannot be a family member and cannot hold a majority interest in your company.

The income door. The self-employment residence permit replaces the facilitator with a points system: score at least 90 of 300 points across personal experience, business plan, and added value to the Netherlands. It is the most transparently mechanical of the six countries' freelance doors — and, at 90 points minimum per category effectively, one of the hardest to clear cold. The escape hatch is built in: if your startup-year facilitator is satisfied with your progress, their declaration exempts you from the points system when you transition to the self-employment permit.

The trade-off. The Netherlands front-loads relationship risk: everything depends on finding and keeping a recognised facilitator. The one-year fuse is the shortest here, but the facilitator declaration at year-end is the smoothest renewal mechanism in this guide — a single signature replaces a points audit. Choose the Netherlands if you can secure a facilitator early; otherwise the points door will likely disappoint.

The decision framework: three questions that pick your route

Forget ranking countries. Answer three questions honestly and the route picks itself.

1. What is your strongest evidence — product or payslip? If you can show a working product, traction, or a credible technical team, endorsement routes (Estonia's committee, ENISA, IAPMEI's incubators, the French Tech network, a Dutch facilitator) convert that strength into residence without demanding wealth. If your strength is documented income — contracts, salary slips, client statements — income routes convert that into residence without demanding anyone's opinion of your idea. Apply through your strength, not your aspiration.

2. How much uncertainty can your timeline absorb? Endorsement routes are slower and less predictable: committees deliberate, incubators court, ENISA takes months. Income routes are documentary and calendar-driven. If you must move by a fixed date, the arithmetic door is the rational choice even when the endorsement door looks cheaper.

3. What does year two look like? This is the question founders skip. Endorsement routes usually ease after entry — the hard test is behind you, and renewals check progress. Income routes re-test the money every cycle — a dry year can cost you the permit. France's four-year Tech Visa and Spain's three-year entrepreneur permit buy the most runway; the Netherlands' one-year startup permit and France's one-year entrepreneur card demand the most vigilance.

Whichever door you choose, the file still needs real numbers behind it — size your runway with the proof of funds calculator → before an embassy or a committee sees a single statement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a startup visa better than a freelance visa?

Neither is better in the abstract. Startup visas replace money tests with expert judgment of your idea, which suits cash-poor founders with strong products; freelance and nomad visas replace judgment with income thresholds, which suits established earners who can document steady remote income. Pick the route whose gatekeeper you can actually satisfy.

Which country has the easiest startup visa in 2026?

Ease depends on your profile. Estonia's Startup Committee decides in about 10 working days with no fixed investment minimum, which is fast if your product is strong. Spain's entrepreneur residence has no investment floor either but requires a favourable ENISA innovation report. The Netherlands demands a recognised facilitator contract before you even apply.

Can I switch from a freelance visa to a startup visa later?

In most of the six countries you apply for the new route afresh rather than converting in place. The cleaner strategy is to pick the right route at the start: income-tested routes if your earnings are already documented, endorsement routes if your product is your strongest asset.

Do startup visas require you to hire employees?

None of the six countries' startup routes examined here sets a fixed job-creation quota at entry. They assess innovation, scalability and growth potential instead. Portugal's IAPMEI looks for qualified-employment potential and a path to significant turnover within five years, but that is an assessment criterion, not a hiring quota.

Which route renews more easily?

Startup routes usually front-load the hard test: once endorsed, renewals check that the business is alive and progressing. Income routes re-test the money at every renewal, which is simpler but means a bad earnings year can end your residence. France's 4-year French Tech Visa is the most renewal-proof of the six; the Netherlands' 1-year startup permit is the shortest fuse.

Can my family come on a startup or freelance visa?

Yes across all six countries, with conditions and higher means thresholds per family member. Spouses typically receive residence and, on most routes, work rights. Check the specific family income scales, because they differ sharply — Spain and Portugal publish explicit per-dependent multipliers.

Bottom Line

The startup door asks experts to believe in your idea; the freelance door asks your statements to prove your income. Across these six countries, founders with a real product and thin savings should chase endorsement, earners with documented income should take the arithmetic, and everyone should choose with year two — the renewal — in mind, not just the entry stamp.
