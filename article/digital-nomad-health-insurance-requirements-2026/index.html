Digital Nomad Health Insurance Requirements 2026

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 14, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Health insurance is the quiet dealbreaker of digital nomad visas. Here is what Spain, Portugal, Thailand, Japan, South Korea and Germany actually require in 2026, what "full coverage" means to a consular officer, and the policy mistakes that sink applications.

Quick answer. Most digital nomad visas require health insurance, but the bar differs sharply by country. Spain demands private cover authorised in Spain with no copays, no deductibles and no waiting periods; Portugal requires at least €30,000 of cover valid in Portugal; Japan requires private insurance of at least ¥10 million (roughly $63,000–$70,000) covering death, injury and illness; South Korea's workation visa needs KRW 100 million (roughly $63,000–$75,000) of hospital and repatriation cover; Germany insists on comprehensive domestic cover and rejects travel insurance outright. Thailand's DTV is the outlier — no mandatory insurance at national level, though some embassies add it locally. Travel insurance alone fails for Spain, Portugal's residency stage and Germany.

Why nomad visas demand health insurance

Strip away the bureaucracy and the logic is blunt. A digital nomad visa lets you live in a country for a year or more without paying into its tax and social security system. Spain is not enrolling you in its public health system. Japan's national health insurance is not open to you. Portugal's public service exists, but it is not automatically yours on day one. So the host country asks the one question that protects its hospitals: if this person gets sick or hurt here, who pays?

Your answer has to be airtight — a private policy that pays in full, covers the whole territory, and lasts the whole stay. Anything less and the application is, in practice, you asking a foreign health system to carry your risk. Consulates read it that way, which is why insurance lines that look like formalities on paper are where so many applications actually die.

Travel insurance vs. health insurance: the distinction that decides applications

If you've ever bought travel insurance for a two-week holiday, forget what that product does. It covers trips — short, defined, emergency-first. A consular officer reviewing a digital nomad application is checking something different: a policy that behaves like a resident's health plan, covering primary care, specialists, hospitalisation and surgery over months, not days.

This is exactly where the most common mistake happens. Applicants submit a solid annual travel insurance plan for a Spain digital nomad visa and get refused, because Spain requires private health insurance, not travel cover — no matter how generous the travel policy looks. Germany works the same way: travel policies are explicitly not accepted for the residence-permit stage. Portugal is the interesting middle case. For the visa approval itself, a travel policy meeting the €30,000 bar is usually accepted; for the residency-permit interview after you arrive, you need proper private health insurance. Same country, two stages, two standards — and plenty of people only prepare for the first one.

Rule of thumb: if the policy was designed to be bought alongside a flight booking, a consulate reviewing a one-year visa will almost certainly find it too thin.

What "full coverage" actually means in practice

"Full coverage" gets thrown around a lot in visa guidance, and it sounds vague until you see what officers check for. Across the strictest countries, it resolves into a short checklist:

  • The insurer is authorised where you'll live. Spain's version is explicit — the provider must be registered with Spain's insurance regulator (DGSFP). It doesn't have to be a Spanish company, but the certificate must show it can legally operate there.
  • No copayments, no deductibles. Spain calls it sin copago — the insurer pays 100% of each visit, you pay nothing extra at the point of care. A $20 copay buried in the fine print has caused real denials at Spanish consulates in the US.
  • No waiting periods. Coverage has to work from day one. Policies that delay coverage for certain conditions — even 30 or 90 days — fail.
  • The scope matches a resident's care. Primary care, specialist visits, hospitalisation, surgery, emergency treatment — the baseline is the host country's public system, and your private policy must equal it.
  • Nationwide, not regional. Cover must work across the whole country. Cheaper local policies that only cover certain provinces don't pass.
  • Long enough. At least the full visa period — many consulates want a minimum of 12 months even for a one-year visa, and won't accept a policy that expires before the visa does.
  • Repatriation included. Several consulates specifically require medical evacuation and repatriation in the policy wording, not just treatment in-country.

None of this is about the insurer being "good" in some abstract sense. It's about the certificate — the one-page document you actually submit — saying each of these things explicitly, in terms the officer can read without hunting through a policy booklet.

Spain: the strictest bar in Europe

Spain's digital nomad visa, running under the 2022 Startup Law, sets the toughest insurance standard of any major nomad programme. Everything in the checklist above applies in full, and it's enforced by consular staff who read certificates line by line.

The policy must come from an insurer authorised to operate in Spain. It must carry no copayments and no deductibles — most standard American health plans fail here immediately. No waiting periods. Coverage must be comprehensive enough to match Spain's public system, nationwide, and valid for at least 12 months. Travel insurance and short-stay policies are rejected outright, and the European Health Insurance Card doesn't help non-EU nationals.

One quirk worth knowing: the minimum coverage amount varies by consulate. Some specify €30,000; others want €500,000 or effectively "unlimited" hospitalisation cover. Because the requirement is consulate-dependent, the practical move is to buy the highest limits you can afford rather than targeting the lowest bar — it's cheaper than a refused application. Portugal: the €30,000 rule and the two-stage trap

Portugal's D8 digital nomad visa sets a cleaner numerical bar: health insurance of at least €30,000, valid in Portugal, covering the stay. It's the same figure as the Schengen travel-insurance minimum — which is exactly why people underestimate it.

The trap is the two-stage structure. Stage one: the visa application at the consulate, where travel insurance meeting the €30,000 minimum is typically accepted and costs a reported €40–€100. Stage two: after you land in Portugal and attend your residency interview with AIMA (the immigration agency), you need proper private health insurance — reported at roughly €600–€1,800 a year — and that requirement continues through your residency period even though you may later be able to register with Portugal's public health service. Applicants who buy only the cheap travel policy for stage one arrive unprepared for stage two.

Portugal also demands proof of accommodation for 12 months, so in practice you're setting up a real life there — the insurance should match that commitment, not just the visa paperwork. The full breakdown is in our Portugal D8 health insurance guide for 2026.

Thailand: the exception that proves the rule

Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) is the notable outlier on this page, and it confuses people precisely because everything else on the list is so strict. At national level, Thailand's foreign ministry does not require health insurance for the DTV. The official checklist asks for a passport, a photo, three months of bank statements showing at least 500,000 THB (about $15,000) and proof of your remote work or approved activity — insurance simply isn't on it. That's a deliberate contrast with Thailand's own O-A retirement visa, which does demand heavy coverage.

But "not mandatory" is not the same as "never asked." Individual Thai embassies keep discretionary authority to add insurance to their local checklists, and some do. When they ask, the bar is usually around $50,000 of medical cover (or roughly 40,000 THB outpatient and 400,000 THB inpatient), with Thailand named explicitly as a covered country. So the right move for a DTV application is a quick check of your specific embassy's checklist before you apply — and, frankly, decent cover anyway, because a year or five in Thailand without medical insurance is a gamble with your savings.

Japan: the ¥10 million requirement

Japan's digital nomad visa — a "Designated Activities" status introduced in March 2024, good for a single stay of up to six months — pairs a high income bar (at least ¥10 million a year from non-Japanese sources) with a matching insurance bar: private cover of at least ¥10 million, roughly $63,000–$70,000 depending on the exchange rate, for death, injury and illness across your entire stay.

The reason for the high figure is structural. Nomad visa holders are not eligible for Japan's National Health Insurance, so the state has no backstop for you — the policy is the backstop, and it has to be sized for Japanese medical costs. The certificate, not just a policy number, must be submitted. One detail families miss: a spouse and children accompanying you need their own insurance at the same ¥10 million level. A single family policy only works if the certificate shows each person is covered to the required amount. More in our Japan mandatory health cover explainer.

South Korea: KRW 100 million for the workation visa

South Korea's workation visa (F-1-D, launched January 2024) follows the same design logic as Japan's: remote workers earning at least double the per-capita GNI — reported around KRW 85 million a year, though the figure is revised annually so confirm it with your consulate — must carry private medical insurance of at least KRW 100 million, roughly $63,000–$75,000, for the duration of the stay.

Consular checklists are explicit that the cover must handle both hospital treatment and emergency repatriation, and the insurance certificate is part of the standard document bundle alongside income proof and the criminal-record certificate. The visa allows an initial year with a possible one-year extension, so the policy needs to run the full period — a 90-day travel policy won't survive the paperwork.

Germany: domestic cover or nothing

Germany doesn't run a purpose-built nomad visa — remote workers typically use the freelance residence permit — but its insurance rule is the hardest-edged in Europe: you need comprehensive health insurance inside the German system, either public cover (if eligible) or fully comprehensive private cover approved by Germany's financial regulator (BaFin). Travel insurance is categorically not accepted for the residence-permit stage. If your plan is Germany, this is the one country where buying the insurance is less about satisfying a document check and more about entering a real domestic insurance system — which means real monthly premiums baked into your relocation budget from day one. What to look for in a policy

When you're comparing plans, ignore the marketing and check these specifics before you pay:

  • Authorisation in the host country — the certificate must show the insurer can legally operate there (Spain's DGSFP registration is the test case).
  • Zero copays and zero deductibles — non-negotiable for Spain; a safe default everywhere.
  • No waiting periods — effective from your arrival date.
  • Scope — primary care, specialist visits, hospitalisation, surgery and emergency care, not just emergencies.
  • Repatriation and medical evacuation — stated explicitly in the certificate wording.
  • Geographic wording — "Spain" / "Portugal" / "worldwide", not a regional plan or a vague "international" label the officer can't verify.
  • Duration — covering at least the full visa period, ideally 12 months minimum.
  • The certificate itself — your full name matching your passport exactly, policy number, coverage dates, the no-copay/no-deductible terms, and covered territory, all on one readable document. The certificate is what gets judged.
  • Dependents — each accompanying family member individually covered to the required level (Japan and Spain both trip people up here).
  • Pre-existing conditions — most visa-compliant policies exclude them; know your position before you buy, especially past age 60.

One more honest point: this guide describes requirements, not products. We're not ranking insurers — the compliant choice depends on your age, health history, the consulate you're applying through and the country you're entering. What matters is that the certificate answers every item above in plain language.

Common insurance-related rejection reasons

Insurance refusals follow patterns, and immigration lawyers who handle these visas describe the same handful of failures:

  • Submitting travel insurance where health insurance is required. The number-one failure, especially for Spain and Germany. The product category itself disqualifies it.
  • A copay or deductible hiding in the fine print. A $20-per-visit copay on an otherwise strong policy has caused real denials at Spanish consulates in the United States. Officers are trained to look for exactly this.
  • An insurer not authorised in the host country. A great American or British policy that isn't registered to operate in Spain fails the Spanish requirement regardless of its coverage.
  • Waiting periods. A policy that activates fully on day one for accidents but imposes a 90-day wait on illness is a policy with a 90-day hole — consulates read it that way.
  • Regional or geographically vague cover. A plan covering "Catalonia" or stating coverage so vaguely the officer can't confirm the host country is named.
  • A policy that starts after your entry date or expires before the visa does. Coverage dates that don't bracket your stay tell the officer you won't be insured for part of it.
  • Missing repatriation. Several consulates list evacuation and repatriation as a specific requirement; a certificate that omits the word is a gamble.
  • A certificate that only summarises. Submitting a one-line confirmation letter instead of a certificate that spells out terms, territory, dates and the no-copay/no-deductible position. The certificate is the document being judged — make it readable.
  • Dependents left off the coverage. Bringing a spouse or child whose name doesn't appear on the certificate, or whose coverage is below the required amount.

Notice what these have in common: every one of them is fixable before you apply, and almost none of them are fixable after a refusal without starting the process again.

When to buy, and in what order

Buy the insurance before you apply — every country on this list needs the certificate in the application file, not after approval. For Spain, the in-country UGE route has a tight window: you enter as a tourist, you're expected to file before day 60 of your 90-day stay, and arriving on day one with nothing prepared is the classic mistake. Have the policy arranged before you land.

Two practical points. First, check the certificate language your consulate wants. Several Spanish consulates prefer or require the certificate in Spanish — confirm before you apply rather than after a delay. Second, buy from a provider that will refund or reschedule if the visa is refused. Visa-denial protection isn't standard on every policy, but it's worth asking about before you pay for a year of cover; a refused application shouldn't also mean a lost premium.

After year one: renewals and ongoing cover

Insurance isn't a one-time box to check — it's a condition of staying. Spain requires proof of continuous private cover at every renewal, and letting the policy lapse between renewals is grounds for the renewal to be denied. Renewals also have a physical-presence test — roughly 183 days a year of residence — so this isn't just about paying the premium; it's about actually living there and staying insured while you do.

Portugal's structure works similarly at the residency stage: private insurance stays required through your residence-permit period even if you later register with the public health service. And for countries like Japan where you're barred from the national system entirely, the private policy is your only cover for the whole stay — there is no domestic system to fall back into later.

The budget implication is straightforward: price a full year (or the full visa period) of compliant cover into your relocation budget, not just the first month. Insurance is an ongoing cost of the lifestyle, the way rent is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all digital nomad visas require health insurance?

Nearly all do, but the standard varies widely. Spain, Portugal, Japan, South Korea and Germany all make insurance a visa condition with specific minimums. Thailand's DTV is the main exception — no mandatory insurance at national level, though some embassies add it to their local checklist. Always check the specific embassy or consulate you're applying through, because local implementation can differ from the national rule.

Can I use travel insurance for a digital nomad visa?

Usually not. Spain and Germany require private health insurance and reject travel insurance outright. Portugal accepts travel insurance meeting the €30,000 minimum for the visa-approval stage, but requires proper private health insurance for the residency-permit stage. Travel policies are designed for short trips and almost never meet the scope, duration or no-copay conditions of a long-stay visa.

What is the minimum coverage amount for a digital nomad visa?

There is no universal figure. Portugal requires at least €30,000 valid in Portugal; Japan requires at least ¥10 million (roughly $63,000–$70,000); South Korea requires KRW 100 million (roughly $63,000–$75,000) covering hospital treatment and repatriation. For Spain, the minimum varies by consulate — some ask for €30,000, others want €500,000 or effectively unlimited hospitalisation cover — so buy the highest limits you can afford.

Can I use my home country's health insurance, like a US or UK plan?

Almost certainly not. Typical US health plans include deductibles and copays and aren't registered to operate in the host country — both are disqualifying for Spain. UK residents relying on the NHS or a GHIC/EHIC card have no cover once they're resident abroad, and EHIC cards are not accepted for non-EU nationals' visa applications. You need a dedicated international or expat health policy that meets the host country's wording.

Does the policy have to cover my entire stay?

Yes. Coverage must span the full visa period — many consulates want a minimum of 12 months even for a one-year visa, and won't accept a policy that starts after your entry date or expires before the visa does. For Japan, the policy must cover the entire stay including death, injury and illness, since you're not eligible for the national health system.

Does my spouse or partner need separate insurance?

In most cases each accompanying family member must be covered to the same required level, and their names should appear on the certificate. Japan explicitly requires accompanying spouses and children to hold their own insurance at the ¥10 million level. A single family policy only works if the certificate shows every person is covered to the required amount.

What happens if my insurance lapses after the visa is granted?

It can cost you the renewal. Spain requires proof of continuous private cover at every renewal, and a lapsed policy between renewals is grounds for refusal. Portugal requires ongoing private cover through the residency-permit period. Insurance is a condition of staying, not just of applying — keep the policy active and keep the certificates.

How much does digital nomad health insurance cost?

It depends on age, health history and the country. For Spain-compliant cover, insurance guides report roughly €45–€80 a month for applicants under 50, rising to €90–€180 a month past 60 — these are reported market figures, not official rates, and pre-existing conditions can change them sharply. For Portugal, guides report roughly €40–€100 for the visa-stage travel policy and €600–€1,800 a year for the residency-stage private policy. Get quotes against the certificate wording your consulate requires, not against a generic price list.

Bottom Line

Bottom Line

For digital nomad visas in 2026, insurance is a visa condition with real teeth: buy private health cover before you apply, make sure the certificate explicitly states the no-copay/no-deductible terms, the covered territory and dates, and cover the full stay — travel insurance fails for Spain, Germany and Portugal's residency stage.
