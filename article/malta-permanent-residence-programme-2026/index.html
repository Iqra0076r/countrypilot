Malta Permanent Residence Programme 2026: Costs After the CBI Ruling

Malta sells two completely different products, and the market keeps confusing them. One of them — citizenship for investment — was killed by Europe's highest court in 2025. The other — permanent residence for investment — is alive, repriced, and arguably better regulated than before. This guide prices the living one honestly.

The short version: The Malta Permanent Residence Programme (MPRP) grants permanent residence — not citizenship — to qualifying non-EU nationals. In 2026 the official schedule is a €60,000 non-refundable administrative fee, a €37,000 government contribution, a €2,000 NGO donation, a €500 residence card per person, and either a property purchase of at least €375,000 or rent of at least €14,000 a year, held for five years. Applicants must also show capital assets of at least €500,000 (with €150,000 in financial assets) or €650,000 (with €75,000 in financial assets). Malta's citizenship-by-investment scheme was ended following the EU Court of Justice judgment of 29 April 2025 and is a separate, closed matter.

If you search for "Malta golden visa" today, you will find pages describing a country that no longer exists: one where a passport could be bought, and where the residence programme cost whatever it cost in 2021. Both halves of that picture are wrong in 2026, and the wrongness is expensive — the difference between the outdated figures still circulating and the current official schedule runs into tens of thousands of euros. This guide is written against the Residency Malta Agency's own published requirements, checked on 15 October 2026, with the court history stated precisely and the residence-versus-citizenship distinction kept exactly where the law puts it.

What the EU court actually killed — and what it didn't

On 29 April 2025, the Grand Chamber of the Court of Justice of the European Union delivered its judgment in Commission v Malta, Case C-181/23. The target was Malta's "Citizenship by Naturalisation for Exceptional Services by Direct Investment" — the investor-citizenship scheme, often called the golden passport. The Court held that granting nationality in exchange for predetermined payments or investments, without a genuine link between the applicant and the country, breached EU law: Article 20 TFEU on Union citizenship and the Article 4(3) TEU principle of sincere cooperation. In the Court's framing, Union citizenship had been commercialised — turned into a product — and a member state may not unilaterally sell rights that are valid across the whole Union.

Malta wound the investor route down during 2025. The ruling did not retroactively strip citizenship from people who had already been naturalised; it prohibited the practice going forward. And here is the part the market keeps garbling: the judgment touched citizenship, not residence. The Malta Permanent Residence Programme was never the scheme before the Court. Residency Malta states it in the plainest possible terms on its own programme page: the MPRP grants permanent residence in Malta and is distinct from the separate legislative provisions governing citizenship. Residence survived because residence was never on trial.

That distinction is the entire foundation of this guide. Everything below prices and describes a residence product. Anyone who tells you in 2026 that a Maltese passport is available for a fixed investment is not describing a legal programme — more on that at the end.

The 1 January 2025 price reset: what the current schedule really says

Before pricing the programme, a warning about the internet: most of what you will read about MPRP costs is out of date. The programme's requirements were amended with effect from 1 January 2025, and the amendments moved nearly every number. Older guides quote the pre-2025 schedule — including regionally split property thresholds that no longer exist — as if it were current. It is not. Here is the schedule the Residency Malta Agency publishes now.

Three things about this table deserve emphasis. First, the government contribution is now a single €37,000 figure for both routes — the old purchase/rental split is gone. Second, the property thresholds are flat across Malta and Gozo; the pre-2025 regional discounts were removed. Third, the five-year property hold is a minimum, not the end of the obligation: after the five years, the beneficiary must continue to maintain a residential property in Malta. The programme wants an ongoing address, not a five-year performance.

The true five-year price tag, route by route

Headline figures mislead because the MPRP is a stack of costs, not a single investment. Work it through for a single applicant. On the rental route, the non-recoverable outlay is the €60,000 administrative fee, the €37,000 government contribution, the €2,000 donation, and the €500 residence card — €99,500 before you have paid a cent of rent. Add five years of the minimum €14,000 annual lease and you are at roughly €169,500 over five years, on top of separately demonstrating the required capital assets. That is the cheapest honest version of this programme.

The purchase route replaces five years of rent with a €375,000 property acquisition — capital that remains yours (subject to property market reality), but capital that is immobilised in Malta for at least five years. Add the same €99,500 in fees and donation, plus transaction costs, and the honest comparison between the routes is not "€169,500 versus €474,500." It is: do you want your money recoverable-but-locked, or spent-but-flexible? Renters keep their capital liquid and their exit cheap; buyers keep their capital intact but their exit tied to a sale.

Families change the arithmetic through the dependant fees: €7,500 for each adult dependant excluding the spouse, plus €500 residence cards per person. Minor children and the spouse add card fees but no dependant surcharge. None of this includes the mandatory licensed agent's professional fees or the due-diligence charges, which are real, separate, and non-trivial. When an agency quotes you a single all-in number, ask for the itemised version — the official schedule above is your checklist for checking theirs.

Note: Applications must be submitted through a licensed agent — direct applications are not accepted. Verify any agent's licence with the Residency Malta Agency before paying anything.

The asset test is the real filter

People fixate on the fees, but the eligibility criterion that quietly disqualifies most casual inquiries is the capital-assets test. The main applicant must show capital assets of not less than €500,000, of which at least €150,000 must be financial assets — or, alternatively, not less than €650,000, of which at least €75,000 must be financial assets. This is separate from the property and the programme payments: it is a standing wealth test, and it must be maintained for the first five years.

Read that as the programme's actual target customer. The MPRP is not designed for someone stretching to afford the fees; it is designed for someone to whom the fees are an administrative detail. The remaining eligibility conditions reinforce that: third-country nationals only (non-EU, non-EEA, non-Swiss), not nationals of sanctioned countries as designated by the Agency from time to time, stable and regular financial resources sufficient to live without recourse to Malta's social assistance, a clean criminal record, and no threat to national security, public policy, public health, or the public interest.

Then comes the due diligence, which Residency Malta describes as thorough and exhaustive — and unusually, it extends beyond the applicant to dependants, donors, benefactors, and business associates. The four-tier background check is the programme's reputation shield: it is why the MPRP survived the political storm that killed the citizenship scheme, and it is why processing rewards complete, well-documented files. Source-of-funds documentation is not a formality here; it is the main event. If your wealth history has gaps you cannot document, address that with your licensed agent before you submit, not after the Agency asks.

Permanent residence without the right to work — and without a minimum stay

The MPRP grants immediate permanent residence — from day one, not after a probationary period of temporary permits. That is genuinely unusual among investment-residence products and it is the programme's strongest selling point. The residence card itself is issued for five-year periods (€500 per person), and renewal is administrative rather than a re-examination.

But "permanent" describes the status, not the entitlements, and the entitlements have sharp edges worth stating plainly. The MPRP certificate carries no employment licence: you may live in Malta, but you may not take a job there on this status. It confers Schengen travel rights — 90 days in any 180 across the zone — but not the right to reside or work in other EU states. And there is no minimum-stay requirement: you can hold the status while living elsewhere, which is precisely why time spent abroad does not count toward anything.

That last point matters for the question everyone asks next: can this become citizenship? Only through ordinary naturalisation, which requires genuine residence in Malta — the MPRP's own lack of a stay requirement means the clock does not run unless you actually live there. Malta's naturalisation framework looks for real, sustained presence plus integration, and the decision is discretionary. Treat the MPRP as what it is: a permanent EU base and a travel document, not a passport queue ticket.

The merit door, the closed door, and the scam in between

Two doors remain adjacent to the closed citizenship-by-investment route, and they could not be more different. The first is citizenship by merit: a discretionary, case-by-case grant under Maltese citizenship law for individuals whose exceptional contribution to Malta — in innovation, culture, public life, or similar fields — genuinely merits it. Malta's authorities have been explicit that this is neither a programme nor a scheme, pathway, continuation, or alternative to the former investor framework. There is no price list, no licensed-agent pipeline, and no timeline. If your record is not genuinely exceptional, this door is not for you, and no agent can open it for money.

The second is the closed door that scammers keep painting on the wall. Because the 2025 ruling was technical and Malta's wind-down was gradual, a market of outdated and outright fraudulent offers persists: "Malta passport by investment" packages, priced citizenship timelines, guaranteed approvals. In 2026, no agent — licensed or otherwise — can lawfully sell Maltese citizenship, and no agent can guarantee approval of any Maltese application. If you are shown a fixed-price passport offer, that is not a legal programme; it is either a misunderstanding or a fraud. Verify every claim against the Residency Malta Agency's published material before you engage anyone.

The honest version of the Malta story in 2026 is this: the EU's highest court ended the sale of citizenship, and what remains is one of Europe's most straightforward residence products — permanent status from the start, a rental route for the capital-light, rigorous vetting that protects the value of the status itself. Price it from the official schedule, apply through a licensed agent, and decide based on what residence is actually worth to you. That is a smaller promise than a passport. It is also a real one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Malta's citizenship-by-investment programme still open in 2026?

No. The Court of Justice of the EU ruled on 29 April 2025 (Case C-181/23) that Malta's investor-citizenship scheme breached EU law, and Malta wound the route down during 2025. There is currently no EU member state offering citizenship by direct investment.

Does the Malta Permanent Residence Programme lead to a passport?

Not by itself. The MPRP grants permanent residence, and Residency Malta states it is distinct from the legal provisions governing citizenship. Holders who genuinely live in Malta can count that residence toward ordinary naturalisation, but the programme has no minimum-stay rule and no citizenship track of its own.

What are the MPRP costs in 2026?

Per the Residency Malta Agency: a €60,000 non-refundable administrative fee, a €37,000 government contribution, €7,500 for each adult dependant excluding the spouse, a €2,000 donation to a registered Maltese NGO, and a €500 residence card fee per person for five years — plus the property requirement (purchase of at least €375,000 or rent of at least €14,000 a year).

Can I rent instead of buying property under the MPRP?

Yes. You can satisfy the property requirement by renting a qualifying residential property for at least €14,000 a year instead of purchasing one worth at least €375,000. Either way, the qualifying property must be retained for at least five years.

Does the MPRP let me work in Malta?

No. The MPRP certificate carries no employment licence. It grants the right to reside in Malta and 90-days-in-180 Schengen travel, but not the right to take a job.

What is citizenship by merit in Malta?

A discretionary, case-by-case grant under Maltese citizenship law for exceptional contribution to the country. Malta's authorities stress it is neither a programme nor a scheme, has no fixed price, and is not a continuation of the former investor route.

How long does the MPRP application take?

Applications must be submitted through a licensed agent and pass multi-tier due diligence covering the applicant, dependants, and connected parties. Industry timelines are typically a few months for a complete file, but the Agency gives no single published processing figure.

Bottom Line

Malta in 2026 offers permanent EU residence — €60,000 in admin fees, a €37,000 contribution, €375,000 to buy or €14,000 a year to rent, and a serious wealth test — with the passport shop permanently closed by the EU court. Buy the residence for what residence is worth; anyone still selling you the passport is selling you something that no longer exists.
