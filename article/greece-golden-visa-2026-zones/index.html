Greece Golden Visa 2026: Zones, Thresholds & Property Rules

Greece is now the only major EU programme where buying a home still buys residence — but the old €250,000 flat rate is gone. Since September 2024 the price depends on the postcode: €800,000 in Athens, Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini and the larger islands; €400,000 elsewhere; €250,000 only for commercial conversions and heritage restorations. Miss the zone rules, the 120-square-metre requirement, or the short-term rental ban, and the permit can be revoked. Here's every rule that matters, verified for October 2026.

Quick Answer

Greece's golden visa runs on a three-zone system under Law 5100/2024, in force since September 2024. Zone A — €800,000: the Attica region (including Athens), the Thessaloniki regional unit, Mykonos, Santorini, and islands with more than 3,100 residents (Crete, Rhodes, Corfu and others). Zone B — €400,000: everywhere else. Zone C — €250,000: commercial buildings converted to residential use and listed heritage buildings being restored — this track applies anywhere in the country. In the €400k/€800k tracks the investment must be a single property of at least 120 m² of main living space; combining smaller units no longer works. Since September 1, 2024, qualifying properties cannot be let short-term (no Airbnb-style rentals); long-term leases are fine. The permit is a five-year renewable residence permit with no minimum stay, covering spouse or partner, unmarried children under 21, and both sets of parents. A new €250,000 startup route (Elevate Greece-registered companies) became operational in early 2026. Processing runs about 6–12 months, and you may stay in Greece while the file is pending.

The three-zone system, explained

Before September 2024, Greece's golden visa was beautifully simple: €250,000 in any property, anywhere. That era is over. Law 5100/2024 replaced the flat rate with a three-zone model that prices by location and property type — and it's permanent law, not a pilot or a transitional arrangement. Get the zone wrong and your purchase falls short of the threshold no matter how nice the view is.

The logic behind the design is housing politics, same as the closures in Spain and Ireland. Prime markets get the high wall; everywhere else gets the middle tier; and the €250,000 track survives only where the investment improves the building stock — conversions and restorations.

Zone A: the €800,000 bracket

Zone A covers the places foreign buyers actually want. The Attica region — all of Athens and its suburbs, which is where most applicants were buying. The Thessaloniki regional unit. Mykonos and Santorini by name. And the islands with a resident population over 3,100: the published lists include Crete, Rhodes, Corfu, Lesbos, Chios, Zakynthos, Naxos, Kos, Kefalonia, Samos, Lefkada, Syros, Kalymnos, Lemnos, Paros, and roughly another dozen.

At €800,000 for a single qualifying property, this is no longer a mass-market product — it's competing with Portugal's fund route on price while demanding you actually buy and maintain a large home. It suits buyers who genuinely want a house in Athens or the islands and see the permit as a bonus, not the product.

Zone B: the €400,000 bracket

Everything else. Mainland regions outside Attica and Thessaloniki, smaller islands under the 3,100-resident line — much of the Peloponnese, Epirus, central Greece, the smaller Cyclades and Dodecanese. €400,000 for a single qualifying property.

This is where the programme's value proposition still lives. €400,000 buys serious property in places like Kalamata, the Mani, or the smaller islands — and the Bank of Greece reported apartment prices rising 9.1% in 2024, 8.1% in 2025, and still climbing in early 2026, so the asset story has tailwinds. But read the next two sections before falling in love with a listing: the size rule and the rental ban reshape what you can buy and what you can do with it.

Zone C: the €250,000 conversion track

The €250,000 figure that still headlines Greek golden-visa ads is real — but it's a narrow exception, not the standard entry point. It covers two property types, in any zone:

  • Commercial or industrial buildings converted to residential use — the change of use must be completed before filing, not promised for later.
  • Listed or heritage buildings being restored — with strict compliance conditions on the restoration.

The due-diligence burden here is higher than a standard purchase. An incomplete change-of-use filing, a heritage authority that hasn't signed off, or a restoration that doesn't meet the conditions can stall or sink the application. This track suits investors working with Greek legal counsel who knows the conversion process — not bargain hunters buying from a PDF brochure.

The 120 m² rule and the single-property requirement

In the €400,000 and €800,000 tracks, the law demands one property, minimum 120 square metres of "main spaces" — habitable interior area, excluding storage, parking, and other auxiliary spaces. The old practice of combining two €200,000 apartments to hit the floor is explicitly dead.

This trips up more buyers than any other rule. A 95 m² Athens flat at €450,000 fails the test. A 130 m² flat at €380,000 fails it too. Price and size must both clear the bar, in a single unit. Have your lawyer confirm the measured "main spaces" figure from the property's official documents before you sign anything — developer marketing brochures measure generously.

The short-term rental ban

Since September 1, 2024, properties bought for a golden visa may not be let short-term through Airbnb, Booking.com, or similar platforms, and may not be sublet. A breach can lead to revocation of the residence permits and an administrative fine reported at €50,000.

Long-term leases to tenants are still allowed, and the rental income is taxed in Greece at progressive rates — so the property can still generate yield, just not tourist yield. This is the cruellest part of the rule for island buyers: the home most naturally suited to short-term rental income is the one most affected. Run the numbers on long-term rental yields in your target area before committing; in many island locations they're modest.

The non-property routes (funds, deposit, startup)

Not everyone wants a house. Greece offers capital-based alternatives under the same programme:

  • Investment funds — €350,000 in qualifying Greece-focused funds.
  • Bank deposit — €500,000 in a fixed-term deposit with a single Greek bank for at least 12 months, renewed automatically while you hold the permit. Interest is taxed at a 15% withholding rate. The upside: no property transaction costs and no rental ban. The downside: the money sits at modest interest with currency risk for non-euro investors.
  • Government bonds or company shares — €500,000, held for around three years in the shares case.
  • Startup investment — €250,000 into a company registered in the Elevate Greece National Registry of Startups, via capital increase or bond issuance. This route (Article 44 of Law 5162/2024) became operational in early 2026 after the implementing ministerial decision was published — the newest door in the programme.

The deposit route is worth a second look for pure permit-seekers: roughly €22,000–€32,000 of transaction costs on a €400,000 property purchase disappear, and the compliance is simpler. The trade-off is return: property has appreciated; a deposit hasn't.

Process and 2026 timeline

The sequence: secure a Greek tax number (AFM), open a Greek bank account, complete the qualifying investment, then file the residence-permit application with the Ministry of Migration and Asylum. Published 2026 guidance puts the wait at 6 to 12 months to a decision, with the backlog falling through 2026. While the file is pending, a temporary document (the "blue paper") lets you stay in Greece legally.

One 2026 legal detail worth knowing: under Law 5275/2026, in force since February, the five-year permit period now begins when the residence card is issued — processing time no longer eats into the permit's validity. Small change, real money: on a five-year permit, a year of processing used to cost you a year of validity.

The full cost stack

The purchase price is the start. Government fees: €2,000 for the main applicant, €150 per adult family member, €16 per residence card (minors are reported exempt from the €150). Then the property transaction stack on a €400,000 home: about 3.09% transfer tax, 1–2% notary fees, about 0.5% land registry, 1–1.5% legal fees, and possibly a 2% agent fee — roughly €22,000–€32,000 on top of the price. Budget it as part of the investment decision, not as an afterthought.

Family, stay, and citizenship

A single application covers spouse or registered partner, unmarried children under 21, and the parents of both spouses. The permit is a five-year renewable residence permit, renewed as long as the qualifying investment stays in place. There is no minimum stay requirement — zero days a year keeps the permit alive — and holders may travel in the Schengen area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period.

The citizenship question needs honesty. Greek citizenship comes after seven years of actual residence plus B1-level Greek — and "actual" is doing the work in that sentence. The permit alone, held from abroad with no days spent in Greece, does not count toward naturalisation. If a passport is the goal rather than a residence option, price that commitment — seven years of genuinely living in Greece — before choosing the programme.

What goes wrong: buying risks

The failure modes in 2026 are technical, not dramatic. A home that falls below the 120 m² main-spaces line. An incomplete change-of-use on a conversion. A short-term letting discovered on a platform. A transaction structure the authority doesn't recognise. Each one interrupts a substantial investment, and each is preventable with a Greek property lawyer reviewing the file before the purchase — not after. The programme's rules are strict but published; the risk sits in the gap between the brochure's summary and the law's text. Read the law's text.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Greece golden visa zones in 2026?

Three zones under Law 5100/2024: Zone A (€800,000) — Attica, Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini, and islands over 3,100 residents; Zone B (€400,000) — everywhere else; Zone C (€250,000) — commercial-to-residential conversions and listed heritage restorations, in any zone.

What is the minimum investment for Greece residency by investment in 2026?

The lowest real-estate figure is €250,000, but only for converting a commercial building to residential use or restoring a listed building. A standard home costs at least €400,000 outside the prime zones and €800,000 in Attica, Thessaloniki, Mykonos, Santorini and the larger islands. Non-property routes start at €350,000 (funds) and €500,000 (deposit, bonds, shares).

Can I rent out my Greece golden visa property on Airbnb?

No. Since September 1, 2024, golden-visa properties may not be let short-term (Airbnb, Booking.com) or sublet. Breaches can bring permit revocation and a reported €50,000 fine. Long-term leases are allowed.

Does the property have to be one unit?

Yes, in the €400,000 and €800,000 tracks: a single property of at least 120 m² of main living space. Combining smaller units to reach the threshold no longer qualifies.

Do I have to live in Greece to keep the permit?

No. There is no minimum stay requirement — the permit renews every five years as long as the investment stays in place. Note that citizenship later requires seven years of actual residence plus B1 Greek.

How long does the Greece golden visa take in 2026?

About 6–12 months to a decision, with the backlog falling through 2026. A temporary "blue paper" lets you stay in Greece while the file is pending, and since February 2026 the five-year permit runs from card issuance (Law 5275/2026).

Who can be included in one Greece golden visa application?

Spouse or registered partner, unmarried children under 21, and the parents of both spouses. Government fees are €2,000 for the investor, €150 per adult family member, and €16 per residence card.

Is there a non-property Greece golden visa route?

Yes: €350,000 in qualifying funds, €500,000 in a Greek bank deposit (12-month minimum, auto-renewed), bonds or shares, and a new €250,000 startup route into Elevate Greece-registered companies, operational since early 2026.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

Greece in 2026 is the last major EU property route standing — priced by postcode: €800,000 where everyone wants to buy, €400,000 elsewhere, €250,000 for conversions and restorations. Buy one unit of at least 120 m², forget Airbnb, budget ~€22,000–€32,000 of transaction costs on a €400,000 purchase, and get a Greek property lawyer reviewing before you sign.
