ETIAS 2026: The Travel Authorization That Isn't Live Yet

For two years, travel blogs have told Americans, Britons, Canadians and Australians to get ready for ETIAS — Europe's new pre-travel authorization. Here is the part most of them bury: as of October 2026, ETIAS does not exist as a working system. The launch date was quietly withdrawn, no replacement has been named, and a thriving scam industry is selling applications to a portal that has never opened. This guide gives you the honest status, who will actually need ETIAS when it launches, what it will cost, and what to do for your trip right now.

Quick answer. ETIAS — the EU's planned travel authorization for visa-exempt visitors — is not in operation as of October 16, 2026. The official ETIAS site says so plainly; the previous "last quarter of 2026" target was removed in July 2026 and no replacement date has been published. When it launches, it will cover visa-exempt nationals (US, UK, Canada, Australia, Japan and others) travelling to 30 European countries, cost a planned €20 (free for under-18s and over-70s), and be valid three years. It will not replace Schengen visas. And right now, no legitimate ETIAS application exists anywhere — any website taking your money for one is a scam.

The status in one paragraph: it isn't live

Let's start with the sentence that should open every ETIAS article on the internet and almost never does: you cannot apply for ETIAS today, and nobody can. The system has never accepted a single legitimate application. The European Union's official ETIAS pages currently describe the system as not in operation, with no launch date attached.

How we got here is a short history of moving goalposts. For years the working target was the last quarter of 2026, repeated across EU communications and duly repeated by every travel site. In July 2026, that target disappeared from the official ETIAS website — quietly, with no replacement date and no new announcement. Industry reporting since then points to 2027 as the realistic window, but that is expert speculation, not an EU position. Until the Commission names a date with several months' notice — which it has committed to doing — every "launching soon" headline is noise.

This matters more than it sounds, because the delay has a cost measured in confusion. Surveys by the travel association ABTA found awareness of the coming border changes climbing from 41% in 2024 to 56% in 2026 — but awareness of a thing that doesn't exist yet is a strange asset. It mostly feeds the businesses described later in this guide, the ones happily selling authorizations to a system with no door.

What ETIAS actually is (and the ESTA comparison)

ETIAS stands for the European Travel Information and Authorisation System. When operational, it will be a pre-travel authorization, not a visa: an online check you complete before departure, linked to your passport, that says the EU's systems have no objection to your visit. The closest comparison is the United States' ESTA — the electronic permission visa-waiver travellers get before flying to America. Like ESTA, ETIAS won't change your visa-free status; it adds a screening step on top of it.

Three properties define it. First, it's for short stays only — the familiar 90-days-in-any-180-day-period allowance that already governs visa-free travel to the Schengen area. ETIAS doesn't extend that allowance by a single day. Second, it's multiple-entry: one authorization covers unlimited trips within its validity, which is planned at three years or until your passport expires, whichever comes first. Third, it is not a guarantee of entry — border officers keep the final say, exactly as they do today.

The "why" is straightforward. Visa-exempt travellers are currently the least-screened people crossing EU borders: they arrive with no prior check at all. ETIAS closes that gap by running applicants against EU security databases — the Schengen Information System, Europol data, and others — before they board. Whether you find that reassuring or intrusive, it's the stated purpose, and it explains why the system exists separately from visas.

Who it will cover: the visa-exempt list

ETIAS is aimed at one group: nationals of countries whose citizens enter the Schengen area without a visa. That's roughly 60 nationalities, and the recognizable names are the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, New Zealand, Brazil, Argentina, Mexico, Israel and the UAE, among others. If you currently board a flight to Paris or Rome with just your passport, you are in the ETIAS group — but you need nothing extra until the system is operational.

Geographically, ETIAS will cover 30 European countries: the 29 Schengen members plus Cyprus. The non-EU Schengen states — Norway, Switzerland, Iceland and Liechtenstein — are in; Ireland is not, since it opted out of Schengen and runs its own immigration controls. This produces the oddity that a US traveller will one day need ETIAS for a weekend in Amsterdam but not for a weekend in Dublin.

Exemptions are built in. EU citizens obviously need nothing. Neither do people holding a residence permit or long-stay visa from an ETIAS country — if you legally live in Spain, your Spanish residence card is your document, not an ETIAS. Beneficiaries of the EU–UK Withdrawal Agreement are covered too. And travellers who need a Schengen visa are a separate category entirely, which brings us to the most misunderstood line in this whole topic.

Who it won't: if you need a Schengen visa, nothing changes

ETIAS has nothing to do with you if your passport requires a Schengen visa — and that includes large travelling populations: India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Philippines, Bangladesh, China, and many others. For these nationalities the Schengen short-stay (C) visa remains the entire process, before and after any ETIAS launch: the application form, the photograph, a passport issued within the last ten years and valid at least three months beyond departure, €30,000 of Schengen-wide medical insurance, evidence of purpose, funds, accommodation and return travel, biometrics stored in the Visa Information System, a €90 fee for adults (€45 for children aged 6–11, free for under-6s), and a decision rule of around 15 days.

This is worth stating bluntly because the confusion is industrial-scale. Visa agencies, forums and even some consulates' comment sections mix the two systems freely. The rule is clean: ETIAS is for people who need no visa; the visa is for people who need a visa. No ETIAS authorization will ever substitute for, shorten, or bypass a Schengen visa appointment. If you need the visa, book the appointment through the official channel and ignore ETIAS news entirely — it changes nothing about your file.

The €20 fee nobody can pay yet

The most-reported ETIAS number is also the most misunderstood. The announced fee is €20 — but "announced" is doing heavy lifting, because there is currently no open channel to pay it through. The original figure was €7, quoted in years of early EU communications; on July 17, 2025, the European Commission announced the increase to €20, formalised in Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1411, citing the system's operating costs, inflation since the fee was first set, and expanded functionality.

Two groups are exempt from paying: travellers under 18 and over 70 — though note the exemption is from the fee, not from the authorization itself; a 16-year-old still needs an ETIAS, applied for by a parent or guardian, it just costs nothing. Certain family members of EU citizens are also exempt under the reported arrangements. Everyone else in the visa-exempt group pays the €20 once per authorization, which then covers three years of travel.

Why belabour a fee you can't pay? Because it's the number scammers use. A fake site quoting €20 looks official; one quoting €50–€178 is hoping you won't know the real figure. Knowing the announced €20 — and knowing that no payment channel is open — disarms both. The fee becomes real the day the official portal opens, and not one day earlier.

What applying will look like when it opens

The EU has described the application as a short online form — about ten minutes — completed on the official ETIAS website or in the official mobile app, with no embassy visit and no biometrics collected at the application stage. You'll provide passport details, personal information, your travel plans including the country of first entry, and background questions covering security, health and immigration history: criminal convictions, past deportations, previous refusals, visits to conflict zones. The data is checked against EU databases, and most applications are expected to clear in minutes.

The edge cases matter. If your application is flagged, a manual review follows — and that can stretch from days to weeks, with the possibility of being asked for additional documents or, rarely, an interview. A refusal comes with reasons, and there's an appeal route. The practical lesson: apply well before you travel, not the night before. The authorization is linked to the passport you applied with, so renewing your passport means a new ETIAS — a detail that will catch frequent travellers who renew mid-validity.

One caution about all of the above: these are the announced arrangements for a system that isn't running. Operational details — the exact form fields, the real-world processing times under launch-week load — will only be confirmed when the portal opens. Treat today's descriptions as the blueprint, not the building.

The one rule until launch day. There is exactly one legitimate ETIAS channel: the EU's official site, and it is currently collecting nothing. Any other website offering to "process," "fast-track," or "pre-register" your ETIAS is either a lookalike scam or selling a service for a system with no door. Bookmark the official page, check it before you book — and pay no one.

ETIAS vs the Entry/Exit System: don't mix them up

Half the confusion around ETIAS comes from its sibling: the Entry/Exit System (EES). They are different machines solving different problems. EES is a border-registration system — when you cross an external EU border, it logs your entry and exit electronically, replacing the passport stamp with a database record (including biometrics taken at the border). ETIAS is a pre-travel authorization — a check you complete before you leave home. One happens at the border, the other before it; one registers movement, the other screens travellers.

The timelines are separate too, and EES has had its own difficult rollout — including well-reported software problems at French entry points that pushed the EU to delay ETIAS further rather than stack a second new system onto struggling borders. Travel-industry bodies have argued openly that ETIAS shouldn't go live until EES is fully working. When you read "EU border system delayed," check which system the article means; the answer changes what you need to do.

The scam economy the delay built

Every month ETIAS stays unlaunched is a month the scam industry monetises. The EU's border agency Frontex has confirmed more than 100 unofficial websites posing as ETIAS application portals — October 2026 reporting puts the count at 150 and climbing — charging travellers between €50 and €178 for an "application" to a system that has never accepted one. The fee disappears. The application processes nothing. The "approval" email is worthless paper.

The playbook is familiar from copycat visa sites: buy search ads against "apply ETIAS," dress the page in EU blue with stars, add a countdown timer or an "urgent" banner, and collect passports scans and card numbers from travellers who reasonably assume a government system must exist by now. Frontex's ETIAS Central Unit has warned publicly that it is "not currently possible to apply for an official ETIAS travel authorisation since the system is not operational yet" — which is about as plain as an EU agency gets.

Protecting yourself is simple and absolute: do not pay anyone for ETIAS until the official portal is open, and verify the portal through the EU's own pages rather than a search ad. A commercial site may one day legitimately offer form-filling assistance, but no intermediary can issue, guarantee, or speed up an authorization — and today, there is nothing to assist with.

What to do for your 2026 trip right now

Strip away the noise and the 2026 travel checklist is the same one it was last year. Visa-exempt travellers: your passport is enough. Keep it inside the validity rules — issued within the last ten years, valid at least three months beyond your planned departure — and count your 90 days in any 180-day period across all Schengen states together; a week in Spain and a week in Italy draw from the same allowance. Visa-required travellers: run the process that exists — the C-visa application through the official channel, the booked appointment, the full document file, the €90 fee. Everyone: if your itinerary touches the UK, remember Britain runs its own separate Electronic Travel Authorisation (£20) — another system, another portal, nothing to do with ETIAS.

The single ETIAS-related action for any 2026 trip: re-check the status on the EU's official pages before you book. Not on a blog, not on social media — on the official site. If launch day ever arrives, it will arrive there first, with a date and an application channel, months in advance.

How you'll know it's really launched

When ETIAS actually launches, you won't need a guide to tell you — but you'll want to know what a real launch looks like so you can distinguish it from the next rumor cycle. Expect three things together: an official Commission announcement naming a start date several months out; the official portal accepting real applications at the EU's ETIAS address; and a transitional period — the EU has consistently described a phased start with a grace period before the authorization becomes mandatory, precisely to avoid stranding travellers on day one.

What a real launch won't look like: a travel blog's "it's happening" post with no EU source, a paid ad promising early access, or an email from a site you never visited. The EU has every incentive to announce this loudly — it affects tens of millions of travellers — so the absence of an official announcement is the information. Until that announcement exists, the correct ETIAS preparation is a bookmark and a calendar reminder, not a credit card.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is ETIAS in operation yet?

No. As of October 16, 2026, ETIAS is not in operation. The EU removed the previous last-quarter-of-2026 target from the official ETIAS site in July 2026, and no replacement date has been published. No legitimate ETIAS applications are being collected anywhere.

How much will ETIAS cost?

The announced fee is €20, raised from the originally proposed €7 by a European Commission decision in July 2025. Travellers under 18 and over 70 are exempt from paying (but must still apply), as are certain family members of EU citizens. Nothing is payable yet — there is no open application channel.

Which travellers will need ETIAS once it launches?

Nationals of visa-exempt countries travelling to 30 European countries (the 29 Schengen members plus Cyprus) — including the US, UK, Canada, Australia and Japan. EU citizens, EU residence-permit holders, and travellers who need a Schengen visa are not in the ETIAS group. Ireland is not part of the scheme.

I need a Schengen visa. Does ETIAS change anything for me?

Nothing. If your nationality requires a Schengen short-stay visa (€90 for adults in 2026), that route is unchanged: the application, the appointment, the biometrics and the decision timeline all stand. ETIAS is a separate authorization for people who need no visa at all.

Can I apply for ETIAS now through a website that offers it?

No — any site taking money for an ETIAS application today is selling nothing. The EU's border agency Frontex has confirmed more than 100 unofficial sites posing as ETIAS portals, charging €50 to €178 for an authorization to a system that has never accepted a single legitimate application. Wait for the official portal to open.

Is ETIAS the same as the Entry/Exit System (EES)?

No. EES is a border-registration system that records entries and exits of eligible non-EU travellers at external EU borders. ETIAS is a pre-travel authorization you apply for before you fly. They are separate systems with separate timelines.

Bottom Line

ETIAS is the EU's planned travel authorization for visa-exempt visitors — announced in detail, priced at €20, valid three years, covering 30 countries — but as of October 16, 2026, it is not operational, with the Q4 2026 target withdrawn and no replacement date named. If you travel visa-free today, your passport is still the whole document; if you need a Schengen visa, the €90 appointment route is unchanged. Bookmark the EU's official ETIAS page, re-check it before you book, and pay no one for an authorization that doesn't exist yet.
