US Student Health Insurance 2026: The Decision Before the Shopping

Most international students start by comparing premiums. That is the wrong first step, and it is how students end up paying twice: once for a private plan that looks cheaper, and again for the school plan when their waiver is refused. Before you compare a single price, you need to answer one question about your own university — is its plan mandatory, waivable, or merely the default? Everything in this guide follows from that answer, including the federal floor that J-1 students cannot negotiate and the waiver test that rejects most bargain policies.

Quick answer. Do not buy a plan until your university tells you which of three doors you are behind. If the plan is mandatory, you stay on it — Northwestern, for 2026-27, grants no exceptions for international students. If it is waivable, your alternative must prove it matches the school plan, usually as Affordable Care Act-compliant coverage from a US-licensed insurer for the full academic year; travel policies and most Marketplace plans fail that test. If you are a J-1 student, federal regulation 22 CFR 62.14 sets a floor you cannot go below: at least $100,000 of medical benefits per accident or illness, $50,000 of medical evacuation, $25,000 of repatriation of remains, and a deductible of no more than $500. The school plan is often the simplest way to clear all three hurdles at once.

The decision before the shopping

Picture the sequence that traps students every autumn. A student arrives, sees a four-figure charge for the university health plan on their student account, and goes looking for something cheaper. They find a plan online at a fraction of the price, buy it, and file a waiver. Weeks later the waiver comes back refused, the charge is still on the account, and the private plan they bought cannot be refunded in full. The mistake was not the search for a better price. It was the order of operations: shopping before asking whether shopping was even allowed.

The reason the order matters is that American student health insurance is not one market. It is a stack of separate authorities, and each one answers to a different rulebook. Federal regulation governs J-1 exchange visitors. State university systems govern their own campuses. Each individual university writes its own enrollment and waiver policy. And the private market sells products that were never designed to satisfy any of those rulebooks — travel medical policies built for a two-week trip, short-term plans built for gaps between jobs, and Marketplace plans built for residents buying their own coverage. A plan can be perfectly legitimate and still be the wrong answer for your waiver.

So the first document you need is not a quote. It is your university's current student-health page, read in full: whether enrollment is automatic, whether waivers exist at all, what a waiver must prove, and the deadline for filing one. Deadlines are short — often within the first month of term — and a missed deadline can leave you on the school plan by default whether you wanted it or not. Get that page, and the waiver form if one exists, before you open a single insurer's website.

Three doors: mandatory, waivable, or merely the default

Every international student stands behind one of three doors, and the door determines the strategy. Behind the first door, the school plan is mandatory: you are enrolled, you are charged, and no alternative is accepted. Northwestern University states this plainly for 2026-27, requiring degree-seeking international students holding J-1 or F-1 visas to confirm enrollment in its student plan and maintain it for their entire time studying there, and advising students not to buy an alternate plan because exceptions will not be granted. Florida State University moved in the same direction for 2026-27: all international students are required to purchase its student plan, with waivers no longer granted except in very limited circumstances, such as non-degree or transient students and State Department-sponsored J-1 students. Behind this door, comparison shopping is a waste of time and a rejected waiver is not a negotiation — it is the policy.

Behind the second door, the plan is waivable: you are automatically enrolled unless you prove comparable coverage by a deadline. The University of St. Thomas in Minnesota works this way for 2026-27 — J-1 students are automatically enrolled in its UnitedHealthcare plan, F-1 students must opt in or file a waiver showing comparable coverage, and the fall waiver deadline is September 28, 2026. The Catholic University of America follows the same pattern, automatically enrolling F-1 and J-1 students unless an accepted waiver is submitted by September 18, 2026. Behind this door, shopping is allowed, but only against the waiver checklist — and that checklist is strict, as the next sections show.

Behind the third door, the plan is merely the default: the university offers a plan, requires you to hold some approved coverage, and lets you choose among qualifying options. This is the friendliest door, but it is also the one where students make the most expensive mistakes, because "some approved coverage" still means approved — against criteria the university sets. The difference between door two and door three is the burden of proof, not the absence of rules. In both cases, the school, not the insurer's marketing page, decides what counts.

The J-1 floor is not negotiable

If you hold a J-1 visa, one part of your decision is already made for you, and it is made in Washington rather than on your campus. Federal regulation 22 CFR 62.14 requires sponsors to ensure that every exchange visitor — and every accompanying spouse and dependent in J-2 status — holds sickness and accident insurance for the entire duration of the program as recorded in SEVIS. The regulation sets minimum coverage, and universities repeat it almost word for word on their own pages, which is how you know it is not a campus preference but a federal floor.

Medical benefitsAt least $100,000 per accident or illnessA single emergency visit can exceed a bargain policy's cap; this floor is already modest against US prices.
Medical evacuation$50,000Transport home for treatment is a separate, large cost that ordinary domestic plans often exclude.
Repatriation of remains$25,000Required even though no student wants to think about it; its absence alone fails a waiver.
DeductibleNo more than $500 per accident or illnessA high-deductible plan that looks cheap monthly can fail this test outright.
CoinsuranceUp to 25% of covered benefits per accident or illnessThe regulation allows the visitor to carry up to a quarter of costs — check what your share actually is.
Insurer standingRated A- or above (A.M. Best or equivalent), home-government backed, or a sponsor group planAn unrated insurer's policy does not satisfy the regulation, however cheap it is.

Two consequences follow that students routinely underestimate. First, the floor applies for the whole program, including periods of academic training, and it applies to dependents too — the University of South Carolina notes that J-2 dependents must hold active insurance even when they are not present in the United States. Second, the penalty for letting it lapse is not a fine. Universities warn that failure to maintain required coverage can result in termination of J-1 status, and the regulation itself treats willful failure to maintain coverage as a status violation requiring departure. A J-1 student who buys a plan that misses any one row of the table above has not saved money; they have bought a compliance problem.

There is also a practical trap inside the table: an otherwise good domestic plan can fail the J-1 test. The University of Wyoming gives the example of its own employee health plan, which does not meet the federal J-1 requirement because its minimum deductible is more than $500 and it lacks medical evacuation and repatriation benefits — so benefited J-1 employees there must buy a supplemental policy on top. If a university's own staff plan can fail the J-1 test, a random internet plan certainly can. For J-1 students, the school plan is very often the path of least resistance precisely because the school has already checked it against the regulation.

The J-1 floor is a minimum, not a target. The University of South Carolina strongly recommends carrying insurance above these minimums, and that advice is worth taking seriously: $100,000 of medical benefits is small against the cost of a serious illness or a long hospital stay in the United States. Meeting the regulation keeps your status safe. It does not guarantee that a major medical event will be fully paid for.

What a waiver actually has to prove

For students behind the waivable door, the waiver form is the real application, and it is graded like one. The question it asks is not "do you have insurance?" but "is your insurance at least as good as ours, for as long as ours, from an insurer we can actually deal with?" The Catholic University of America's waiver criteria show what that means in practice: coverage through the academic year, both emergency and non-emergency care, inpatient and outpatient mental health benefits, inpatient and outpatient medical care, ambulance transport within 50 miles of campus, and a plan from a company licensed to do business in the US with a US claims office, a US phone number, plan literature in English, and benefits provided in accordance with the Affordable Care Act. Every clause in that list exists because some student's alternative plan failed it.

Read that list again and notice what it excludes. A travel medical policy fails on several counts at once: it covers emergencies, not routine or non-emergency care; it ends after weeks or months, not at the end of the academic year; and it often excludes the mental health and preventive care the school plan includes. A short-term domestic plan fails on the Affordable Care Act requirement, because short-term plans are not required to cover pre-existing conditions or essential benefits. And a plan from a home-country insurer with no US claims office fails on administration alone, even if its benefit table looks generous — the school needs an insurer it can verify and a claims process its students can actually use.

The most instructive waiver rule comes from the University of Wisconsin-Madison, which states it without softening: individual and private insurance plans, including travel insurance and Marketplace plans, do not qualify for a waiver at all. That is not a judgment about the quality of those plans. It is a statement about what the university will accept as proof, and it shows why the waiver checklist must be read before anything is bought. A student who buys first and reads second may own a perfectly good plan that their university will never accept — and still owe the school plan charge.

There is one more waiver reality to absorb: approval is not permanent. Schools audit waiver submissions — Catholic University notes that waiver submissions are audited by its plan administrator — and a plan that lapses mid-year, or a sponsor whose coverage ends, puts you back at the start. If you do win a waiver, keep the approval letter, keep the policy active for the full academic year, and diary the renewal date. A waiver is a one-year permission, not a permanent exemption.

The premium is the smallest number

Once you know which door you are behind, the price comparison can finally begin — and the first thing to learn is that the monthly premium is the least informative number on the page. What you will actually pay in a bad year is the premium plus the deductible plus your coinsurance share plus everything the plan does not cover, and those numbers vary far more between plans than the premium does. A plan with a $500 deductible and a plan with a $5,000 deductible can have similar monthly prices and wildly different worst-case years, and the J-1 deductible cap of $500 exists precisely because regulators know this.

A concrete 2026-27 example shows the scale. The University of St. Thomas publishes its UnitedHealthcare plan for international students at $1,102 for the fall term (August 1, 2026 to January 14, 2027) and $1,310 for spring and summer (January 15 to July 31, 2027), with a shorter summer option at $477 — and notes that rates are subject to regulatory approval and may change. That is the school-plan anchor for one university in one year: roughly $2,400 for a full academic year before any deductible or copay. It is not a national average, and this guide deliberately gives no national average, because no official source publishes one and an invented average would mislead more students than it helps. What the St. Thomas figures do give you is a reality check: if an alternative plan claims to cover the same year for a small fraction of that price, something is missing from its coverage — the usual candidates are the deductible, the network, the mental health benefit, or the evacuation and repatriation cover.

The other numbers that decide your real cost are the network and the out-of-pocket maximum. A plan whose network has no in-network clinic near your campus turns every ordinary visit into an out-of-network bill. A plan with a high out-of-pocket maximum leaves you exposed in exactly the year you most need protection. Before you sign anything, check three things in writing: which clinics and hospitals near campus are in network, what you pay for an ordinary primary-care visit and for an emergency-room visit, and the maximum you could owe in one policy year. If any of those three answers is hard to find, treat that as information about the plan, not as a detail to sort out later.

Marketplace and private plans: where they fit

The Affordable Care Act Marketplace is the option students ask about most, and it is the one most surrounded by confusion — because two different questions get tangled together. The first question is whether you are allowed to buy a Marketplace plan. The second is whether your university will accept it as a waiver. The answers are different, and only the second one decides your student account charge.

On the first question, HealthCare.gov lists "individual with non-immigrant status (including worker visas and student visas)" among the immigration statuses that may be eligible for health coverage through the Marketplace. In other words, F-1 and J-1 students are lawfully present for Marketplace purposes and may buy a plan, subject to the usual residence and enrollment rules. That matters most after graduation or between jobs, when the school plan no longer covers you and the Marketplace becomes a genuine route to comprehensive, pre-existing-condition-proof coverage.

On the second question, the answer is usually no, as Wisconsin-Madison's rule shows. The reason is structural, not personal: a Marketplace plan is an individual plan bought by you, and many universities only accept waivers backed by a sponsor, an employer group plan, a government sponsor, or another institution — the kinds of coverage whose benefits and continuity the university can verify in bulk. Buying a Marketplace plan first and hoping it waives the school plan is therefore the single most common version of the autumn trap described at the start of this guide. If you are considering the Marketplace while still enrolled, get the waiver answer from your school in writing before you enroll anywhere else.

Private plans bought outside the Marketplace sit in the same position, with one extra warning attached. The private market for international students is crowded with products whose names sound comprehensive — "international student insurance," "study abroad cover" — but whose benefit tables are travel products in disguise: emergency-only coverage, low per-incident caps, exclusions for the very activities and conditions a student is most likely to claim for. The test is always the same and it is boring on purpose: does it cover the full academic year, emergency and non-emergency care, mental health, and evacuation and repatriation, from a US-licensed insurer with a US claims office? If the sales page does not answer all of those plainly, the plan has not passed the test — it has avoided it.

The gaps that catch students: arrival, OPT, dependents

Even students who choose correctly get caught by timing. Coverage gaps open at the edges of the academic year, and each edge has its own fix. The first edge is arrival: school plans typically start on a fixed date, often August 1 or the start of term, and a student who lands earlier — or whose flight is delayed, or whose enrollment is processed late — can be uninsured for days or weeks. The regulation even anticipates this for J-1 students, noting that sponsors may offer supplemental "entry to exit" coverage from departure to return. Ask your school what covers you between landing and the plan start date, and buy a short bridge policy for that window if nothing does — a bridge is the one place where a short-term travel policy is the right tool, because the gap itself is short-term.

The second edge is the end of study. Some schools require students on Optional Practical Training to stay on the student plan — St. Thomas, for example, states that all OPT students are required to purchase its plan unless proof of comparable coverage is furnished — while other schools end student-plan eligibility at graduation. The difference decides whether your OPT year starts with continuity or with a shopping exercise, so ask your international office which rule applies to you before your final term ends. If you move to employer coverage or a Marketplace plan, confirm the new start date in writing and leave no uncovered days: enrollment windows after losing coverage are time-limited, and a gap you discover after the window closes can last until the next open enrollment.

The third edge is family. Students who bring dependents face a second, parallel set of decisions: J-2 dependents must hold J-1-compliant coverage for the whole program, including when they are not in the United States, and school plans do not always cover dependents automatically — St. Thomas notes that enrolled students "may also insure their eligible dependents," which means dependents are an active choice, not a default. Price the dependent coverage explicitly, check whether it meets the same J-1 floor, and do not assume a spouse's employer plan or a home-country policy satisfies your school without checking it against the waiver list.

A checklist before you pay anything

If this guide reduces to a single page you can work through in an evening, it is this list. Work it in order, and do not skip to the price comparison until the first four items are answered in writing from your university.

1. Find your door. Is your school plan mandatory, waivable, or the default? Get the answer from your university's current student-health page, not from a forum post about another campus. If the answer is mandatory, stop here and enroll.

2. Get the waiver checklist and deadline. If waivers exist, download the form, note the deadline, and list every criterion the alternative plan must meet. If the list says Marketplace and private plans do not qualify, believe it.

3. If you are J-1, check the federal floor first. Run any candidate plan against the 22 CFR 62.14 table: $100,000 medical, $50,000 evacuation, $25,000 repatriation, $500 maximum deductible, rated insurer. One failed row ends the conversation.

4. Price the bad year, not the good month. Add premium, deductible, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum for each candidate, and check the near-campus network. Compare worst cases, not headlines.

5. Close the edges. Confirm coverage from your landing date to the plan start date, from graduation into OPT or employment, and for every dependent. A plan that covers the middle of the year and none of the edges is not a plan.

6. Keep the paper. Save the waiver approval, the policy certificate, and every start and end date. If your coverage is ever questioned — by the school, the sponsor, or an immigration officer — the paper is the answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a federal health insurance minimum for F-1 students?

No. Federal regulations set a minimum floor for J-1 exchange visitors under 22 CFR 62.14, but there is no equivalent federal floor for F-1 students. For F-1 students, the binding rule is the school's own policy, sometimes set by a state university system, and it varies from campus to campus. That is why two F-1 students at different universities can face completely different requirements.

Is my university's student health plan mandatory?

It depends on your school, and you must check its current policy rather than assume. Some schools, such as Northwestern for 2026-27, require international students to stay on the university plan and do not grant exceptions. Others allow a waiver if you prove comparable coverage. Florida State University, from 2026-27, stopped granting waivers to degree-seeking international students except in very limited circumstances. The answer is always school-specific.

Can a Marketplace plan be used to waive the school plan?

Usually not. Students in F-1 or J-1 status are lawfully present and may be eligible to buy a Marketplace plan, according to HealthCare.gov, but eligibility to buy is not the same as meeting a school's waiver standard. The University of Wisconsin-Madison states plainly that individual and private plans, including travel insurance and Marketplace plans, do not qualify for a waiver. Check your school's waiver list before buying anything.

What does a waiver application actually have to prove?

That your alternative coverage is at least as strong as the school plan, for the full academic year, through a US-licensed insurer with a US claims office. The Catholic University of America, for example, requires coverage for emergency and non-emergency care, inpatient and outpatient mental health care, ambulance transport near campus, and benefits provided in accordance with the Affordable Care Act. A cheap travel policy that covers only emergencies will fail that test.

What happens if a J-1 student lets coverage lapse?

It is a status problem, not just a billing problem. J-1 exchange visitors and their J-2 dependents must hold coverage meeting 22 CFR 62.14 for the entire program, and universities warn that failure to maintain it can lead to termination of J-1 status. J-2 dependents must hold coverage even when they are not present in the United States, according to the University of South Carolina.

What should I do about insurance when I move to OPT?

Plan the handover before your student plan ends. Some schools require OPT students to stay on the student plan, while others end eligibility at graduation, so ask your international office which rule applies to you. If you move to employer coverage or a Marketplace plan, confirm the start date in writing and leave no uncovered days between the old plan ending and the new plan starting, because enrollment windows after losing coverage are time-limited.

Bottom Line

Start with your university's door, not with a price. If the plan is mandatory, enroll and move on. If it is waivable, grade every alternative against the waiver checklist and the full academic year — most bargain policies fail on non-emergency care, mental health, or the US-licensed insurer requirement. If you are J-1, the federal floor in 22 CFR 62.14 ($100,000 medical, $50,000 evacuation, $25,000 repatriation, $500 maximum deductible) is not a suggestion, and a lapse can cost you your status, not just money. Price the bad year — premium plus deductible plus coinsurance plus out-of-pocket maximum — and close the edges at arrival, graduation, and for dependents.
