Thailand Retirement Visas 2026: O-A vs O-X Compared

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Thailand doesn't have one retirement visa — it has a one-year route (O-A) and a five-plus-five-year route (O-X) that look similar on paper but behave like completely different products. The decision most applicants get wrong isn't the money test. It's underestimating the insurance paperwork that decides both.

Both the O-A and O-X retirement visas require applicants aged 50 or older to pass a financial test and hold qualifying health insurance. The O-A grants a one-year renewable stay and asks for an 800,000-baht deposit or 65,000 baht of monthly income, while the O-X grants five years plus one five-year extension against a 3-million-baht deposit (or 1.8 million baht plus 1.2 million baht of annual income). Both require at least USD 100,000 (about THB 3,000,000) of health coverage per policy year, including COVID-19 cover. The O-A is applied for outside Thailand at a Royal Thai Embassy or Consulate; fees at the Los Angeles consulate run US$200 with at least 15 business days of processing, effective 2026.

Why the O-A's real price isn't 800,000 baht — it's the insurance form

Retirees shopping for Thailand usually start with the famous number: 800,000 baht in the bank, or 65,000 baht of monthly income. That's the financial test for the Non-Immigrant O-A, and it's been stable for years — the consulate pages updated in September 2026 still print it unchanged. But ask immigration lawyers in Bangkok what actually kills O-A applications and renewals, and they point somewhere else: the health insurance certificate.

Since the 2021 reform, O-A (and O-X) applicants must show active health coverage of at least USD 100,000 — roughly THB 3,000,000 per policy year — including COVID-19 cover, valid for the entire stay. That's not travel insurance; it's proper medical coverage, and it has to be the right paperwork, not just the right money. A Thai policy needs to come from an insurer recognized under the Thai General Insurance Association's long-stay framework. A foreign policy can qualify, but only if the insurer completes, signs, and stamps the prescribed Foreign Insurance Certificate. A membership card, a policy summary PDF, or a letter from a call center will not survive the document check.

The sharper point is about timing. Your permission to stay tracks your insurance period — if your policy covers eight months, your stay can't exceed what the insurance covers, and the whole thing caps at one year at a time anyway. Retirees who buy a policy that starts a month after their arrival date discover this the hard way: the visa follows the insurance, not the other way around. Line the two start dates up before you submit anything.

Note: The checklist is set by the embassy or consulate where you apply, and different missions enforce the insurance detail differently. Treat the LOS Angeles page's September 2026 figures as the baseline, then re-verify at your own mission before buying a policy.

O-A vs O-X: you're not choosing a visa, you're choosing a renewal calendar

On paper the O-X looks like the rich cousin of the O-A: ten years of stay against a 3-million-baht Thai bank deposit (or 1.8 million baht deposited plus 1.2 million baht of annual income), versus the O-A's one year against 800,000 baht or 65,000 baht a month. But the "ten years" is a marketing compression of what the visa actually grants: five years, then one possible five-year extension. Each phase re-tests your finances and your insurance. What you're really buying with the bigger deposit is fewer renewal seasons — five years at a time instead of one.

There are three other differences that matter more than the headline money. First, the O-X is restricted to nationals of the countries named on the consulate's O-X page — check the current published list at your mission, because eligibility, not wealth, is the first gate. The O-A has no nationality restriction. Second, the O-X demands the same THB 3,000,000-per-policy-year insurance, plus a police clearance and a medical certificate addressing the prohibited-disease list. Third, both prohibit employment, full stop — if you're imagining a slow consulting practice on the side, neither visa is your instrument.

So the real decision: if you can park 3 million baht in Thailand indefinitely and appear on the nationality list, the O-X converts a decade of yearly renewal paperwork into two events. If not — and for most retirees, the nationality list or the locked-up capital is the disqualifier — the O-A is the honest, cheaper, one-year-at-a-time product, and it's what the rest of this guide is mostly about.

The seasoning trap: a Thai bank balance with the wrong birthday

The most common way to fail the O-A's money test has nothing to do with being short of cash. It's timing. The 800,000-baht deposit must be seasoned — sitting in a Thai bank account for a set period before the application date, typically two to three months for an initial application. Applicants who wire the money a week before applying and attach a bank statement learn that the statement's date is doing as much work as its balance.

A few practical consequences follow. First, you need a Thai bank account before you apply, and opening one as a non-resident has its own paperwork dance — plan that step weeks ahead of the visa appointment, not days. Second, the balance has to survive the whole stay season: dipping below the threshold later can poison a renewal. Third, if you're using the combination method — deposit plus monthly income totaling 800,000 baht a year — document both legs cleanly; a half-documented combination is worse than either leg alone, because it gives the officer two things to question.

Who the 65,000-baht pension path is actually for

The monthly-income alternative — 65,000 baht a month, roughly US$1,800–1,900 at typical 2026 exchange rates — suits a specific profile: retirees whose money arrives reliably every month and who don't want six figures locked in a foreign bank. Military pensions, state pensions with direct deposit, and corporate defined-benefit payouts all play well here, provided you can get an income certificate that the consulate recognizes. The certificate's provenance matters as much as the number: an embassy-issued or government-issued income letter carries weight that a self-printed bank export doesn't.

The failure mode is the lumpy-income retiree. Rental income that arrives quarterly, dividends that arrive annually, a portfolio you draw from irregularly — all of these can total more than 65,000 baht a month on average and still fail the monthly framing. If your income has seasons, the 800,000-baht deposit is usually the path of least resistance, because a balance is a balance regardless of when your money moved. Match the proof to your income's shape, not to the option that sounds cheapest.

The 90-day reporting habit nobody budgets for

Once you're in on either visa, residence becomes a small administrative routine: report your address to immigration every 90 days (the TM30/TM47 system), and get a re-entry permit before any trip abroad, or your permission to stay evaporates when you leave. This is routine for long-term expats in Thailand and genuinely low-effort once it's a habit — online filing exists for the 90-day report — but it surprises retirees who imagined "long-stay" meant "set and forget."

The O-X's longer validity doesn't exempt you from the 90-day cycle; it only reduces the financial re-proof. And a re-entry permit is not optional color: leave Thailand without one and the stamp that took you months to earn is gone, whatever your visa's theoretical validity. Budget one morning per quarter and a permit before every flight, and this section of expat life becomes background noise.

The Thailand tax question retirees keep dodging

Thailand taxes residents — generally, anyone present 180 days or more in a tax year — on foreign income remitted into the country. In plain terms: money you earn abroad but don't bring into Thailand usually stays out of the Thai tax net; money you remit in can be taxable. Retirees living on a foreign pension remitted monthly to a Thai account are exactly the people this rule is written for, and the honest move is to get local tax advice before your first remittance, not after your first tax year.

This is one reason some retirees compare the O-A against Thailand's LTR "wealthy pensioner" route, which the Board of Investment runs on different terms — including an exemption on foreign income brought into the country for qualifying pensioners. The bar is much higher (US$80,000 a year of passive income, US$50,000 of insurance), and it isn't an O-A upgrade; it's a separate product with its own five-year terms. But if your pension clears that line, the tax treatment alone can be worth the comparison before you commit to the O-A's paperwork calendar.

The two documents that expire while you wait

Two of the O-A's required documents have a shelf life, and they're the two applicants most often order wrong. The police clearance — an FBI record for Americans, or the equivalent criminal-record check for other nationalities — is typically only accepted if issued within three months of the application date. The medical certificate addressing the prohibited-disease list has a similarly short window. Order both too early and they die in your drawer while the bank deposit seasons; order both too late and they hold up a filing that was otherwise ready.

Sequence the application backwards from your target submission date. The deposit seasoning is the longest pole — start it first, typically two to three months out. The insurance policy can be arranged once the filing date firms up, with the start date aligned to your planned arrival. The police clearance and medical certificate come last, in the final weeks, because they're the most perishable. And build in the consulate's own processing time: the Los Angeles mission cites a minimum of 15 business days, and practitioners recommend starting the whole e-visa process 30 to 45 days before departure. A document that expires mid-processing doesn't just delay you — in some missions it means re-filing from scratch.

One more quiet detail on the police clearance: online-only records without an authorized signature or seal are routinely rejected. Get the version that comes on official letterhead, with the stamp, from the issuing authority itself. It's a small expense that prevents a very specific, very common rejection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I apply for the Thailand O-A visa while I'm already inside Thailand?

No. The Non-Immigrant O-A is issued exclusively at a Royal Thai Embassy or Consulate outside Thailand. If you are already in Thailand on a tourist entry, the route available to you is the Non-Immigrant O (retirement), which you can obtain or convert locally and then extend for one year at a time at an immigration office.

How much health insurance do I actually need for the O-A in 2026?

The consulate checklist asks for at least USD 100,000 (about THB 3,000,000) of coverage per policy year, including COVID-19 cover, valid for your entire stay. A foreign insurer can qualify, but it must complete, sign, and stamp the prescribed Foreign Insurance Certificate — a membership card alone is not accepted.

Is the O-X visa really a 10-year visa?

Not in one grant. The O-X gives a five-year permission to stay, with one possible five-year extension, for a total of ten years. You must also keep meeting the financial test and the insurance requirement throughout.

Does my spouse get covered under my O-A or O-X?

No. Thailand's retirement visas do not include dependents. Your spouse applies separately in their own right — usually as a Non-O dependent of your status or, if they meet the age and financial tests themselves, on their own O-A.

Can I work in Thailand on an O-A or O-X?

No. Both the O-A and the O-X prohibit employment. If you intend to work, Thailand runs entirely separate routes — the LTR work-from-Thailand category and the Smart Visa — and those have their own financial tests.

How long must the 800,000 baht sit in the bank before I apply?

Consulate and immigration practice requires the deposit to be seasoned for a set period before the application date — typically two to three months for an initial application — and it must be in a Thai bank account. Check the current checklist at the embassy or consulate where you will apply, because the exact seasoning period is where applications most often fail.

Bottom Line

The O-A and the O-X test the same thing — age 50+, real money, real insurance — but they sell different calendars: one year at a time versus five plus five. Pick the O-X only if you clear the nationality list and can lock 3 million baht in Thailand; otherwise the O-A's 800,000-baht deposit or 65,000-baht monthly income is the honest route, and the insurance certificate deserves more of your preparation time than the bank statement does.
