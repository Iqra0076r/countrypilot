Spain Digital Nomad Visa Health Insurance 2026

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 14, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Spain rejects more digital nomad visa applications over insurance than any other document. Here's the exact standard — no copays, no deductibles, authorised insurers — how consulates check your certificate, what compliant cover costs, and the traps that catch people.

Quick answer. Spain's digital nomad visa requires private health insurance from an insurer authorised to operate in Spain, with no copayments, no deductibles and no waiting periods , comprehensive cover matching Spain's public system, nationwide validity and at least 12 months of cover. Travel insurance is not accepted. The minimum coverage amount varies by consulate — some accept €30,000, others want €500,000 or effectively unlimited hospitalisation — so buy the highest limits you can afford. The certificate you submit must state your name (matching your passport), policy number, coverage dates, the no-copay/no-deductible terms and the covered territory explicitly.

Why Spain takes insurance so seriously

Spain's digital nomad visa runs under the 2022 Startup Law, and the insurance rule was written with a simple principle: a non-EU resident should never become a cost to the public health system. Unlike an employee of a Spanish company, a digital nomad isn't paying social security contributions, so there's no automatic route into the public system — Seguridad Social. The private policy you submit isn't just a document; it's your entire health cover for the duration of the visa.

That's also why the standard is stricter than a Schengen tourist visa. A tourist's €30,000 travel policy is fine for a ten-day trip because the exposure window is tiny. A nomad visa is a year, potentially three. The exposure window is enormous, and consulates calibrate the insurance rule accordingly. If you're comparing countries, this is the single biggest difference between Spain and lighter-touch programmes — our hub guide to nomad visa insurance lays out the full comparison.

The requirements, point by point

Every Spanish consulate applies the same core standard. Miss one element and the application is at serious risk:

  • Authorised to operate in Spain. The insurer doesn't have to be Spanish, but it must be legally authorised to operate there — registered with Spain's insurance regulator, the DGSFP (Dirección General de Seguros y Fondos de Pensiones). If you can't verify the registration, find another provider.
  • No copayments (sin copago). The policy must pay 100% of each visit with zero out-of-pocket charge per visit. This is the requirement that sinks the most applications.
  • No deductibles. A €500 or $500 deductible disqualifies the policy, full stop. This alone rules out most standard American health plans and most travel insurance.
  • No waiting periods (sin carencias). Coverage must work from day one of your arrival. A policy with a 30- or 90-day waiting period for certain conditions won't be accepted.
  • Comprehensive, public-system-equivalent scope. Primary care, specialist visits, hospitalisation, surgery, emergency care — treat Spain's public system as the baseline your private policy must match or exceed.
  • Nationwide. Valid across all of Spain, not limited to one region or province.
  • At least 12 months. The policy must cover your first full year. Many consulates won't accept a policy that expires before the visa does.
  • Private health insurance — not travel insurance. No matter how comprehensive a travel policy sounds, it will not be accepted. Schengen travel policies, short-stay products, and EHIC cards for non-EU nationals all fail here.

One narrow exception exists: if you or your employer are covered under a bilateral social security agreement with healthcare provisions (the Spain–US treaty is the most commonly invoked), documented proof of that coverage can substitute for a private policy. That's a niche case — most applicants should assume the private policy is required.

How consulates actually check your policy

Here's what people get wrong about this step. They assume the consulate glances at the policy and moves on. In practice, consular staff read the insurance certificate closely — it's one of the few documents in the file they can verify in minutes, which is exactly why it's such a common refusal point.

What they're looking for on the certificate: your full name matching the passport exactly, a policy number and the insurer's formal details, active coverage dates that bracket your stay, an explicit statement that there are no copayments and no deductibles, and the covered territory stated as Spain (or worldwide — a vague "international" label that can't be verified is weaker).

Immigration lawyers report a consistent pattern: small copays buried in the fine print — $20 a visit, something you'd barely notice in a US plan — have caused real denials at consulates including Los Angeles and Miami. The certificate, not just the underlying policy, has to spell out the no-copay, no-deductible position in plain terms. And check the language: several consulates prefer or require the certificate in Spanish. Confirm with your specific consulate before you apply, because a certificate in the wrong language is a delay you don't need.

Minimum coverage: the consulate lottery

Spain publishes no single minimum coverage figure, and this is where applicants get contradictory advice. Some consulates specify a minimum of €30,000. Others require €500,000. Others want "unlimited" hospitalisation cover. The requirement is genuinely consulate-dependent, and there's no master list.

The practical strategy is straightforward: buy the highest coverage limits you can afford, ideally a policy that states unlimited hospitalisation. Targeting the lowest possible bar is a false economy — the premium difference between a €30,000-limit policy and a high-limit one is small next to the cost of a refused application and a second round of consulate fees. If your consulate publishes a specific figure, treat it as a floor, not a target.

Sin copago: the fine-print traps

Sin copago — no copayments — deserves its own section because it's where well-prepared applications die. American applicants are the most exposed: copays and deductibles are so normal in US health insurance that a policy can look comprehensive and still fail. A $500 deductible means the policy pays nothing until you've spent $500 — and a Spanish consulate reads that as a $500 hole in your coverage.

Before you buy, ask the insurer for the visa certificate first and read it like an officer would. Check for: per-visit copays, annual deductibles, waiting periods on specific treatments, exclusions that effectively carve out common conditions, and any clause where "100% coverage" turns out to mean "100% after the deductible." If the certificate doesn't state the no-copay position explicitly, assume the consulate will notice — because they will.

Repatriation coverage

Several Spanish consulates specifically require repatriation coverage — transport home in a medical emergency — stated in the policy. It's not universal, but it's common enough that you should treat it as required. A policy that covers treatment in Spain but says nothing about medical evacuation or repatriation leaves an obvious gap in the certificate wording, and at some consulates that gap is a refusal. When you're comparing policies, search the certificate for the word itself. If it's not there, keep shopping.

Pre-existing conditions and age

Here's the honest picture. Most visa-compliant Spanish insurers exclude pre-existing conditions — the no-waiting-period rule means they price that risk by exclusion instead. If you have a managed condition, this is the part of the process that needs the most care: you need a policy that both satisfies the consulate's wording and actually covers what you're likely to need.

Specialist guides report that at least one Spanish insurer's plan is noted for covering pre-existing conditions for applicants up to age 75 without exclusions — an exception rather than the rule, and the kind of detail worth confirming with an expat insurance broker rather than taking on trust from a blog post. Age also moves premiums sharply (more below). If you're over 60 with a health history, start the insurance search before you start the visa paperwork — this is the longest-lead-time item in the whole application for you.

What compliant cover costs in 2026

There are no official premium figures — prices are set by insurers, not the state — but insurance comparison sources report consistent bands for visa-compliant cover: roughly €45–€80 a month for applicants under 50, rising to roughly €90–€180 a month past 60. Treat these as market reports, not quotes: your age, health history and the coverage limit you choose move the number.

Budget it as a full year. The policy has to run 12 months minimum, so the real planning figure is €540–€960 a year if you're young and healthy, more like €1,080–€2,160 if you're past 60 — before any health-history loading. Compared with US health insurance prices, most Americans find this cheap. Compared with "free," which is what you'd hoped the policy would cost, it's a real line in the relocation budget. Either way, it's far cheaper than the alternative: private medical bills in Spain without insurance.

Buying and sequencing: consulate vs. in-Spain route

There are two ways to get Spain's digital nomad visa, and the insurance timing differs slightly.

The consulate route. You apply at the Spanish consulate in your country of residence. The visa is valid for one year. The insurance must be in place before you apply — the certificate goes in the file — and it must run at least 12 months from your intended entry date. Buy too early and the dates drift; buy it to align with your planned move.

The in-Spain (UGE) route. If you can enter Spain visa-free (US, UK, Canadian and Australian passport holders can), you may apply from inside Spain through the Large Companies Unit (UGE). The permit can run up to three years. The timing here is tight: you enter as a tourist with 90 days of legal stay, and the UGE expects your application filed before day 60. Everything — apostilled criminal record, employer letter, income evidence, and the health insurance — should be arranged before you land. Arriving on day one and starting from scratch is the most common sequencing mistake; by the time the paperwork is assembled, the window is nearly closed.

One more sequencing note: if there's any chance your visa is refused, buy from an insurer that offers a refund or rescheduling on visa denial. Paying for a year of cover and then losing the premium to a refusal is a double hit — and the refusal itself is the likelier expense.

Renewals: insurance stays mandatory

Getting the visa is half the story. At every renewal, you must prove continuous private health cover — letting the policy lapse between renewals is grounds for the renewal to be refused. Some guides still cite a roughly 183-day-a-year physical-presence benchmark at renewal, but recent court rulings have challenged its enforceability — confirm with an immigration lawyer before relying on it. So renewal checks two things — that you lived there, and that you stayed insured while doing it.

Over time, some employed nomads find flexibility: if your foreign employer enrolls you in a social security arrangement, the picture can change. Freelancers should assume private cover continues for as long as they hold the visa. Either way, keep every renewal certificate — the paper trail is the proof.

Insurance rejection reasons, ranked by frequency

Immigration lawyers who handle Spain's DNV describe the same failures, in roughly this order:

  • Travel insurance submitted instead of health insurance. The product category disqualifies it, regardless of how generous the limits look.
  • A copay or deductible in the fine print. The classic: a US-style plan with a $20 copay or $500 deductible that the applicant never thought to check.
  • An insurer not authorised in Spain. A strong home-country policy that isn't DGSFP-registered fails the authorisation test.
  • Waiting periods. A 30- or 90-day wait on certain conditions reads as a coverage hole.
  • A certificate that summarises instead of stating. A one-line confirmation letter rather than a certificate spelling out terms, territory, dates and the no-copay position.
  • Regional-only coverage. A plan valid in one autonomous community rather than all of Spain.
  • Missing repatriation. No mention of medical evacuation or repatriation where the consulate requires it.
  • Dates that don't bracket the stay. A policy starting after entry or expiring before the visa does.

Every item on that list is checkable before you apply. Read your certificate like the officer will — line by line, in the language they want it — and fix what you find before the file goes in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my insurer have to be a Spanish company?

No, but it must be authorised to operate in Spain — registered with the DGSFP, Spain's insurance regulator. Many international expat insurers meet this requirement. Confirm the registration before you buy, and make sure the certificate reflects it.

Can I use my American health insurance for the Spain digital nomad visa?

Almost certainly not. US health plans typically include deductibles and copays, and most aren't registered to operate in Spain — both are disqualifying. You'll need a dedicated expat or international health insurance product that meets the Spanish consulate's wording.

What is the minimum coverage amount for Spain's digital nomad visa?

It varies by consulate: some specify €30,000, others require €500,000, and some want effectively unlimited hospitalisation cover. Because there's no single published figure, the practical move is to buy the highest limits you can afford rather than targeting the lowest bar.

Is travel insurance accepted for Spain's digital nomad visa?

No. Spain requires private health insurance, not travel insurance or Schengen travel-medical products — no matter how comprehensive the travel policy looks. EHIC cards are not accepted for non-EU nationals either.

Does the insurance certificate need to be in Spanish?

Several consulates prefer or require the certificate in Spanish, so check with your specific consulate before applying. At minimum, the certificate must explicitly state your name (matching your passport), policy number, coverage dates, the no-copay/no-deductible terms and the covered territory.

Do I need the insurance in place before I apply?

Yes. The certificate goes in the application file. For the consulate route, the policy must run at least 12 months from your intended entry date. For the in-Spain UGE route, arrange the insurance before you land — you're expected to file before day 60 of your 90-day tourist stay, and arriving unprepared burns the window.

Do I need insurance to renew the visa?

Yes. At every renewal you must prove continuous private cover, and letting the policy lapse between renewals is grounds for refusal. Renewals also carry a physical-presence test of roughly 183 days a year of residence.

Bottom Line

Bottom Line

For Spain's digital nomad visa in 2026, buy private health insurance from a DGSFP-authorised insurer with no copays, no deductibles and no waiting periods, high coverage limits, nationwide validity and at least 12 months of cover — and read the certificate the way your consulate will.
