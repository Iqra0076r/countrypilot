Panama Pensionado Visa 2026: Pension, Discounts & Rules

Panama's Pensionado is the odd one out in the retirement-visa world: it hands you permanent residence the day it's approved, and it comes stapled to a 1987 law that makes restaurants, hotels, and hospitals legally cheaper for you. In 2026, that discount law nearly got rewritten — and the fight over it is the most important thing a would-be pensionado needs to know.

The Panama Pensionado visa grants permanent residence to foreigners who can document a guaranteed lifetime pension of at least USD 1,000 per month, plus USD 250 per month for each dependent, from a foreign government, international organization, or private company. Holders access the Law 6 of 1987 discount schedule — 50% on entertainment and weekday hotels, 25% at restaurants, 20% on medical consultations, 15% on hospital bills, and 10% on medicines. A 2026 bill expanding those discounts was vetoed by President José Raúl Mulino in September 2026, so the original 1987 schedule remains in force.

The pensionado is two things at once: a visa and a discount law

Most retirement visas are just permission to stay. Panama's Pensionado is permission to stay plus a legally enforceable discount card. The visa itself is a permanent-residence category run by the Servicio Nacional de Migración (SNM), Panama's national immigration service: prove a lifetime pension, get indefinite residence, no renewals, no countdown. Layered on top is Ley 6 de 1987 — the "Law 6" retirees talk about — which doesn't care about your visa at all. It cares about your status as a jubilado or pensionado, and it obliges businesses across the country to charge you less.

The discount schedule, as consolidated in the Texto Único de la Ley 6 de 1987 published in the Gaceta Oficial, reads like a cost-of-living strategy document: 50% off entertainment (cinemas, theaters, sporting events), 50% off hotels Monday to Thursday and 30% Friday to Sunday, 30% off bus, ferry, and train fares, 25% off national and international airfares, 25% off à-la-carte restaurants (15% at fast-food counters), 15% off hospital bills, 20% off medical consultations, and 10% off medicines. Businesses that refuse the discounts can be fined — the law created the enforcement mechanism, not just the promise. For a retiree budgeting monthly life in Boquete or Coronado, these aren't perks; they're line items that change what $1,000 a month actually buys.

Understanding the two-layer structure matters because the two layers are governed by different authorities and can change on different timelines — which is exactly what happened in 2026, as we'll get to.

The $1,000 number, and the word that matters more than the number

The visa's financial test is disarmingly simple: a guaranteed lifetime pension of at least USD 1,000 per month, with an additional USD 250 per month for each dependent you include. The pension must come from a foreign government, an international organization, or a private company — US Social Security, a military pension, a teacher's pension, a corporate pension from a recognizable employer. There is no minimum or maximum age; Panama cares about the pension's durability, not your birth certificate, which is why early retirees with a genuine lifetime pension qualify.

But the number isn't the gate — the word is. The pension certification, issued by the pension-granting authority and properly apostilled or consular-authenticated, must establish that the benefit is vitalicia: lifelong. SNM will reject certifications that don't say so explicitly. Term-certain annuities, lump-sum payout plans dressed up as pensions, savings drawdowns, and investment withdrawals don't substitute — the law wants a benefit that cannot expire before you do. This is where applications stall: the money exists, the paperwork just doesn't use the magic word. Before you courier anything to Panama, confirm with your pension administrator that your certification letter states the benefit is lifelong, and have it translated and apostilled in that exact form.

Plan the filing itself like a project, not an errand. Lawyers in Panama typically report three to six months from a complete filing to approval, and you must be physically present in Panama when the visa is issued. Document validity windows are tight — police certificates and pension certifications generally must be recent — so ordering paperwork too early is as dangerous as ordering it too late. Arrive with documents that are fresh, not with documents that are "about right."

The $750 shortcut nobody at the embassy will advertise

There's an exception buried in the Pensionado regulations that changes the math for buyers: a retiree who personally owns Panamanian property purchased for more than USD 100,000 can reportedly qualify with a lifetime pension as low as USD 750 per month. You won't find it on most embassy checklists — it's the kind of provision lawyers know and websites simplify — and because it sits in the implementing regulations rather than the headline law, its application can vary.

Treat it as a hypothesis to verify, not a guarantee. If you're already planning to buy a home in Panama, ask your lawyer to confirm the current SNM position on the reduced floor before you structure your application around it. The same caution applies to the dependent math: a married couple applying on one pension faces the $1,250 combined threshold, while couples who can combine two lifetime pensions may qualify against the $1,000 base — a quirk of the rules that makes two modest pensions more powerful than one slightly larger one. Your lawyer should map which configuration applies to your household before anyone files.

Law 6 in 2026: what the veto changed, and what it didn't

Here's the story that matters more than any requirement list. In August 2026, Panama's National Assembly passed Bill 226 in third debate — 47 votes to 1 — to expand the Law 6 discount schedule for the first time in a generation: wider entertainment discounts, bigger hotel rebates on weekends, and entirely new categories like internet service, cable television, and mobile phone plans. For a month, it looked like the pensionado discount card was about to get substantially more valuable.

Then, in September 2026, President José Raúl Mulino vetoed the bill in full. His stated reasons were inconvenience and unconstitutionality — he argued that several provisions contravened existing legislation and the Constitution, and that the fiscal design (full income-tax deductibility of all discounts via transferable tax credits) was unworkable. Panamanian press reported the veto as total: none of the expanded discounts entered into force, and the penalties and new benefit categories died with the bill. The objection was to the reform, not to the law — everything Law 6 of 1987 already grants stays granted.

As of October 2026, retiree associations have presented a new, negotiated reform bill — one that reportedly keeps some discount expansions (utilities, internet, a higher medicine discount) while adding safeguards the private sector demanded. It's still early in the legislative process, with the Assembly's ordinary sessions scheduled to end October 29. The honest read for anyone applying now: budget your retirement on the 1987 schedule, which is law and has survived every challenge for nearly forty years. Anything the new bill adds is upside, not something your plans should depend on.

Note: Discount rules are written in Spanish legal text and interpreted by the ACODECO consumer authority in practice. If a specific discount matters to your budget — say, the 25% airfare reduction — verify how local carriers implement it before you count on it monthly.

What permanent residence from day one actually changes

The Pensionado's most underrated feature is structural: it's permanent residence from approval, not a temporary permit you graduate from. Compare that to Costa Rica's Pensionado (temporary, renewable, with a three-year wait for permanence) or Thailand's O-A (one year at a time): Panama hands you the cédula E resident ID and indefinite status in a single process. There is no renewal season, no re-proof of the pension, no permit-expiry cliff.

That permanence compounds. After roughly five years of residence, pensionados may apply for Panamanian citizenship — a real, if discretionary, path that most retirement visas in the region don't offer at any price. Two caveats, stated plainly: naturalization is at the state's discretion, not a right, and Panama doesn't formally recognize dual citizenship — the Constitution asks naturalized citizens to renounce prior nationality, though enforcement varies in practice. Even if you never take citizenship, permanence means your legal status stops being a project and starts being background. For retirees who have watched friends in other countries rebuild their paperwork every two years, that's the actual luxury.

The no-work rule and the business-shaped loophole

The Pensionado visa does not authorize employment. The premise of the category is that you are retired from working life, and the law follows that premise to its conclusion: no work permits for pensionados. What you can do is own a business and manage it — the capital is welcome; your labor is not. The line immigration lawyers draw is practical: you may not perform a job that a Panamanian could be hired to do. Running a bed-and-breakfast means you can own the property, set the strategy, and collect the returns — but if you're the one cooking breakfast and cleaning rooms, you're on the wrong side of the line.

If your retirement plan includes real work — consulting, a salary, a hands-on business — the Pensionado isn't your visa. Panama's Friendly Nations visa, which does lead to a work permit, is the lane lawyers usually point to instead. Be honest with yourself at the decision stage, because the Pensionado's permanence is only an advantage if the category actually fits your life. A permanent visa you have to work around is worse than a temporary visa you don't.

The carnet at the till: how the discounts actually work

Law 6's discounts sound abstract until you're standing at a restaurant counter in David or a clinic in Panama City. In practice, you prove beneficiary status with your cédula E (the resident ID) or your pensionado carnet, and the merchant applies the statutory percentage. The big chains know the drill cold — hotel front desks in Boquete will ask for the carnet before you do. Small businesses sometimes don't, which is why the law created ACODECO, the consumer protection authority, as the enforcement arm: establishments that refuse the discounts face fines, and the law requires businesses to display the discount schedule visibly.

Two practical notes from how retirees actually use the system. First, the discounts apply to the regular tariff — this was one of the friction points in the 2026 reform debate, where the vetoed bill would have extended them to promotional rates. Budget on regular prices. Second, the medical discounts compound with Panama's already low private-healthcare prices in a way that genuinely changes the healthcare calculus: a 20% consultation discount on a $30 specialist visit and 10% on prescriptions means routine care costs a fraction of US prices even before any insurance. That's not a visa benefit — it's a cost-of-living structure — and it's the part of the Pensionado package that shows up in your budget every single week.

If a merchant pushes back, you have two tools: the visible-discount requirement (ask them to show the posted schedule) and ACODECO's complaint process. Most retirees report they rarely need the second one. A law that has survived nearly forty years of enforcement tends to be obeyed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much pension do I need for the Panama Pensionado visa?

You need a guaranteed lifetime pension of at least USD 1,000 per month, plus USD 250 per month for each dependent. The pension must come from a foreign government, an international organization, or a private company, and the certification must confirm it is lifelong.

Is there an age requirement for the Pensionado visa?

No. Panama's immigration service sets no minimum or maximum age for the Pensionado category. The qualifying test is the lifetime pension itself, so early retirees with a qualifying pension can apply.

What discounts do Pensionado holders actually get?

Under Law 6 of 1987, beneficiaries get 50% off entertainment, 50% off hotels Monday to Thursday (30% Friday to Sunday), 25% at restaurants, 20% on medical consultations, 15% on hospital bills, and 10% on medicines, among other benefits. A 2026 bill to expand these discounts was vetoed by the president in September 2026, so the original 1987 schedule remains the law.

Can I work in Panama on the Pensionado visa?

No. The Pensionado category does not authorize employment, since the premise of the visa is that you are retired from working life. You may own and manage a business, but you cannot perform a job that a Panamanian could do. If you plan to work, the Friendly Nations visa is the usual alternative route.

How long does Pensionado processing take?

Panamanian immigration lawyers typically report three to six months from a complete filing to approval. You must be physically present in Panama when the visa is issued.

Does the Pensionado visa lead to citizenship?

After roughly five years of residence, Pensionado holders may apply for Panamanian citizenship, though naturalization is discretionary. Panama does not formally recognize dual citizenship, and naturalized citizens are constitutionally asked to renounce prior nationality, though enforcement varies in practice.

What happened to the 2026 Law 6 discount expansion?

The National Assembly passed Bill 226 in August 2026 to expand retiree discounts — including on utilities, internet, and airfares — but President José Raúl Mulino vetoed it in full in September 2026 on constitutional and legal grounds. None of the new discounts took effect. Retiree groups presented a new reform bill in October 2026, which is still working through the legislative process.

Bottom Line

Panama's Pensionado remains the hemisphere's cleanest retirement deal: a $1,000-a-month lifetime pension buys permanent residence from day one, and a 38-year-old discount law makes daily life measurably cheaper. The 2026 lesson is to build your budget on the 1987 discount schedule — the law that survived a veto — and treat any future expansion as a bonus, not a plan.
