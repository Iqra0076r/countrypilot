B-1 vs B-2 Visa: What's the Difference?

B-1 is for business, B-2 is for tourism — but most travellers receive a combined B-1/B-2 visa anyway. This guide explains what each category actually authorizes, where the line sits in practice, and what never changes regardless of the letter on your visa.

Quick Answer

B-1 covers business visits (meetings, conferences, consultations); B-2 covers tourism (holidays, visiting family and friends, medical treatment). Both cost the same $185, use the same DS-160 form and interview, and are very often issued as a single combined B-1/B-2 visa — so most applicants never need to pick one. Neither authorizes employment of any kind.

How to use this comparison

The short version: for most applicants this comparison is informational rather than a real choice, because consular officers very often issue the two categories as a combined B-1/B-2 visa. Read it to understand what each letter authorizes, to describe your trip accurately at the interview, and to avoid the one genuine trap — doing something the category does not cover. The full application sequence behind both is in our United States Visa & Entry Requirements Guide; the facts below are drawn from it and verified against travel.state.gov in October 2026.

What the B-1 visa covers

The B-1 is the business visitor category. It covers attending business meetings and conferences, consulting with business associates, negotiating contracts, and settling estates — in short, commercial activity that does not amount to employment in the United States. The key legal idea is that the traveller's real work and payroll remain abroad; the US trip is the business purpose, not the workplace.

What B-1 does not cover matters more: it never authorizes employment — not a US job, not remote work for a foreign employer while physically in the US, not unpaid "helping out" at a family business. Physical presence while working is what counts, not where the employer sits. If the trip involves productive work performed in the US, B-1 is the wrong category and a work-authorized route is needed instead — see our US work permit guide for how those routes differ.

What the B-2 visa covers

The B-2 is the tourist visitor category: holidays and sightseeing, visiting family and friends, and medical treatment in the US. It is the category behind the classic family visit — and the one most often refused under INA section 214(b) when the officer is not convinced the applicant will return home, which is why the evidence file (employment, property, family ties, prior compliant travel, fundable trip) matters so much.

B-2 shares B-1's hard boundary: no employment, no study. Entering as a tourist intending to "sort out" work or enrolment later is misrepresentation at entry — a separate ground of inadmissibility, far harder to overcome than a 214(b) refusal. If study is the real plan, the route is F-1 status with SEVIS registration and a Form I-20, covered in our US student visa guide.

The combined B-1/B-2 most people get

In practice, consular officers frequently issue a single visa annotated B-1/B-2, covering both business and tourism travel for the life of the visa — often up to 10 years with multiple entries. That is why the "which one do I apply for?" question rarely changes the outcome: you complete one DS-160, pay one $185 fee, attend one interview, describe the actual trip honestly, and the officer issues the appropriate annotation.

The combined visa is also the practical answer for mixed trips — a conference plus a week of sightseeing, or a business visit that includes seeing relatives. You do not need two visas and you do not need to pick a side; the annotation covers both purposes, and at each entry the CBP officer admits you for the purpose you state, up to the usual 6-month ceiling recorded on the I-94.

Where the line sits in practice

The line that matters is not between B-1 and B-2 — it is between visiting and working. Attending a conference as a B-1 visitor is fine; staying on to do the project work is not. Visiting family as a B-2 tourist is fine; "helping out" unpaid at the family shop crosses into unauthorized employment. Short recreational courses incidental to a visit are tolerated; enrolling in a degree program on a visitor visa is not — and attempting to adjust status from a visit is exactly the misrepresentation trap the interview is designed to catch.

When the purpose is genuinely unclear, describe the primary purpose at the interview and in the DS-160, and keep every answer consistent with the documents. Vague itineraries read as risk; a dated plan with bookings and a return ticket reads as a genuine visit — the same 214(b) logic applies whichever letter is on the foil.

What never changes, whichever you hold

Several rules are identical across B-1, B-2 and the combined visa. The $185 fee covers the application, never a guaranteed outcome. The CBP officer — not the visa — sets each admission, commonly up to 6 months on the electronic I-94 (check it at i94.cbp.dhs.gov after every entry); the visa's multi-year validity is not a permitted stay. Each family member needs their own DS-160 and interview, infants included. The passport should be valid six months beyond the stay unless the country is exempt (the UK is). And overstaying — even briefly — can void the visa and trigger 3- or 10-year re-entry bars, so holders who need more time must apply to USCIS for an extension before the I-94 expires.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Stress-picking B-1 vs B-2 on the application. Describe the real trip; officers routinely issue the combined B-1/B-2 anyway.
  • Working on a visitor visa "because the employer is abroad." Physical presence while working is what counts — B-1 and B-2 never authorize employment.
  • Entering as a tourist to arrange work or study. Misrepresentation at entry is a separate ground of inadmissibility.
  • Reading a 10-year visa as a 10-year stay. The I-94 date governs each admission.
  • Booking non-refundable travel before the interview when your post's wait runs to many months (the State Department's July 2026 table showed ~8.5–10.5 months at Indian posts, ~7.5–8 in Pakistan, ~2 in London).
  • Letting the passport slip under six months' validity — airlines will deny boarding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I apply for a B-1 and a B-2 visa separately?

No. There is one application — one DS-160, one $185 fee, one interview — and officers very often issue a combined B-1/B-2 visa covering both business and tourism travel. Describe your actual trip honestly and the officer issues the appropriate annotation.

Can I work in the US on a B-1 business visa?

No. The B-1 covers business visits — meetings, conferences, consultations — not employment. Working in the US in any form, including remote work for a foreign employer while physically present, requires a work-authorized immigration category.

I'm attending a conference and then sightseeing — which visa do I need?

The combined B-1/B-2, which is what officers usually issue anyway. One visa covers the mixed trip; at each entry, tell the CBP officer the purpose of that visit.

Is the fee the same for B-1 and B-2?

Yes — both carry the same $185 machine-readable-visa fee as of 2026, the same DS-160 form and the same interview. Since most applicants receive the combined B-1/B-2, there is a single fee in practice.

How long can I stay on a B-1 or B-2 visa?

The CBP officer decides at each entry and records it on your electronic I-94 — commonly up to 6 months, but it can be shorter. The visa's validity (often up to 10 years) is not the permitted stay. Check your I-94 at i94.cbp.dhs.gov after arrival.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

B-1 means business visits, B-2 means tourism — same $185 fee, same form, same interview, and usually one combined B-1/B-2 visa anyway. The distinction that actually matters is visiting versus working: neither category authorizes employment or study, and misrepresenting your purpose at entry carries far worse consequences than picking the "wrong" letter. Describe the real trip, evidence your ties home, and let the officer annotate accordingly.
