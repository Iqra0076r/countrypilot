Current official context — checked 5 October 2026

For Norway's skilled-worker route, UDI generally requires a concrete job offer from one specific employer; the job normally must be full-time (an 80% position can be accepted), require skilled-worker qualifications, and offer pay/conditions not poorer than normal in Norway. Since 2026, some applicants filing from abroad must use an employer-confirmed job-offer code as an anti-fraud measure.

Official page: https://www.udi.no/en/want-to-apply/work-immigration/skilled-workers/

Teaching: where sponsorship is most plausible

For teaching roles in Norway, sponsorship is most plausible where employers have persistent skill needs, the occupation fits an immigration route and the candidate can satisfy professional or technical requirements. Do not infer sponsorship from sector demand alone. Verify the occupation classification, licensing/registration and employer's legal ability to hire from overseas.

Tailor the CV to the country's terminology and make regulated credentials obvious. For healthcare, teaching and some engineering roles, professional registration can be the real bottleneck even when jobs are plentiful. Separate the job search from immigration eligibility

Search for jobs and immigration eligibility in parallel. Skilled-worker permits depend on qualifications, a concrete job and Norwegian employment conditions. Create a one-page route summary before applying: route name, sponsor requirement, occupation rule, salary/skill requirement, language rule, professional registration and whether dependants can accompany.

This prevents a common failure: investing months in a job that cannot support the intended immigration route. How to verify an employer or sponsor

Use official sponsor/accreditation registers where they exist, the company's own careers site, business registry details and a professional email domain. Compare the legal employer name on the offer with the entity in the sponsor register. A recruitment agency may be legitimate, but it should be clear who the actual employer is. Red flags include payment for a job offer, requests to reimburse prohibited sponsor costs, interviews conducted only through anonymous messaging apps, contracts with a different company name, and salary promises that are inconsistent with the written offer. Build an overseas-ready application

A useful international CV is evidence-led: role, employer, dates, quantified responsibilities, tools/technologies, regulated credentials and language ability. Remove vague objective statements and focus on the requirements in the vacancy. In the cover letter, answer the employer's risk questions directly: Are you eligible for the occupation? Do you need sponsorship? Are your qualifications recognised? When can you relocate? If the employer uses a formal sponsorship programme, say which route you believe applies without pretending to give legal advice. Offer-stage checks

Before resigning or paying relocation costs, verify salary, hours, probation, location, remote-work expectations, relocation support, visa costs, health insurance and family benefits. Check whether the contract is conditional on immigration approval. From job offer to lawful work

The employer and applicant may have separate steps. In some systems the employer first obtains accreditation, sponsorship approval, labour-market approval or a job check; only then can the worker apply. In others, the processes are linked. Use https://www.udi.no/en/want-to-apply/work-immigration/ to map the actual sequence.

Do not start work until the status authorises it. Entry as a visitor or student does not automatically permit full-time employment.
