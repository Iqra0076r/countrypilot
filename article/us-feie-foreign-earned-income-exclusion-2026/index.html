US Foreign Earned Income Exclusion 2026: $132,900

The United States taxes its citizens on worldwide income no matter where they live — but the foreign earned income exclusion lets qualifying Americans shield up to $132,900 of 2026 earnings from US income tax. This guide explains what the number actually covers, the two very different tests that unlock it, the housing benefit hiding behind it, and how to claim it on Form 2555. This is information to help you understand the rules, not tax advice: run your own facts past a qualified US tax professional before you file.

For tax year 2026, qualifying US citizens and resident aliens can exclude up to $132,900 of foreign earned income from US income tax (Revenue Procedure 2025-32, announced October 2025). You must have a tax home in a foreign country and pass either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test (330 full days abroad in 12 months). Claim it on Form 2555 — it is never automatic, and it does not remove self-employment tax.

What $132,900 actually shields — and what it doesn't

Start with the number, because everything else hangs off it. In its annual inflation adjustments for tax year 2026, the IRS set the foreign earned income exclusion at $132,900 per qualifying person, up from $130,000 for 2025. A married couple filing jointly, where both spouses independently qualify, can exclude up to $265,800 combined. Those figures apply to income earned in calendar year 2026 — the return you will file in 2027.

Now the part people get wrong: the exclusion covers foreign earned income, and both adjectives do work. "Earned" means pay for work you performed — wages, salaries, professional fees, and self-employment earnings. "Foreign" means the work was physically performed outside the United States. Your employer's address is irrelevant; a salary paid in dollars by a US company counts as foreign earned income if you did the work from Toronto, Lisbon, or Bangkok.

What falls outside is just as important. Pensions, dividends, interest, capital gains, gambling winnings, and most rental income are not earned income and cannot be excluded. Neither can pay for work you performed inside the United States, even if you were paid abroad. If your income is a mix — say a salary plus a stock sale — the exclusion shields the salary and leaves the stock sale fully taxable.

And the exclusion is not all-or-nothing. If you qualify for only part of the year — because you moved abroad in March, for example — the maximum is prorated to the number of qualifying days. Planning a mid-year move? That proration is the first thing to model.

The two doors in: bona fide residence vs the 330-day count

The IRS offers two completely different ways to qualify, and choosing the right one is the single most consequential decision in this guide.

Door one: the bona fide residence test. You must be a bona fide resident of a foreign country — or countries — for an uninterrupted period that includes an entire tax year (January 1 through December 31). There is no day-counting. The IRS judges residence on your facts: where you live, where your life is centered, your intention to stay. You do not acquire bona fide residence merely by being present for a year; but equally, brief trips home do not destroy it. US citizens may use this test, and so may resident aliens who are citizens or nationals of a country with which the US has an income tax treaty in effect.

Door two: the physical presence test. You must be physically present in a foreign country or countries for at least 330 full days during any period of 12 consecutive months. A "full day" is a 24-hour period starting at midnight — travel days generally do not count. The 12-month window is yours to choose; it does not have to be the calendar year, and you can pick the window that maximizes your exclusion. Any US citizen or resident alien can use this test.

Both tests share one more requirement that trips people up: your tax home must be in the foreign country throughout the qualifying period. Your tax home is generally your main place of business or employment. If your tax home stays in the United States — if you are, in the IRS's eyes, temporarily abroad while your real working life remains stateside — neither test will save you.

Why the physical presence test breaks nomads in December

Here is where the two tests diverge in real life. The bona fide residence test is about intention and roots; the physical presence test is about arithmetic. And arithmetic is unforgiving.

The classic failure looks like this: a nomad spends January through November working from Mexico City and Medellín, flies home for the holidays in December, stays three weeks, and discovers she has only 327 full days abroad in her chosen 12-month window. Three days short — the entire exclusion for the year is gone. Not reduced. Gone. The IRS is explicit that even a few days out of compliance can disqualify a filer, and there is no partial credit under the physical presence test.

This is why experienced expats treat the 330-day test as a calendar discipline, not a retrospective calculation. Count before you book the flight home. Remember that days in international waters or in the air do not count as foreign days. And remember that the 12-month period must include part of the tax year for which you claim the exclusion — you cannot borrow days from a window that sits entirely outside 2026.

The bona fide residence test, by contrast, forgives the December trip home — a bona fide resident can vacation in the US without breaking residence. But it demands something harder to manufacture: a genuine, settled life abroad for a full calendar year. Nomads who move countries every few months often cannot honestly claim bona fide residence anywhere, which pushes them back to the 330-day count. Know which test fits your actual life before the year starts, not when you file.

Housing costs: the second exclusion hiding behind the first

Most explainers stop at the $132,900. But Form 2555 carries a second benefit that can be worth thousands more: the foreign housing exclusion (or the foreign housing deduction, for the self-employed).

The mechanics: qualifying housing expenses — rent, utilities (not telephone), insurance, occupancy taxes, furniture rental, parking — that exceed a statutory base amount can also be excluded from income. The base amount is 16% of the maximum exclusion, and the general cap is 30% of it. For 2026, that works out to roughly $21,264 a year as the base and $39,870 a year as the general limit. Spend $30,000 on qualifying housing and you can potentially exclude about $8,700 on top of the FEIE itself.

Two catches. First, the housing exclusion only applies to amounts paid with employer-provided income — employees exclude it; the self-employed deduct it instead, which is economically similar but mechanically different. Second, the IRS publishes higher limits each year for high-cost cities — London, Hong Kong, Singapore, and dozens of others get location-specific caps well above the general $39,870. If you live in an expensive city, check the current year's IRS notice before assuming the general limit applies.

Moving between countries also means moving money — when you repatriate savings or fund the move itself, compare the real cost with the money transfer fee calculator → rather than your bank's headline rate.

Form 2555: the filing that makes it real

Nothing in this guide happens automatically. The exclusion is claimed on IRS Form 2555, filed with your annual return — and the IRS will not grant it to you if you forget. The form walks you through the qualifying test you are using, your foreign earned income, and the housing computation.

A few filing realities worth knowing. Americans living abroad get an automatic two-month extension, so the 2026 return is due June 15, 2027 rather than April 15 — though any tax you owe still accrues interest from April 15. If you need more time, you can request the standard extension to October 15. You must attach Form 2555 to a timely filed return (including extensions); claiming the exclusion years late requires special relief procedures that you do not want to need.

Keep records as if you will be audited, because the tests are factual. For the physical presence test, that means a travel log: every entry and exit date, every country. For the bona fide residence test, it means evidence of a settled life — lease, local registrations, the texture of residence. And keep your housing receipts; the housing exclusion is only as good as its documentation.

Note: The figures in this guide are the IRS's published 2026 amounts. Tax law changes — the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 already reshaped several adjacent provisions — so re-verify the current year's Revenue Procedure before you file, and get professional advice for anything beyond a straightforward salary.

The traps: self-employment tax, the credit you can't stack, and the states

Three traps catch even careful filers.

Trap one: the FEIE does not touch self-employment tax. If you are self-employed abroad, the exclusion removes your net earnings from income tax, but you still owe the 15.3% self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) on those earnings. The only escape is a totalization agreement — the US has them with about 30 countries — that assigns your coverage to the other country's system. Freelancers who model "zero US tax" on the FEIE alone are in for an unpleasant April.

Trap two: you cannot stack the Foreign Tax Credit on excluded income. It is one or the other per dollar: income you exclude under the FEIE cannot also generate a foreign tax credit, and you cannot deduct foreign taxes attributable to excluded income. In high-tax countries, some filers do better skipping the FEIE entirely and using the Foreign Tax Credit alone — especially once income exceeds the exclusion cap. Run both calculations; the right answer depends on your country and your income level.

Trap three: your state may not care about the FEIE. Most states start their tax computation from federal adjusted gross income — which already reflects the exclusion — but several states (California most famously) do not conform to the FEIE at all. Moving abroad does not automatically end state tax residence either; states have their own sticky residency rules. The federal exclusion is federal only.

One more interaction worth flagging: the exclusion does not exempt you from FBAR and FATCA reporting. Foreign bank accounts over $10,000 aggregate still trigger FinCEN Form 114, and FATCA Form 8938 thresholds still apply. The FEIE is about income tax, not information reporting — the paperwork continues regardless.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the foreign earned income exclusion for 2026?

$132,900 per qualifying person for tax year 2026, up from $130,000 for 2025, set by the IRS in Revenue Procedure 2025-32. It applies to foreign earned income only — wages, salaries, and self-employment earnings for work performed abroad — and you claim it on Form 2555.

Do I have to live abroad 330 days to claim the FEIE?

Only if you use the physical presence test. The alternative bona fide residence test requires no day-counting: you must be a bona fide resident of a foreign country for an uninterrupted period that includes an entire tax year. US citizens may use either test.

Does the FEIE cover rental income or capital gains?

No. The exclusion covers earned income — pay for work you performed abroad. Pensions, dividends, interest, capital gains, and most rental income do not qualify and remain taxable.

Do I still owe self-employment tax if I claim the FEIE?

Yes. The exclusion removes qualifying income from income tax, but self-employed Americans still owe the 15.3% self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) on their net earnings, unless a totalization agreement with the country of residence says otherwise.

What is the foreign housing exclusion?

A second, separate benefit on Form 2555: qualifying housing costs above a base amount can also be excluded (or deducted, for the self-employed). The base amount is 16% of the maximum exclusion and the general cap is 30% — about $21,264 and $39,870 for 2026 — with higher limits the IRS publishes annually for high-cost cities.

Can both spouses claim the FEIE?

Yes, if each spouse independently qualifies — the exclusion is per person, not per return. A qualifying couple filing jointly can exclude up to $265,800 of combined foreign earned income for 2026.

When do Americans abroad have to file?

US taxpayers living abroad get an automatic 2-month extension, making the deadline June 15 rather than April 15, though any tax owed still accrues interest from April 15. You must still file a return to claim the FEIE on Form 2555 — the exclusion is never automatic.

Bottom Line

The FEIE is the most powerful tool in the American expat's tax kit — $132,900 of 2026 earnings shielded from US income tax — but it rewards planning, not improvisation. Pick your qualifying test before the year starts, count your days if you pick the 330-day one, claim it properly on Form 2555, and remember what it does not do: no self-employment tax relief, no stacking with the Foreign Tax Credit, and no protection from state taxes or information reporting.
