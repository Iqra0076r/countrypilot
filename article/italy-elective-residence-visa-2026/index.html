Italy Elective Residence Visa 2026: The €31,000 No-Work Route

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Italy's elective residence visa asks more of you upfront than any other European retirement route: a signed home and roughly €31,000 a year in passive income, documented before you ever board the plane — in exchange for a work ban that is absolute. Here is how the 2026 process actually works, from the consulate checklist to the questura.

Italy's elective residence visa for 2026 requires roughly €31,000 a year in stable passive income for the main applicant — plus a home in Italy, owned or rented on a registered long-term lease, secured before you apply. All work is forbidden, employed or self-employed. You apply at the Italian consulate for a national visa, then convert it into a permesso di soggiorno at the local questura after arrival.

Why Italy makes you sign a home before you can even apply

Every European retirement visa wants proof of accommodation. Italy is the only one that makes it a structural pillar of the application rather than a box to tick. The official consulate checklists require documentation proving the availability of owned or rented accommodation to be used as your residence — and for a rental, the contract must already be signed and filed with the Revenue Agency. A printout of a rental listing, a hotel booking, a friend's spare room: none of these count.

The reasoning is characteristically Italian: the state wants evidence of rootedness before it grants you the right to root yourself. A registered lease is a legal commitment — it is filed, taxed, and enforceable — and it tells the consulate you have done more than browse property portals. Some consulates also accept, on the purchase side, a preliminary purchase contract drawn up by an Italian notary with proof of the down payment settled. But purchase is never mandatory. A genuine, registered, long-term lease satisfies the requirement completely.

This housing-first design has a practical consequence most applicants underestimate: you need an Italian address strategy before you have the visa that lets you live at it. That means engaging with the Italian rental market — or a notary — from abroad, usually with help. Budget for that help, and for the timing: finding a landlord willing to sign a registered long-term lease with a tenant who does not yet hold a residence permit is the part of the process where a good local contact earns their fee.

The housing-first rule makes the budget math unusual — lease deposits, registration taxes, and setup costs land before the visa does. Map the full first-year spend with the relocation budget calculator → before you sign anything.

The €31,000 figure: a 2000 ministerial table, still setting the bar

The income test for elective residence is defined with bureaucratic precision: financial resources must not be less than three times the annual amount set out in Table A of the Ministry of the Interior directive of 1 March 2000 — which the consulates themselves translate as a minimum of approximately €31,000 a year for the main applicant. It is an old figure wearing a new suit: the directive dates from 2000, but it remains the legal anchor, and the consulate checklists published in 2026 still cite it directly.

What counts toward the €31,000 matters more than the number. The official wording demands substantial, stable, and regular independent financial resources, reasonably expected to continue over time, derived from sources other than employment. The embassy guidance lists the qualifying sources: pensions or annuities, personally owned real estate, personally held stable economic or commercial activities, or any other kind of annuity — expressly excluding any income deriving from employment.

Note: "stable economic or commercial activities" does not mean your salary from running a business. Consulates distinguish between income from assets you own (dividends, rents, royalties) and income from work you do. If the money stops when you stop working, it is not elective-residence income.

The consulates also assess the resources with unusual thoroughness: the guidance speaks of documented guarantees regarding ample and constant economic resources over at least the last year, cross-reading bank statements, securities, company shares and financial statements, plus documents certifying real-estate ownership and leases. Bring a full year of evidence, not a snapshot — and make sure the story it tells is one of continuity.

The absolute work ban — and why "a little consulting" ends applications

Among European retirement visas, Italy's work prohibition is the most absolute in its wording. The official checklist includes a declaration on the possession of sufficient resources to live in Italy without being engaged in any work activity — and the guidance specifies that this means no employment and no self-employment, in Italy or, in practical terms, as the economic basis of your life there.

This is stricter than it first appears. On Portugal's D7, residence-permit holders retain a general right to professional activity under immigration law, even though the visa is for passive income. Italy's elective residence route has no such safety valve: the visa's entire legal premise is that you live on your own resources. A file that shows "a little consulting on the side" is not a file with a minor blemish — it is a file that contradicts the visa's premise. Remote work for foreign clients falls under the same ban. If you need to keep earning, Italy has separate routes — the digital-nomad visa and the self-employment visa — that are built for exactly that, with their own income tests.

The practical upshot: treat the elective residence visa as a one-way door on work. The income you show at application must be income that survives your retirement from earning, because the permit offers no legal path back into earning later without changing status.

Spouses and children: the 20% and 5% add-ons that vary by consulate

The official embassy guidance sets the family arithmetic: the income requirement increases by at least 20% for a spouse and at least 5% for each child. On the €31,000 base, that means roughly €37,200 a year for a couple and about €38,750 for a couple with one child — before any consulate-specific interpretation.

And interpretation is where this gets interesting. The family add-ons are the one part of the elective residence rules where consulates exercise visible discretion, and practice has not been perfectly uniform across posts. Some consulates apply the percentages mechanically; others assess the household's overall means. The safe approach is to clear the stated percentages with margin and then confirm the exact expectation with the consulate that holds jurisdiction over your legal residence — the same office that will decide your file. Family members need their own documented relationship evidence too: notarized kinship certificates, such as marriage and birth certificates, legalized by the competent authorities.

From national visa to permesso di soggiorno: the questura stage

The consulate grants a national (type D) visa. That visa is your entry ticket, not your residence. After you arrive in Italy, you apply for the residence permit — the permesso di soggiorno per residenza elettiva — at the questura, the police headquarters of the province where you have established your home.

This two-stage structure is worth internalizing because it shapes the whole timeline. The consulate assesses your income, your housing, and your documents. The questura stage confirms you: the person who arrived matches the file, the address is real, the conditions still hold. The permit is then renewed periodically as long as the income, housing, and no-work conditions continue to be met. Keep your documentation current across the years — the renewal is where consulates' and questure's expectations converge, and a file that has drifted (an expired lease, income that quietly became salary) is a renewal problem.

One detail from the official guidance that hints at how the stages interlock: if the formal issue of the residence permit precedes the final deed on a property you are buying, you must still demonstrate full availability of an accommodation to be used as your residence for the entire duration of the visa. The state does not care about your conveyancing timetable; it cares that you are housed, continuously, from day one.

Who the elective residence visa is really for — and who should look elsewhere

The elective residence visa is for a specific person: someone with genuinely passive, ample, documented income — a pensioner with a strong occupational pension, a landlord with a serious rental portfolio, a dividend investor — who wants to live in Italy, has no intention of working, and is willing to commit to a home before the visa exists. If that is you, the route is straightforward and the rules, while demanding, are at least legible.

It is not for the almost-retired consultant who plans to keep a few clients, the remote worker who likes the idea of a Tuscan base, or the property buyer who assumes a house purchase confers residence — it does not. Each of those people has a different Italian route: the digital-nomad visa, the self-employment visa, or simply the Schengen 90/180-day rhythm for a holiday home. The elective residence visa rewards honesty about which of these you are. Choose the wrong route and the housing-first design turns from a formality into an expensive sunk cost.

And one last piece of honesty about money: €31,000 a year is the visa's bar, not a comfortable Italian budget in the cities most applicants dream about. Milan and Rome will test it; smaller cities and the south will stretch it. The visa proves you can support yourself. Where in Italy you choose to do that determines whether the number feels generous or tight.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much passive income do I need for Italy's elective residence visa in 2026?

The official consulate checklists set the minimum at approximately €31,000 per year for the main applicant — defined as three times the annual amount in Table A of the Interior Ministry directive of 1 March 2000. The income must be stable, regular, expected to continue, and derived from sources other than employment.

Does buying a house in Italy give me residency?

No. Buying property in Italy confers no automatic right of residence or long-stay visa. But the elective residence visa does require you to show a home — owned or rented — before you apply. A signed, registered long-term lease is sufficient; you do not have to buy.

Can I work in Italy on the elective residence visa?

No — the prohibition is absolute. The official checklist requires a declaration that you possess sufficient resources to live in Italy without engaging in any work activity, employed or self-employed. Remote work for foreign clients is also out. If you need to work, Italy's digital-nomad or self-employment routes are the alternatives.

Do I need to own property, or is renting enough for the visa?

Renting is enough. You must document the availability of owned or rented accommodation to be used as your residence; for a rental, the contract must already be signed and filed (registered) with the Revenue Agency. Some consulates also accept a preliminary purchase contract drawn up by an Italian notary with a down payment, but purchase is never mandatory.

How much income is needed if my spouse or children come with me?

The official embassy guidance increases the income requirement by at least 20% for a spouse and at least 5% for each child. Consulates have some discretion in how they apply the family amounts, so confirm the exact figure with the consulate that has jurisdiction over you.

What happens after I arrive in Italy on the elective residence visa?

The national visa gets you into Italy; the residence permit (permesso di soggiorno) is what lets you stay. After arrival you apply for the permit at the questura (police headquarters) of the province where you live. The permit is renewed periodically as long as the income, housing, and no-work conditions continue to hold.

Can I travel around Europe with the Italian elective residence permit?

Yes — a valid Italian residence permit lets you travel to other Schengen states for short stays under the 90-days-in-any-180 rule, like any EU residence permit. It does not give you the right to live or work in another EU country.

Bottom Line

Italy's elective residence visa is the continent's most upfront retirement deal: roughly €31,000 a year in genuinely passive income and a registered Italian home, both documented before you apply, in exchange for residence with an absolute ban on work. The two decisions that matter are proving your income would survive you stopping work entirely, and securing housing through the registered-lease route rather than assuming a purchase confers rights it does not.
