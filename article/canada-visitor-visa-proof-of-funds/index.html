Canada Visitor Visa Proof of Funds: How Much You Need in 2026

Canada publishes no fixed bank balance for visitor visas. How IRCC officers judge "enough money for your stay", the documents that prove it, and how to work out your number.

CountryPilot Editorial TeamUpdated October 12, 20266 min read

Search for the bank balance a Canada visitor visa requires and you will find confident figures everywhere except on IRCC's website — because there isn't one. Canada publishes no fixed minimum for visitors. This guide explains the test officers actually apply, how to work out the number your trip needs, and the evidence that makes it believable.

Quick Answer

IRCC sets no fixed minimum balance for a visitor visa. The official test: you "have enough money for your stay" — an amount that "depends on how long you will stay and if you will stay in a hotel, or with friends or relatives." The fee starts at CAN $100 per person, plus $85 for biometrics if fingerprints are required. Officers approve files where the money covers the actual itinerary and comes from a stable, explainable financial life — not files with the biggest lump sum.

The rule IRCC actually applies

IRCC's eligibility page for visitor visas lists the basic requirements plainly: a valid passport, good health, no criminal or immigration-related convictions, ties that will take you back home, an intention to leave Canada at the end of your visit — and enough money for your stay. No dollar figure is attached, and that is deliberate: a four-day business trip staying with a sibling and a six-week hotel tour are different financial propositions, and the officer judges your plan against your means.

Two consequences follow. The question is never "is this balance big?" but "does this money cover this itinerary, with a sensible margin?" And funds are judged together with ties and purpose — a strong balance attached to a vague plan still fails, which is why our Canada visa refusal guide treats money as one leg of a three-legged test.

Work out your number in four lines

Build the figure the way an officer reads it — from the itinerary down, not from a rumoured threshold:

  • Travel: return airfare for every applicant, at real prices for your route and dates.
  • Accommodation: nightly rate × nights. Zero if a host provides a home — but then their invitation letter must say so.
  • Daily costs: food, local transport and activities, per person per day.
  • Margin: a buffer for price movements. A file balanced to the last dollar reads as fragile.

Our proof-of-funds calculator runs exactly this arithmetic. Treat the output as your target — a planning figure derived from your itinerary, which is precisely how IRCC frames the test. (Processing, for planning: IRCC states times vary by country and publishes live figures — check yours before booking anything non-refundable.)

Illustrative example only — a planning method, not an IRCC threshold: a 10-day trip for one person staying with family in Toronto budgets the return flight, 10 days of food and transit, and a buffer; the same 10 days in hotels needs several thousand dollars more. Same visa, same officer — different "enough".

The documents that prove it

Money is proven on paper. IRCC's document checklists centre on bank statements — visa offices commonly ask for the most recent three months, showing your name, regular transactions and the running balance. Around them, the strongest files add:

  • Employment evidence — a letter from your employer confirming your role, salary and approved leave, with recent pay records.
  • Tax records — recent income-tax returns or assessments that corroborate the income your statements show.
  • Other assets — investment or deposit statements as supporting (not substitute) evidence.
  • Host documents — if you are staying with, or being supported by, someone in Canada: their invitation letter plus evidence of their status and means.

The statements do the heavy lifting because they show a pattern: salary arriving, ordinary spending leaving, a balance that behaves like a real financial life. A summary showing a large closing balance, with no history behind it, proves almost nothing.

Money that hurts your application

The fastest way to turn adequate funds into a refusal is to make them look manufactured. Borrowed balances — a large deposit shortly before applying, returned to the lender afterwards — are the classic case: officers read recent lump sums against your income history, and a deposit your salary cannot explain invites the conclusion the money is not genuinely yours. The same logic catches inconsistent files, where stated income, tax record and statement credits tell three different stories, and itinerary mismatches, where the funds would not cover the trip described. If a large credit is legitimate — a property sale, a matured investment, a bonus — document its source; explained money is safe money.

Do not confuse this with study-permit funds

One source of the "fixed amount" myth: Canada does publish set funds figures for some other routes — study permits use a published cost-of-living table, and work and immigration programs have their own thresholds. Those tables belong to those programs; they are not the visitor test, and quoting them does not strengthen a visitor application. If you are actually planning to study, start from our Canada country guide, where the student routes are covered on their own terms.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing a rumoured threshold. Blog figures like a single magic balance are not IRCC policy — an officer who sees a balance that exactly matches an internet rumour and nothing else is not reassured.
  • Depositing a lump sum just before applying. Unexplained recent money is worse than a smaller, older, explainable balance.
  • Submitting stale statements. Statements should run up to when you apply, not stop months earlier.
  • Claiming a hotel itinerary on a family-stay budget. The plan and the funds must describe the same trip.

Useful answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a minimum bank balance for a Canada visitor visa in 2026?

No. IRCC publishes no minimum. The official requirement is to "have enough money for your stay," with the amount depending on how long you stay and whether you stay in a hotel or with friends or relatives. Any site quoting one universal figure is describing its own rule of thumb, not Canadian policy.

How many months of bank statements does IRCC want?

It depends on the visa office checklist generated for your application — commonly the most recent three months. Follow your personal checklist exactly; where it specifies a period, it outranks any general guide.

Can my host in Canada cover my costs?

Yes — IRCC's own framing counts staying "with friends or relatives" as a different funds calculation. Support it with the host's invitation letter, evidence of their legal status in Canada, and evidence of their means. Your own finances still matter: the officer is also judging your ties and your ability to leave at the end of the visit.

Does a large balance guarantee approval?

No. Funds are one of several basic requirements. Officers must also be convinced you will leave Canada at the end of your visit and that ties — a job, home, financial assets or family — will take you back. Money supports that case; it cannot replace it.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

For a Canada visitor visa there is no official number to hit — there is a trip to fund, visibly and credibly. Cost your actual itinerary, hold a balance covering it with margin, prove the money with several months of statements matching your income and tax records, and document any host support properly. That is what "enough money for your stay" means in practice.
