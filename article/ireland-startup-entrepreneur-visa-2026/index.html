Ireland Startup Entrepreneur Programme (STEP) 2026

Ireland's founder route is the strictest of the four in this series — not because the money is high, but because the ambition bar is. €50,000 is deliberately modest; what Ireland actually demands is a venture that could create ten Irish jobs and a million euros of sales within a few years. If your startup cannot credibly make that promise, no paperwork rescues it. If it can, the state gives you two years and watches quarterly.

Ireland's Start-up Entrepreneur Programme (STEP, run by Immigration Service Delivery since 2012) offers non-EEA founders a 2-year residence permission — renewable for a further 3 years — for an innovative business backed by at least €50,000 in funding. An independent Evaluation Committee assesses proposals quarterly; the business must qualify as a high-potential start-up (innovative, export-oriented, capable of 10 Irish jobs and €1 million in sales within 3–4 years). The application fee is a non-refundable €350. Retail, catering and personal services are excluded, and Russian and Belarusian citizens are currently not accepted.

The €50,000 is the cheapest part

Founders fixate on the headline number: the required €50,000 in funding, stated on the official STEP page alongside good character and an innovative proposal as the core eligibility trio. But the money is the least interesting part of STEP's design, because Ireland deliberately made it achievable — €50,000 is startup seed money, not a wealth test. The official STEP guidelines set €30,000 for each subsequent founder on the same proposal, which confirms the figure is about project capital, not personal fortune.

What makes the funding requirement harder than it looks is the evidence chain. The Immigration Service Delivery guidance requires three things about the money: the amount, its provenance, and your ability to transfer it to Ireland. The acceptable sources are your own resources, a business loan, business angel or venture capital funding, or a grant from an Irish State agency. For own-resources funding, that means a letter from a financial institution regulated by the Central Bank of Ireland showing the funds in your name, or — if held elsewhere — personal bank statements from a bank regulated by your home regulator. Money that exists but cannot be documented through regulated institutions does not count, and money that cannot reach Ireland is equally useless.

One administrative note with real teeth: the application fee is €350, payable by electronic fund transfer with the reference "STEP – your passport number," and it is non-refundable. Applications are not processed until the full €350 is lodged. Incomplete applications are returned, and any lobbying or canvassing disqualifies the applicant and the project outright — a prohibition the official page states plainly enough to take seriously.

High-potential start-up: the 10-jobs, €1-million test

STEP exists to attract high-potential start-ups — HPSUs — and the official definition is where most applications live or die. The programme aims at ventures that introduce a new or innovative product or service to international markets, or are involved in manufacturing or internationally traded services; that are capable of creating 10 jobs in Ireland and realising €1 million in sales within three to four years of starting up; that are led by an experienced management team; that are headquartered and controlled in Ireland; and that are less than five years old.

Read that as a filter, not a forecast. "Capable of" is doing real work in the sentence — the state is not asking you to guarantee ten jobs in year one (the official guidance explicitly sets no initial job-creation targets, recognising that such businesses take time to get off the ground). It is asking whether the business model could plausibly get there. A consultancy billing your own hours cannot. A product company with export markets can. The distinction is structural, not rhetorical, and it is why the business plan — which must follow the official sample template — is scrutinised rather than skimmed.

The "headquartered and controlled in Ireland" limb has a practical consequence founders miss: the company must genuinely live in Ireland — its decision-making, its records, its substance. A brass-plate entity whose real operations stay elsewhere fails the definition even if the paperwork is perfect. And "less than five years old" means established startups relocating to Ireland are welcome, but only while they are still young enough to be startups.

The Evaluation Committee: quarterly, independent, and final

Applications may be submitted electronically at any time — but they are not decided continuously. Proposals are considered on a quarterly basis by an independent Evaluation Committee of senior public and civil servants, which assesses the suitability of each proposal and can request further information from the candidate. The committee recommends to the Minister for Justice, and the Minister's decision is final: a refusal is not subject to review or appeal.

The quarterly rhythm shapes strategy. A complete, well-documented application filed just after a quarterly sitting waits; one filed just before it moves. There is no published calendar to game, but the structure means timing matters in a way it doesn't for continuously-decided routes like Spain's 20-day UGE process. More importantly, the no-appeal rule raises the stakes of the first submission. You get one clean shot per proposal — incomplete applications are returned, and refused applications cannot be argued. Front-load the quality: the business plan, the funding evidence, the character documentation, all of it, in the first filing.

The character requirement runs deeper than a single certificate. Eligibility requires good character and no criminal convictions in any jurisdiction, and the supporting documentation includes police character statements from each country lived in for more than six months in the last ten years. Start assembling these early — they are the slowest documents in the file for founders who have lived in several countries.

The 2-year permission and its strict conditions

Successful applicants — and their immediate families — are granted residence permission for 2 years initially. "Immediate family" means your spouse, civil partner or partner and dependent children under 18, with children aged 18 to 24 considered in certain cases. Multi-entry visas are issued for the same duration, which matters practically: founders travel, and the permission assumes they will.

The conditions attached to the permission are stricter than most founder routes, and each one is worth reading literally. You must establish the business as outlined in your application. You must work on the business full time. You are not permitted to be employed in any other capacity — no side job, no part-time employment to pay the bills. You must not become a financial burden on the Irish State. And you must not be convicted of a criminal offence in any jurisdiction. Breach any of these and the permission may be withdrawn.

The "no other employment" condition is the one that surprises founders most. In practice it means the startup — plus your €50,000 and whatever it generates — must carry you for two years. Ireland is buying your full-time attention, not your evenings and weekends. Budget the two years accordingly, because the state will not subsidise the experiment: the "not a financial burden" condition and the ban on other employment together define a founder who is fully committed and fully self-funded.

Renewal for three more years — then the long road

After the initial two years, the permission can be renewed for a further 3 years — and this is where the Evaluation Committee re-enters the story. Renewal requires the startup to be operating and to have secured an assessment by the Evaluation Committee stating its success and viability. In other words, the quarterly judges who admitted you now grade your homework. The business must have become real: trading, hiring or on a credible path to it, matching the proposal that won admission.

After those first five years, the entrepreneur can apply for long-term residence. From there, the path to citizenship runs under the ordinary Irish Nationality and Citizenship Acts — STEP confers no shortcut, and naturalisation applications currently require eight years of reckonable residence. Founders should understand this as a five-to-eight-year commitment to Ireland, not a two-year experiment with an exit. The programme is designed to keep the companies it attracts; the renewal assessment is the mechanism.

The sectors STEP will not touch

The official guidance names the exclusions explicitly: retail, catering, personal services, and similar domestically-oriented businesses are not usually considered suitable. This is not snobbery — it is the HPSU definition applied consistently. A café cannot create ten export jobs or a million euros of international sales; neither can a barbershop. STEP is an industrial-policy instrument dressed as an immigration route, and domestically-oriented services belong to other permits.

One current political exclusion deserves mention because it is stated as an instruction on the official page: the Minister for Justice has directed that no further applications from Russian or Belarusian citizens be accepted into STEP. It is a reminder that the programme is discretionary state policy, not a rights-based visa — the door can narrow for reasons that have nothing to do with your business plan.

Note: if your project is genuinely innovative but domestically focused — a service business serving the Irish market, say — the honest alternative is Ireland's ordinary employment-permit system or the general business-permission routes, not STEP. Forcing a domestic business into STEP's export-shaped criteria wastes the €350 fee and, more expensively, the quarters.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much funding do I need for Ireland's STEP in 2026?

€50,000 for the first founder (the official STEP guidelines set €30,000 for each subsequent founder on the same proposal). The funds can come from your own resources, a business loan, business angel or venture capital funding, or a grant from an Irish State agency — but you must evidence the amount, its source, and your ability to transfer it to Ireland. The application itself costs a non-refundable €350.

What counts as a high-potential start-up under STEP?

A venture introducing a new or innovative product or service to international markets, involved in manufacturing or internationally traded services, capable of creating 10 jobs in Ireland and realising €1 million in sales within three to four years, led by an experienced management team, headquartered and controlled in Ireland, and less than 5 years old. Retail, catering, personal services and similar businesses are excluded.

How long is the residence permission under STEP?

Successful applicants and their immediate families are granted residence permission for 2 years initially. It can then be renewed for a further 3 years, and after the first 5 years you can apply for long-term residence.

Who decides my STEP application, and when?

An independent Evaluation Committee of senior public and civil servants assesses proposals on a quarterly basis and recommends to the Minister for Justice, whose decision is final with no review or appeal. Applications may be submitted electronically at any time.

Can my family come with me under STEP?

Yes — your spouse, civil partner or partner and dependent children under 18 (in some cases children 18–24) receive residence permission alongside you, and multi-entry visas are issued for the same duration.

What conditions attach to the STEP permission?

You must establish the business as outlined in your application, work on it full time, not be employed in any other capacity, not become a financial burden on the Irish State, and not be convicted of a criminal offence. Breach can lead to withdrawal of the permission.

Does STEP lead to Irish citizenship?

Not directly. After five years of permission you can apply for long-term residence; citizenship runs under the ordinary Irish Nationality and Citizenship Acts (currently requiring 8 years of reckonable residence for naturalisation applications). STEP builds the residence history, not a shortcut.

Bottom Line

Ireland's STEP is the founder visa for people who think in jobs and exports: €50,000 of documented, transferable funding buys you into a quarterly judgment by senior civil servants, and the prize is two years of full-time, no-side-job permission to build a company that Ireland believes can employ ten people and sell a million euros. It is the hardest ambition test in this series and the most honest one — pass the HPSU definition on paper, survive the renewal assessment in reality, and five years later you are on the road to long-term residence.
