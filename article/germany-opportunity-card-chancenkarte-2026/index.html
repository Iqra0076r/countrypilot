Germany Opportunity Card 2026: Points Guide

The Chancenkarte is Germany's answer to a specific problem: skilled people who could get hired in Munich or Berlin but cannot get hired from Karachi, Lagos, or Lahore. It lets you move to Germany for up to a year to find a job, with no offer in hand, on the strength of a points score. Six points is the pass mark — but the points are only half the story. The other half is a blocked bank account, a formal qualification you must already hold, and a one-year clock that starts the day you land.

The Chancenkarte (§20a of the Residence Act) gives non-EU nationals up to one year in Germany to search for qualified work. You need a formally recognized qualification, German A1 or English B2 as a baseline, and either a fully recognized qualification (no points needed) or at least 6 points in the points system. Financing: a blocked account of at least €1,091 net per month in 2026, or a declaration of commitment. Holders may work up to 20 hours a week while searching.

The Chancenkarte is a bridge, not a prize

Introduced on June 1, 2024, as the centerpiece of Germany's reformed Skilled Immigration Act, the Opportunity Card replaced the old job-seeker visa for non-graduates and widened the door considerably. But keep the frame right: this card does not give you a job, permanent residence, or even a long stay. It gives you presence — up to 12 months of legal residence in Germany to do what is nearly impossible from abroad: interview in person, attend trial employment, and convert conversations into contracts.

The card's economics are the point. German employers hire foreign candidates reluctantly when the candidate is eight time zones away; the Chancenkarte moves you into the local candidate pool. If you land qualified employment, you switch to a skilled-worker residence permit or an EU Blue Card from inside Germany. If you do not, the card expires. Treat it as a one-year option on a German career, priced at a year of living costs — and only buy the option if you can afford the year.

Two doors: recognition or six points

Advertisement

Section 20a of the Residence Act offers two routes, and which one you take changes everything about the application:

  • Door 1 — recognized skilled worker. If your foreign vocational or academic qualification is fully recognized in Germany (or you earned the qualification in Germany), you are treated as a skilled worker and skip the points system entirely. No points math, no score anxiety — and no language certificate required by law, though German is strongly recommended for the job hunt.
  • Door 2 — the points system. If your qualification is not fully recognized, you need at least 6 points across the categories below, plus the baseline requirements: a completed qualification and language proof.

The strategic insight: if recognition is within reach — a degree from a listed university, a vocational certificate that maps cleanly — pursuing full recognition can be simpler than gaming the points table. Check anabin or get a ZAB Statement of Comparability before deciding which door to knock on.

The points table, line by line (2026)

Here is the official scoring, per the federal government's Make it in Germany portal. Every claim needs evidence or certificates at the visa interview — points you cannot document do not exist.

  • Partial recognition of your qualification — 4 points. If you have started the recognition process and the competent body found your foreign qualification partially equivalent (Teilanerkennung), that alone gets you two-thirds of the way. For regulated professions where you must complete compensatory measures before getting a licence to practise, the four points still apply.
  • Qualification in a shortage occupation — 1 point. If your formal qualification sits in one of Germany's listed shortage occupations (IT, engineering, healthcare, teaching, and others on the official list), add one.
  • Professional experience — 2 or 3 points. Two points for at least two years of relevant experience (tied to your qualification, gained after graduation) within the last five years; three points for at least five years within the last seven. Within this category you count only the higher tier — they do not stack.
  • Language skills — 1 to 4 points. German above the A1 baseline: A2 = 1 point, B1 = 2, B2 or higher = 3. Plus one bonus point for English at C1 or native level. Note the asymmetry that rewards German effort: English B2 is the entry requirement and earns zero points on its own; you need C1 English or German to score.
  • Age — 2 or 1 point. Two points if you are no older than 35 on the day of application; one point from 35 to 40; nothing above 40. There is no hard age cutoff — older applicants simply score zero here and make it up elsewhere.
  • Previous stays in Germany — 1 point. At least six months of continuous legal residence in Germany within the last five years — study, language, or work stays count; tourism does not. Old passports with visas and entry stamps are the evidence.
  • Spouse or partner also qualifies — 1 point. If your married or partnered spouse independently meets the Opportunity Card requirements, you get one point — and they get their own card.

Note: Work a typical case: a 32-year-old software engineer (2 age points) with a partially recognized degree (4 points) and B1 German (2 points) already has 8 — before experience, English, or shortage-occupation bonuses. The 6-point bar is designed to be reachable for exactly this profile.

The qualification myth: self-taught alone doesn't qualify

This is the most expensive misunderstanding about the Chancenkarte, and it deserves a section of its own. The points route still requires a completed vocational or academic qualification as a baseline — one that is recognized in the country where you obtained it, with vocational training lasting at least two years. You evidence it through anabin, a ZAB Statement of Comparability (university degrees), or the Digital Statement on Professional Qualification (vocational). A brilliant self-taught developer with no formal qualification cannot enter through the Chancenkarte at all.

The route that does exist for degree-less IT professionals is the EU Blue Card for IT specialists: a specific job offer, at least three years of relevant IT experience in the past seven years at university level, and a salary of at least €45,934.20 in 2026. That route requires the job offer up front; the Chancenkarte requires the qualification up front. Know which prerequisite you actually hold before you pick a lane.

€1,091 a month: pricing your job-search year

However you score, you must finance the stay. During the visa process you demonstrate this with a blocked account (Sperrkonto) holding at least €1,091 net per month — the amount applicable in 2026 — for every month you plan to spend in Germany, or with a declaration of commitment (Verpflichtungserklärung) from a sponsor in Germany. For a full 12-month card that means roughly €13,092 parked in the blocked account, released to you monthly after arrival.

Two honest observations. First, €1,091 a month is a survival budget in Munich or Frankfurt, not a comfort budget — plan your actual runway above the legal minimum. Second, if you cannot demonstrate financing for the full 12 months, the card can be issued for a shorter period initially and extended once your finances improve. Do not borrow the blocked-account money from a lender and return it after the visa; consulates have seen that move, and the account statements tell the story.

20 hours a week and the two-week trial

While searching, you may hold one or more part-time jobs totalling 20 hours a week — secondary employment to cover living costs. More strategically, you may do trial employment of up to two weeks per employer, provided the trial aims at qualified employment, an apprenticeship, or a qualification programme. This is the Chancenkarte's secret weapon: German hiring culture loves the Probearbeit, and two weeks inside a company converts to offers at a rate cold applications never match.

The language baseline also lives in this section because it shapes your year: German A1 or English B2 is the legal minimum to apply. In practice, B1 German transforms the job search — most qualified roles outside international tech teams operate in German, and the points table pays you up to three points for getting there. Start the language course in your home country; the card's 12-month clock is too precious to spend in a classroom.

What the card turns into

The Opportunity Card is issued initially for a maximum of one year, and it is not renewable as a job-search card. Its value is entirely in the conversion: find qualified employment and you switch — from inside Germany, without leaving — to a residence permit for skilled workers or an EU Blue Card, both of which put you on the road to the settlement permit.

Plan the conversion from day one. The jobs that convert are qualified roles matching your recognized skill set; the 20-hour part-time work is for rent, not for the resume. Keep a running file of every application, interview, and trial — if you need an extension of any kind or a status change, the foreigners authority will ask what the year produced. And if the year ends without an offer, you leave; the Chancenkarte is a bridge you cross or you don't. The good news is that one year inside the German labour market, with in-person interviews and trial weeks, is worth more than three years of applying from abroad.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many points do I need for the German Opportunity Card?

At least 6 points across qualification recognition, shortage occupation, experience, language, age, prior stays, and spouse qualification — unless your foreign qualification is already fully recognized in Germany, in which case you skip the points system entirely.

Can I get the Chancenkarte without a degree?

No. The points route requires a completed vocational or academic qualification recognized in the country where you obtained it — vocational training must have lasted at least two years. Degree-less IT professionals have a separate route: the EU Blue Card for IT specialists with three years of relevant experience in the last seven.

How long is the Opportunity Card valid?

Up to one year as a job-search card. It is not a settlement route on its own — you convert it into a skilled-worker permit or EU Blue Card once you find qualifying employment.

Can I work while on the Chancenkarte?

Yes: part-time work of up to 20 hours a week while you search, plus trial employment of up to two weeks per employer to test a qualified job.

How much money do I need in a blocked account for the Chancenkarte in 2026?

At least €1,091 net per month for the duration of your planned stay — about €13,092 for a full 12 months. Alternatively, a declaration of commitment from a sponsor in Germany can cover the financing requirement.

Do I need German to apply for the Opportunity Card?

You need either German at A1 level or English at B2 level as a baseline. German above A1 earns points (A2 = 1, B1 = 2, B2+ = 3), and English at C1 or native adds one bonus point.

Bottom Line

The Chancenkarte is a one-year, self-financed bridge into the German labour market: six points (or a fully recognized qualification), a formal qualification you already hold, €1,091 a month in a blocked account, and 20 hours a week of part-time work while you hunt. It does not forgive a missing qualification and it does not extend itself — but for a skilled engineer who needs to interview in person, it is the most honest route Germany offers. Score yourself on the official table before you start saving.
