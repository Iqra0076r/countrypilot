France French Tech Visa 2026: Founder's 4-Year Route

France will give a foreign founder up to four years of renewable residency, family included, with no fixed investment and no diploma requirement — if the French state agrees the startup is genuinely innovative. That single judgment, made by the Ministry of the Economy, is the entire visa. This guide explains the 2026 rules under their real legal name, the €22,404.20 means test, the two-stage consulate-to-prefecture journey, and where applications actually fail.

"French Tech Visa" is the label for a fast-track procedure inside France's Talent residence framework. For founders, the legal category is Talent – Innovative Economic Project: no fixed investment, personal means of €22,404.20 (one year's gross Smic) in 2026, a Ministry of Economy innovation opinion, and a residence card valid up to 4 years, renewable, with family included.

First, the name on the card isn't "French Tech Visa"

This is the single most useful thing to know before you file anything: "French Tech Visa" appears on no official document. It is the French Tech Mission's marketing label for a simplified procedure that channels three kinds of tech talent — startup founders, employees of innovative companies, and investors — into France's Talent residence framework. Since the 2024 reform, even the old "Passeport Talent" name is gone from the permits themselves; the card says Talent.

For founders, the exact legal category is "Talent – Porteur de projet: projet économique innovant" — Talent, project bearer: innovative economic project. Business France, the French government's own agency, maintains the official English-language page for the "French Tech Visa for Founders" procedure (verified 8 June 2026), and it uses precisely this terminology: the procedure grants the status of "Talent – Innovative Economic Project" to non-EU/EEA/Swiss founders whose project is recognized as innovative by the French authorities, with a residence permit issued for up to four years, renewable, and the founder's family included.

Why does the naming matter? Because consulates, prefectures, and the Ministry of the Economy all process your file under the legal category, not the brand. Every form you fill, every appointment you book, and every question you ask a fonctionnaire should use "Talent – projet économique innovant." Using the marketing name with officials marks you, fairly or not, as someone working from blog posts rather than the rules.

The innovation gate: what the Ministry of Economy actually judges

Strip away everything else and the French Tech founder route is a single assessment: does the French state consider your project innovative? There is no points grid, no investment threshold to clear instead, and no diploma to wave. The Ministry of the Economy — in practice through DRIEETS, its regional economic authority — issues an opinion on the project's innovative character, and that opinion is the load-bearing wall of the application.

In practice, the assessment usually travels with backing from a French Tech-listed incubator or accelerator. France maintains a recognized ecosystem of incubators, and acceptance into one functions as the market's pre-verdict on your innovation claim: an incubator that has staked its own reputation on you is powerful supporting evidence for the ministry's reviewer. The dossier typically includes support letters from people inside the French Tech ecosystem — founders are consistently advised these must come from verifiable ecosystem participants, not from generic well-wishers, and the letters need to answer the same two questions the ministry asks: what is genuinely new here, and is there real demand for it in France?

Understand what is being judged and, just as importantly, what is not. The test is novelty plus demand in the French market — not deep-tech credentials, not total addressable market slides, not traction metrics. A brilliantly engineered product nobody in France wants fails; a simple product that fills a real, evidenced gap in the French market can pass. Founders who write their dossier as a pitch deck — TAM, hockey-stick graphs, adjectives — misunderstand the audience. The reviewer is an economist asking "is this new here, and does anyone here need it," and the winning dossiers answer those two questions in French, with evidence, before anything else.

The money test: €22,404.20, and why it's not an investment

Here is the number that confuses everyone, because it looks like an investment threshold and is not one. The innovative-project founder route requires no fixed investment in the company at all — the sharpest contrast with the neighboring Talent branches (the business-creation branch references around €30,000 invested, and the direct-investment branch is built around €300,000). What the founder route requires instead is proof of personal means equal to one year's full-time gross minimum wage (Smic): €22,404.20 in 2026.

Read that carefully, because the distinction decides how you prepare. This is not money you put into the startup, lock in escrow, or spend on the business. It is evidence that you can support yourself for a year — bank statements, not a cap table. The figure moves with the Smic, so it is re-set periodically; the €22,404.20 figure is the 2026 value confirmed across independent 2026 analyses of the route, and you should verify the current Smic-derived amount on service-public.fr before filing, since a mid-year Smic revaluation would move your target.

The practical consequence is that this is one of the cheapest founder visas in Europe by capital requirement — cheaper than the Netherlands' self-employment income test, cheaper than Denmark's proof-of-funds, and dramatically cheaper than any golden-visa-adjacent route. The price France charges instead is the innovation judgment. Cheap in euros, expensive in effort: that is the honest summary of the bargain.

The two-stage journey: consulate, then prefecture

No version of this route skips a step, and understanding the two stages prevents the most common timeline shock. Stage one happens in your country of residence: you file for a long-stay visa marked for the Talent procedure at the French consulate, and this is where the dossier — including the innovation assessment — is built and reviewed. The consulate stage is also where you pay the €99 long-stay visa fee. Budget realistically here: consulate responsiveness varies enormously, and the innovation opinion does not arrive on a fixed clock.

Stage two happens after you land in France: you validate the long-stay visa and collect the actual residence card through OFII (the immigration and integration office) and your local prefecture. The card issuance costs €350 in 2026 — though note that French residence-permit fees were revised during 2026, so confirm the current amount at your prefecture rather than treating any single figure as permanent. End to end, founders consistently report 3 to 6 months from first filing to card in hand, with the consulate stage accounting for most of the variance.

Two admin details that matter more than they look. First, the dossier is substantially a French-language exercise: the project description and support letters that the ministry's reviewer actually reads need to be in French, and founders who submit English-only innovation arguments are relying on a reviewer's goodwill. Second, keep every receipt, validation certificate, and prefecture appointment confirmation — French administration runs on paper trails, and the renewal in four years will ask what happened in year one.

What the 4-year card really gives your family

The Talent card's family provisions are among the strongest attached to any European founder visa, and they are worth spelling out because they affect the whole household's planning. Your spouse and dependent children are included in the procedure, and the spouse's card — issued under the famille accompagnante (accompanying family) status — carries immediate authorization to work in France on arrival. That is rare in continental Europe, where spousal work rights often arrive late, conditionally, or not at all, and it materially changes the household economics: a two-earner relocation is a different proposition from a one-earner one.

The card itself is valid for up to four years and renewable — the duration can reflect the project's horizon, but the headline is four. Four years is long enough to build something real: to incorporate, hire, find product-market fit, and still have runway left when renewal comes around. Compare that with the one-year startup permits some EU countries issue (the Netherlands being the classic example), where the founder spends half the permit preparing the next permit, and the French duration starts to look like a genuine strategic advantage rather than a footnote.

Renewal, when it comes, re-tests the substance: is the innovative project still alive, still yours, and still being pursued in France? The founders who renew smoothly are the ones who kept the paper trail described above — evidence of activity, not just presence. And after five years of continuous residence, the standard French path toward long-term options opens, subject to the usual integration requirements including French language — a bridge to cross later, but worth knowing exists from day one.

French Tech founder vs. the other Talent doors

Founders sometimes apply through the wrong Talent branch, so a quick map of the neighboring doors is worth having. The business-creation branch (Talent – création d'entreprise) is for founders whose project is a solid business rather than an innovative one: it references around €30,000 of investment, expects a viable business plan, and historically leaned on diploma or experience thresholds. If your venture is a restaurant, an agency, or an import business — real businesses, but not innovative projects — this is your door, not the French Tech one, and applying through the innovation branch with a non-innovative plan is the fastest route to a refusal.

The employee branches matter if you are joining rather than founding. The French Tech procedure also covers employees recruited by innovative companies, where the bar is a €39,582 gross annual salary with R&D-linked functions (per the official service-public.fr Talent card rules). The separate qualified-employee branch sits higher still. These are worth knowing because some founders hedge: if the innovation opinion looks shaky, a job offer from a French Tech company at the salary threshold is a parallel path to the same 4-year Talent card.

And the investor branch — built around roughly €300,000 of direct investment — is a different universe: capital-first, innovation-optional. If you have that kind of money to deploy, you do not need the founder route's innovation judgment at all. Each branch optimizes for a different founder; the French Tech founder route optimizes for innovation with minimal capital, which is exactly why its assessment is the strictest on novelty.

Where founder applications actually fail

1. The innovation case is asserted, not evidenced. "We use AI" is not an innovation argument. The ministry wants to know what is new in the French market and who here needs it. Founders who cannot name the French incumbents they differ from have not done the homework the reviewer expects.

2. Support letters from the wrong people. Letters must come from verifiable participants in the French Tech ecosystem, written in French, answering the ministry's two questions — novelty and demand. Letters from friends, foreign mentors, or paid intermediaries outside the ecosystem carry no weight and can actively undermine credibility.

3. Confusing the means test with an investment. Founders who wire €22,404.20 into a company account and call it the investment have misunderstood the requirement — it is personal means, evidenced in your own finances. Conversely, founders who show the means but cannot explain how the business will be funded raise the viability question the ministry will ask next.

4. Filing at the wrong consulate stage. The long-stay visa must be filed in the country of residence with the complete dossier, including the innovation elements. Incomplete filings do not queue politely — they stall while the consulate asks for the missing pieces, and the 3-to-6-month timeline becomes 9.

5. Treating arrival as the finish line. OFII validation, prefecture appointments, and the paper trail for renewal all happen after landing. Founders who celebrate at the airport and ignore the prefecture paperwork discover at renewal that year one left no administrative trace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the French Tech Visa a separate visa category?

No. "French Tech Visa" is the marketing label for a simplified procedure within France's Talent residence framework. The legal category for founders is "Talent – Porteur de projet: projet économique innovant" (Talent – Innovative Economic Project), and every official document you file will use that name — not the marketing label.

How much money do I need for the French Tech Visa in 2026?

The innovative-project founder route sets no fixed investment minimum. You must show personal means equal to one year's full-time gross minimum wage — €22,404.20 in 2026. This is money evidencing you can support yourself, not capital invested in the company. (The separate business-creation branch references around €30,000 of investment instead.)

How long is the French Tech Visa valid?

The Talent residence card is issued for up to 4 years and is renewable. Your spouse and children are included in the procedure, and your spouse receives immediate work authorization on arrival under the accompanying-family status.

What does the French Tech Visa cost?

Budget €99 for the long-stay visa at the consulate plus €350 for the residence card issuance in France (2026 figures — French permit fees were revised during 2026, so confirm the current amounts at your consulate and prefecture). Any incubator or advisory fees you choose to pay are separate.

Do I need a diploma or French language skills for the French Tech Visa?

The founder route has no diploma requirement — a founder without a Master's degree is not shut out the way they might be on other routes. French is not a formal requirement for the card itself, though the innovation dossier is a French-language exercise and running a company in France without French is genuinely difficult.

How long does the French Tech Visa application take?

Expect roughly 3 to 6 months end to end: the consulate filing and innovation assessment in your country of residence, then visa validation and card issuance through OFII and the prefecture after arrival. Timelines vary significantly by consulate — anyone promising papers in weeks is not describing this route.

Who decides whether my startup is innovative enough?

The French Ministry of the Economy assesses the project's innovative character — in practice through DRIEETS, the regional economic authority — typically supported by backing from a French Tech-listed incubator or accelerator. The test is whether the project brings something genuinely new to the French market with real demand, not deep-tech credentials or market-size slides.

Bottom Line

The French Tech founder route is Europe's best deal for capital-light founders with a genuinely new idea: no fixed investment, no diploma requirement, a €22,404.20 means test, and a 4-year renewable card with immediate spousal work rights. Its price is the innovation judgment — a French-language dossier that must convince the Ministry of the Economy your project is new here and needed here. Get the innovation case right, keep the prefecture paper trail from day one, and France gives you the longest runway of any EU startup visa.
