Portugal Golden Visa 2026: The €500K Fund Route, Explained

The Lisbon apartment route is long dead. Portugal's golden visa has run on regulated investment funds since October 2023 — €500,000 into a qualifying fund, five years of holding, seven days a year in-country. It's the best-known residency-by-investment programme in Europe and also the most misunderstood: backlogs, fund lock-ins, and a shifting citizenship timeline all sit between you and the card. Here's the complete, verified picture for October 2026.

Quick Answer

Portugal's golden visa (ARI) has been fund-only since October 2023, when Law 56/2023 removed real estate as a qualifying route. The flagship route in 2026: subscribe €500,000 to a qualifying investment or venture-capital fund that is regulated by Portugal's securities commission (CMVM), has a minimum five-year maturity, and keeps at least 60% of its capital in Portuguese companies. Hold the investment for at least five years; spend an average of seven days a year in Portugal; include your spouse, dependent children up to 26, and dependent parents on the same application. Government fees run about €6,800 per applicant (€632.10 analysis + €6,314.20 card issuance, 2026 figures), with a €3,157.80 renewal every two years. The honest bottleneck is processing: with AIMA working through a large backlog, expect 12 to 24 months from submission to residence card. Start every check at the official AIMA programme page — and treat any page still advertising a Portuguese property route as stale.

What changed in 2023 — and what survived

Portugal's golden visa was the programme that defined the category: launch in 2012, a wave of foreign capital into Lisbon and Porto property, then — like Spain — a political reckoning about housing. Law 56/2023, in force from October 2023, cut real estate out of the programme entirely. No Lisbon apartment, no Algarve villa, no renovation project qualifies anymore. Applications lodged before the change fall under transitional rules; everything since must use one of the surviving routes.

What survived is, frankly, a more interesting programme. The fund route redirected foreign capital into Portuguese companies — technology, renewable energy, hospitality, healthcare — instead of apartments. It also made the programme more investor-shaped than buyer-shaped: you're subscribing to a professionally managed vehicle, not hunting for a flat. That suits diversified investors and frustrates people who wanted a holiday home with a permit attached. If a holiday home with a permit is the goal, look at Greece instead — Greece's property route is still open.

The €500,000 fund route, in detail

The qualifying subscription is precise, and every word of the definition matters when you're choosing a fund. The €500,000 goes into an investment fund or venture-capital fund that:

  • is regulated by the CMVM — Portugal's securities regulator. This is your first due-diligence check; if the fund isn't on the CMVM register, it doesn't qualify.
  • has a minimum maturity of five years — most qualifying funds are structured with 6- to 10-year terms, some running to 12.
  • invests at least 60% of its capital in Portuguese-headquartered companies — the money must genuinely land in the Portuguese economy.

You hold the subscription for at least five years to satisfy the programme. In practice the fund's own term often runs longer, so your capital may be locked for six to ten years depending on the fund's life and exit mechanics. That lock-in is the single biggest difference from the old property route, where you could in theory sell sooner and just lose the permit. Here, the exit timetable is the fund manager's, not yours.

What kinds of funds qualify

The market that grew up after 2023 sorts into a few recognisable flavours. Hospitality funds invest in hotel and tourism developments — popular because the underlying asset is easy to understand. Technology and growth funds take equity in Portuguese startups and SMEs. Renewable-energy funds finance solar and related infrastructure. There are also diversified private-equity vehicles mixing sectors.

How to choose: read the fund's subscription documents like an investor, not a visa applicant, because you are both. Check the manager's track record, the fee structure (management and performance fees vary widely), the exit mechanics — some funds offer contractual buyback at a set year, others return capital only on the fund's liquidation — and whether returns are projected, guaranteed, or neither. "Guaranteed returns" on a venture vehicle deserves hard scrutiny; understand whose balance sheet stands behind the guarantee. Published 2026 guides report fund returns spanning low single digits to double digits depending on type — treat any specific number as the fund's marketing until the documents say otherwise.

The other routes nobody talks about

The fund route gets the headlines, but four more doors are open under the same ARI programme:

  • Cultural heritage contribution — €250,000 (€200,000 in low-density areas). A donation to preserving Portuguese cultural heritage or supporting artistic production. The capital doesn't come back, but the ticket is half the fund route's.
  • Scientific research contribution — €500,000 into Portugal's national scientific and technological system.
  • Business capitalisation — €500,000 into a Portuguese company combined with creating or maintaining five permanent jobs.
  • Job creation — no minimum capital. Ten permanent positions (fewer in low-density areas), for entrepreneurs building real teams.

There's also a capital-transfer route at €1.5 million. Realistically, almost everyone serious picks the fund or the cultural contribution. The job-creation and business routes are genuine, but they demand an operating presence in Portugal — a different project from a passive investment.

The process, step by step

End to end, the published sequence looks like this. Timings are working ranges from 2026 programme guides — yours will vary with document readiness and AIMA's queue.

1. Eligibility and setup (2–4 weeks). Obtain your Portuguese tax number (NIF) and open a Portuguese bank account — both usually handled through a legal representative, often remotely. Engage an immigration lawyer who does this programme routinely; the fund documents and the AIMA file are not DIY territory.

2. Document compilation (4–6 weeks). Passport, criminal-record certificates from countries of residence, proof of health insurance, proof of the qualifying investment once made, and family documents with apostilles and sworn translations where required.

3. Fund subscription (1–2 weeks). Choose the fund, review the subscription agreement, transfer the €500,000 to the fund's designated account, and receive the official subscription certificate — the document AIMA wants to see.

4. AIMA submission and biometrics (12–24 months). Your lawyer files the application through AIMA's ARI portal. AIMA pre-approves in principle, then schedules your biometric appointment in Portugal — budget a trip of a few days — then issues final approval and the residence card.

5. Renewals and onward status. Renew every two years while you hold the investment; permanent residence becomes available after about five years of legal residence.

The AIMA backlog: the real timeline

Here's the part marketing skips. Portugal's migration agency AIMA inherited a mountain of files when it replaced the old SEF service, and the golden-visa queue is part of that mountain. Published 2026 guidance puts the journey from submission to card at 12 to 18 months typically, up to 24 months for complex cases, with some families reporting faster and some slower. The 2026 state budget allocated €70 million to digitise files and work down an estimated 20,000-case backlog — a real commitment, but digitisation projects move at civil-service speed.

Two practical consequences. First, the citizenship clock: several 2026 sources report that a May 2026 Nationality Law reform now measures the residence period from permit issuance and extends it — roughly ten years for most non-EU nationals, seven for EU and CPLP nationals — versus the five years older guides still quote. This is the most contested number in the programme; do not plan around it without Portuguese counsel confirming the current rule. Second, the waiting period itself: budget your life for the long timeline. This programme rewards patient capital, not urgent plans.

What it actually costs

The investment is the headline; the fees are the fine print. Government fees published for 2026 (AIMA's digital-channel rates — the figures you actually pay when filing online): €632.10 for the application analysis, €6,314.20 per applicant for the residence permit issuance, and €3,157.80 for each two-year renewal. Budget about €6,800 per applicant for the first round (analysis + issuance), before lawyers, fund fees, translations, and travel.

Family and the seven-day rule

One application covers the family: spouse or partner, dependent children up to age 26 (one of the most generous cut-offs in Europe), and dependent parents of either spouse. Each member gets their own residence card and their own fee bill, and everyone does biometrics in Portugal.

The stay requirement is the programme's signature perk: an average of seven days a year — structured as seven days in the first year and fourteen days across each subsequent two-year renewal period. Keep travel records (flights, hotels) as proof; renewals check them. You can maintain the permit indefinitely without relocating, which is exactly why it suits investors keeping a primary base in the US, UK, Canada, or Australia.

Tax and the citizenship question

Two topics where 2026 guides diverge — so here are the guardrails. On tax: Portugal's famous Non-Habitual Resident regime is largely history; the replacement incentive is IFICI, a 20% flat tax on qualifying professional and scientific income for eligible newcomers. Whether you qualify depends on your profession and circumstances — this is a question for a Portuguese tax adviser, not a blog post. Do not assume any special rate applies to you.

On citizenship: the old story was "five years to a Portuguese passport." The current story, per multiple 2026 publications, is longer — a reported ten-year residence period from permit issuance for most non-EU nationals (seven for EU/CPLP), plus an A2 Portuguese language certificate and a clean record, before you can apply. Other sources still quote the old figure. Because the difference is five years of someone's life, we will not pick a winner here: confirm the current nationality-law rule with Portuguese counsel before you build any plan around a passport. Dual citizenship is permitted by Portugal, but check whether your home country allows it too.

The risks agents won't lead with

Three risks, stated plainly. Fund risk: your €500,000 rides on a manager and a portfolio — if the fund underperforms, the residence survives but the capital doesn't fully. Liquidity risk: five years minimum, often six to ten in practice; money you might need sooner has no business in this programme. Timeline risk: the AIMA backlog and the shifting citizenship rules mean the "path to a passport" agents sell is longer and less certain than the brochure implies. None of this makes the programme bad — it remains the most flexible EU residency product for non-relocators — but price it with open eyes, and read every fund document before the transfer, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Portugal's golden visa still open in 2026?

Yes — the fund route at €500,000 remains active and is the dominant pathway. The real-estate route closed in October 2023 (Law 56/2023) and has not returned.

What makes a fund eligible for the Portugal golden visa?

Three tests: it must be regulated by Portugal's CMVM, have a minimum five-year maturity, and invest at least 60% of its capital in Portuguese-headquartered companies. Verify all three in the fund's documents before subscribing.

How long do I have to hold the €500,000 fund investment?

At least five years for the visa. Most qualifying funds run on 6- to 10-year terms, so your capital may be locked longer — check the fund's exit mechanics before you invest.

How long does the Portugal golden visa take in 2026?

Plan for 12 to 24 months from submission to residence card. AIMA carries a large backlog; the 2026 budget funded a digitisation push, but published guidance still runs to two years for complex cases.

Do I need to live in Portugal to keep the golden visa?

No. The stay requirement averages seven days a year (seven days in the first year, fourteen per two-year renewal). Keep travel records as renewal proof.

Can my family be included in one application?

Yes: spouse or partner, dependent children up to age 26, and dependent parents of either spouse. Each pays their own government fees (about €6,800 per applicant in the first round) and does biometrics in Portugal.

How long until a Portuguese passport?

This is the contested number. Older guides say five years; several 2026 publications report a nationality-law reform extended it to roughly ten years of residence from permit issuance (seven for EU/CPLP nationals), with an A2 Portuguese language certificate required. Confirm the current rule with Portuguese counsel before planning around a passport.

What are the government fees in 2026?

€632.10 application analysis fee plus €6,314.20 residence-permit issuance per applicant, and €3,157.80 per renewal every two years — before legal, fund, and translation costs.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

Portugal's golden visa in 2026 is a fund subscription, not a property purchase: €500,000 into a CMVM-regulated vehicle with a five-year maturity and 60% Portuguese deployment, held at least five years, with seven days a year in-country. Budget ~€6,800 per applicant in government fees plus legal and fund costs, plan for a 12- to 24-month wait at AIMA, and get the citizenship timeline confirmed by Portuguese counsel — it's the programme's least settled number.
