Portugal D7 Passive Income Visa 2026: The €920/Month Retirement Route

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

The D7 is Europe's lowest-bar retirement visa on paper — one minimum wage a month. In practice the number is the easy part: what decides your application is whether your income is genuinely passive, whether you can show a year of savings behind it, and whether you can survive the AIMA appointment queue. Here is the 2026 version, checked against Portugal's own documents.

Portugal's D7 residence visa for 2026 requires stable passive income of at least €920 a month (€11,040 a year) for the main applicant — one national minimum wage — plus 50% for a spouse and 30% per child. It is strictly for income you do not work for; remote workers must use the D8 instead. You apply at a Portuguese consulate, enter on a four-month visa, then convert it into a two-year residence permit with AIMA, Portugal's immigration agency.

Why the bar is one minimum wage — and what €920 a month really means

Most European retirement visas set their income floor at some round number dreamed up in a ministry. Portugal does it differently: the D7's income test is pegged to the national minimum wage, the retribuição mínima mensal garantida. That pegging is why the number moved on 1 January 2026. Decree-Law No. 139/2025, published in the Portuguese Official Gazette on 29 December 2025, raised the 2026 minimum wage to €920 a month, up from €870 in 2025 — and the D7 income bar moved with it, effective the same day.

The arithmetic is straightforward. The main applicant must show means of at least 100% of the minimum wage: €920 a month, or €11,040 a year, effective 2026. A spouse or second adult adds 50% — €460 a month. Each dependent child adds 30% — €276 a month. A couple therefore plans around €1,380 a month, and a couple with one child around €1,656 a month.

Two things about this peg matter more than the number itself. First, it moves every year. Guides written for 2025 that quote €870 are already wrong for anyone applying in 2026, and the same will happen again whenever the government adjusts the wage — there is a political roadmap toward €1,020 by 2028. Check the current minimum wage in the month you file, not the month you started researching. Second, the minimum wage is a planning reference, not a magic line where €919 fails and €921 passes. Consulates assess whether your income is stable, regular, and sufficient overall. But every serious application should meet or exceed the reference, because consular officers use it as the first sorting test.

There is also a useful piece of arithmetic hidden in the official embassy checklist: means of support can be reduced by half when you prove that accommodation is already secured, and by up to 90% of the amount when food is also secured. In practice this means a signed 12-month lease can meaningfully soften the income test — your biggest cost is demonstrably covered.

The passive-income filter is where applications actually fail

Here is the uncomfortable truth about the D7: the income amount is not what rejects people. The income type is. The visa exists for retired people and people living on their own income — the official Portuguese phrase is visto de residência para reformados e pessoas que vivam de rendimentos próprios. Pensions, Social Security, rental income from property, dividends, interest, royalties, annuities: these are the qualifying sources. What does not qualify is money you earn — a salary, freelance invoices, client retainers.

The embassy checklist makes the distinction explicit in the documents it demands. Pensioners must produce a document proving the amount of the pension and a document guaranteeing its receipt or availability in Portugal. People living on their own income must produce documents proving the existence and amount of income from movable or immovable property, intellectual property, or financial investments — plus proof that the income is available in Portugal.

Notice the emphasis on availability in national territory. This is where many applications quietly die. A pension paid into a foreign account with no evidence it reaches Portugal is weaker than the same pension routed to a Portuguese bank account. Most successful applicants open a Portuguese bank account before filing and move savings or regular payments into it. The consulate wants to see not just that you are wealthy on paper, but that the money will actually be where you are.

The savings buffer nobody tells you about until the appointment

Separate from the monthly income figure, the official checklist requires proof of financial resources for a period of at least 12 months, under Ordinance No. 1563/2007. In plain language: consulates expect to see savings covering roughly a year at the threshold — about €11,040 for a single applicant in 2026 — demonstrated through bank statements alongside your income documentation.

This is the most commonly overlooked part of the D7 file. Applicants fixate on proving they earn €920 a month and forget the second bar. The logic from the consulate's side is sound: they are admitting someone who will not be allowed to fall back on the Portuguese labour market, so they want a cushion against the month the dividend is cut or the rental sits empty. Show three months of bank statements at minimum, and make sure the balance tells a story of stability rather than a last-minute deposit that appeared the week before your appointment.

D7 vs D8: choosing the wrong visa is the expensive mistake

Portugal runs two residence routes that look similar from a distance and are legally different animals. The D7 is for passive income at one minimum wage. The D8 — Portugal's digital-nomad visa — is for people who work remotely for employers or clients outside Portugal, and it demands average monthly income of at least four minimum wages over the last three months: €3,680 a month in 2026.

The rule of thumb is simple and worth stating plainly: if your money arrives whether or not you work that month, you are a D7 case. If it arrives because you worked, you are a D8 case. The D8 threshold is exactly four times the D7 one, so there is no middle ground to argue about. A freelancer billing foreign clients at €2,000 a month is not a weak D7 applicant — they are a D8 applicant who does not qualify, and they need to know that before spending months on the wrong file.

Note: remote workers sometimes ask whether they can apply for the D7 with freelance income and "just not mention the work." Do not. Consulates check income sources, and a D7 granted on misrepresented income is a renewal problem — or worse — later.

The two-stage timeline: consulate visa first, AIMA permit second

The D7 is not one application; it is two stages in two places. Stage one happens at the Portuguese consulate with jurisdiction over where you legally live. You submit the national visa application, passport, photos, criminal-record certificate (with Hague Apostille, generally no more than six months old), travel insurance covering medical expenses and repatriation, proof of accommodation, and the financial evidence described above. The standard processing window is about 60 days, and what you receive is a residence visa valid for four months with two entries.

Stage two happens in Portugal. Within that four-month window you must attend your appointment with AIMA — the Agência para a Integração, Migrações e Asilo, the agency that replaced the old SEF in late 2023 and now handles all residence-permit processing. At that appointment the visa converts into a residence permit. This is also where the timeline gets honest: the consulate stage runs roughly on schedule, but AIMA appointments have been the slow part of the Portuguese system, with backlogs running into the hundreds of thousands of cases. Plan for a gap of months between arrival and card in hand, and keep your visa's validity window in mind when booking travel.

A few practical notes from the official document lists. Your passport should be valid well beyond the intended stay. The criminal-record certificate must come from your country of nationality or any country where you lived for over a year, with the Apostille. Family members can apply alongside you for accompanying-family visas, assessed together with yours. And the national D-type visa fee was raised in early 2025 — confirm the current fee at your consulate before you pay, since fees are revised periodically.

Renewal and real residence: the two-year permit, the three-year renewals

The first residence permit is issued for two years. Under Portugal's immigration law, temporary residence permits then renew for successive three-year periods, provided the conditions that justified the permit still hold. After five years of legal residence, permanent residence becomes available — and the D7 is a residence visa in the full sense: Schengen travel, access to the public healthcare system, and the right to have family join you are all part of the package.

One condition deserves emphasis because it is the quiet counterpart to the low income bar: the permit is for people who actually live in Portugal. The D7 is not a flag-of-convenience document for someone who wants a European residence card while spending eleven months a year elsewhere. Renewal assumes genuine residence, and long absences can put a renewal at risk. Before planning extended time away, check AIMA's current guidance on permitted absences for your permit type.

A final word on tax, since it comes up in every D7 conversation: the old Non-Habitual Resident flat-tax regime closed to new applicants in 2024, and foreign pensions are now taxed under Portugal's normal brackets. A successor incentive regime exists for qualified professionals, but a D7 retiree living on a pension should budget on ordinary tax treatment and take proper advice rather than relying on outdated blog posts about tax-free pensions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum income for the Portugal D7 visa in 2026?

The reference is one Portuguese national minimum wage: €920 per month in 2026, or €11,040 per year for the main applicant. Add 50% (€460/month) for a spouse or other adult, and 30% (€276/month) for each dependent child. Consulates treat it as a planning reference, not a hard pass-fail number — stability and regularity of income matter too.

What is the difference between the D7 and the D8 visa in Portugal?

The D7 is for passive income — pensions, rental income, dividends, royalties. The D8 is Portugal's digital-nomad visa for people who work remotely, and it requires average monthly income of at least four minimum wages (€3,680/month in 2026). If your money arrives because you work, apply for the D8; if it arrives whether or not you work, apply for the D7.

Can I work in Portugal on a D7 visa?

The D7 is designed for people who do not need to work — your application must show stable passive income. Portuguese residence law does not strip permit holders of the right to professional activity, but a D7 renewal is assessed on your passive means, so quietly turning the stay into a working life is a renewal risk. If you intend to keep earning, the D8 is the honest route.

How much savings do I need to show for the D7 in 2026?

Separate from monthly income, consulates commonly expect savings covering roughly 12 months at the threshold — about €11,040 for a single applicant in 2026. The embassy checklist requires proof of financial resources for at least 12 months, and many posts check that the money is available in Portugal, typically via a Portuguese bank account.

How long does the D7 application process take?

The consulate stage has a standard processing window of about 60 days, and the residence visa it grants is valid for four months (120 days) with two entries. After you arrive in Portugal, the AIMA residence-permit appointment is the second stage — and that appointment has been the slow part of the process, so plan for months between arrival and card in hand.

Does the D7 require accommodation in Portugal before applying?

Yes — proof of accommodation is a required document, usually a 12-month rental contract or proof of property ownership. There is a useful trade-off in the official checklist: if you can prove accommodation is secured, the required means of support can be reduced by half, because your biggest cost is already covered.

Does the D7 visa lead to permanent residence or citizenship?

The first residence permit is issued for two years and renewed in three-year periods under Portugal's immigration law. After five years of legal residence you may apply for permanent residence. The path to citizenship changed with a 2026 nationality-law revision — check the current rules with AIMA or the consulate before counting on any particular timeline.

Bottom Line

The D7 remains Europe's most accessible retirement visa in 2026 — €920 a month in genuinely passive income, a year of savings behind it, and a home lined up in Portugal. The number is the easy part; the decisions that matter are proving the income is passive and available in Portugal, choosing the D7 over the D8 honestly, and budgeting real time for the AIMA appointment queue after you land.
