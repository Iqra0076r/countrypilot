Spain's Beckham Law 2026: the 24% flat tax for nomads

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

Spain lets qualifying newcomers pay a flat 24% on employment income up to €600,000 a year instead of the progressive scale that climbs to 47% — and since 2023, remote workers on the digital nomad visa are explicitly invited in. This guide explains who the regime actually covers, the 24%-versus-47% math, the six-month application clock, and the catches that calculators miss. This is information to help you understand the rules, not tax advice: Spanish tax residence has real consequences, so confirm your position with a qualified asesor before you opt in.

Spain's special inbound regime (Article 93 LIRPF, the "Beckham Law") taxes qualifying newcomers at a flat 24% on employment income up to €600,000 a year — 47% above that — for the year of arrival plus five more tax years. You must not have been Spanish tax resident in the previous five tax years, and you opt in on Modelo 149 within six months of Social Security registration. Digital nomad visa holders employed by a foreign company qualify; freelance autónomos generally do not.

What the Beckham Law is, and why nomads got invited in 2023

The regime's official name is a mouthful — the special tax regime for workers, professionals, entrepreneurs, and investors relocated to Spanish territory — and its legal home is Article 93 of the Personal Income Tax Law (Ley 35/2006). The nickname comes from David Beckham, whose 2003 move to Real Madrid made the regime famous. The mechanism is elegant: you become a Spanish tax resident, but you elect to be taxed under non-resident rules, keeping your income-tax-resident status.

For two decades the door was an employment door in the narrow sense: you had to move to Spain for a job with a Spanish company, or as a company director. The 2023 Startup Law (Ley 28/2022, effective January 1, 2023) rewrote the guest list. Among other changes, it expressly added telework for a company located outside Spain as a qualifying reason for the move — which is the clause that opened the regime to digital nomad visa holders. The same reform cut the prior-non-residence requirement from ten tax years to five, and let spouses and young children opt in as associated taxpayers.

Understanding that history matters because so much online advice predates it. Anything written before 2023 that says remote workers cannot use the Beckham Law is describing a regime that no longer exists.

The 24% question: when flat beats progressive

The headline is simple. Under the regime, employment income is taxed at a flat 24% up to €600,000 a year, and 47% on the slice above €600,000. The standard alternative is Spain's progressive IRPF scale, which starts at 19% nationally (plus the regional component, which varies by autonomous community) and climbs to 47% at the top.

Whether flat beats progressive depends entirely on your income. At the top end the comparison is not close: a €400,000 salary that would face marginal rates approaching 47% under the ordinary scale pays a flat 24% under Beckham — a difference worth tens of thousands of euros a year. That is the use case the regime was built for, and it is why high-earning relocations to Madrid and Barcelona so often hinge on this election.

But the flat rate is not a discount at every income. Because the ordinary scale starts at 19%, a modest salary can pay less under standard taxation than under the 24% flat. Expat tax analysis typically puts the breakeven in the low €60,000s in Madrid — earn less than that and the Beckham election can cost you money rather than save it. The honest move is to model both: your actual salary, your actual autonomous community's scale, six years of it. The regime is a tool, not a prize.

Who actually qualifies: the employment door, not the freelance door

The conditions, as AEAT sets them out, are cumulative — miss one and the regime is unavailable:

1. Five clean years. You must not have been tax resident in Spain during the five tax years before your move. (This was ten years before the 2023 reform — another reason to ignore pre-2023 explainers.)

2. A qualifying reason for the move. The displacement must result from one of the statute's circumstances: an employment contract (including telework for a foreign employer, since 2023), becoming a director of an entity in which you hold no controlling stake, or — for the self-employed — qualifying as an entrepreneur with a favourable ENISA report or as a highly qualified professional doing R&D or working for a startup.

3. No Spanish permanent establishment. Apart from the narrow entrepreneur and professional exceptions, you must not earn income through a permanent establishment in Spain.

Read that second condition carefully and the nomad picture snaps into focus. A digital nomad visa holder who is an employee teleworking for a foreign company walks through the employment door cleanly — the visa and the tax regime were designed to meet. A nomad on the visa's freelance track, registered as an autónomo billing clients, generally does not qualify: self-employment income is not employment income, and the statute's exceptions for the self-employed are narrow (the ENISA-favourable entrepreneur, certain startup and R&D professionals). This is the single most expensive misunderstanding in the nomad-Beckham conversation. Your visa says "digital nomad"; the tax law asks "employee or freelancer?" — and only one answer opens the door.

If you are weighing the move itself, model the full cost of relocating — not just the tax — with the relocation budget calculator → before you commit to a Spanish base.

The 6-month clock: Modelo 149, Modelo 151, and how opting in works

The regime is elective, and the election has a hard deadline. You opt in by filing Modelo 149 with the AEAT within six months of your registration with Spanish Social Security (or, where applicable, of the documentation that maintains your home-country social security coverage). Miss the window and the regime is gone for that move — there is no late filing, no appeal to fairness.

The filing itself is documentary: the form plus evidence of the qualifying circumstance — the employment contract or displacement letter with the start date, the directorship documentation, or the entrepreneur/professional accreditation as applicable. AEAT issues a certificate confirming the regime applies, and you then file your annual return on Modelo 151 rather than the ordinary Modelo 100.

Two exit mechanics deserve attention before you enter. You can renounce the regime, but only during November and December of the year before the renunciation takes effect — and renunciation is irrevocable; you cannot opt back in later. You are excluded automatically if you breach any condition (for example, by acquiring a permanent establishment), with effect in the year of breach, and you must report the breach to AEAT within a month on Modelo 149. The regime rewards the organized and punishes the inattentive.

Note: The six-month clock runs from Social Security registration, not from your visa issue date or your arrival flight. Coordinate the tax election with your alta en la Seguridad Social — or with your employer's Spanish payroll registration — from day one.

What changes beyond the rate: wealth tax, foreign income, family

The 24% rate gets the headlines, but the regime rewrites three other parts of your tax life.

Foreign income mostly drops out. Apart from employment income — which is taxed on a worldwide basis at the flat rates — only Spanish-source income is taxed. Foreign investment income, dividends from foreign shares, and gains on foreign property generally fall outside the Spanish net entirely. For a newcomer with a portfolio abroad, this can matter more than the rate itself.

Wealth tax shrinks to Spanish assets. Electing the regime puts you under wealth tax by obligación real — real obligation — meaning only assets located in Spain count. Under ordinary residence, your worldwide assets would be in scope. (Spain's wealth tax is levied regionally with varying thresholds and reliefs, Madrid's 100% relief being the famous example — but the Beckham narrowing of the base applies regardless of region.)

Family can ride along. Since the 2023 reform, your spouse and children under 25 — or of any age in case of disability — can opt in as associated taxpayers, provided they move with you and meet the residence conditions. Before 2023 the regime was strictly individual; the family extension meaningfully changes the calculus for relocating households.

The 2025–2026 wrinkles worth knowing

Two recent developments belong in any 2026 guide. First, in July 2025 Spain's Central Economic-Administrative Tribunal confirmed that Beckham beneficiaries must include imputed rental income on their Spanish primary residence in taxable income — a charge that does not apply under ordinary residence. If you buy your home in Spain under the regime, budget for the imputación. It is a small line with an outsized lesson: the regime's differences from ordinary taxation cut both ways.

Second, the regime's interaction with double-tax treaties remains genuinely unsettled in edge cases. Because you are taxed under non-resident rules while remaining treaty-resident, treaty provisions can produce surprising results — particularly for UK and US nationals with treaty-protected income types. This is squarely "get advice" territory, not "read a blog" territory.

And a final structural note: the Beckham clock is six tax years, fixed. It does not renew, and there is no second helping later — if you leave Spain and return, the five-clean-years condition starts counting again from zero. Treat the six years as a window to be used deliberately: the regime is most valuable when your high-earning years and your Spanish years overlap.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Beckham Law in Spain?

The special tax regime for workers, professionals, entrepreneurs and investors relocated to Spain (Article 93 of the Personal Income Tax Law). Qualifying newcomers are taxed under non-resident rules while remaining income-tax residents: a flat 24% on employment income up to 600,000 euros a year, 47% above, for the year of arrival plus the five following tax years.

Can digital nomad visa holders use the Beckham Law?

Yes, if they are employees teleworking for a foreign company — the 2023 Startup Law reform expressly added international remote work as a qualifying reason. Self-employed digital nomads on the freelance track generally cannot, unless they qualify through the entrepreneur route with a favourable ENISA report or as certain startup or R&D professionals.

How long does the Beckham Law last?

Six tax years in total: the tax year in which you become Spanish tax resident plus the five following tax years, provided you keep meeting the conditions. You can renounce the regime in November or December of the preceding year, but renunciation is final — you cannot opt back in.

How do you apply for the Beckham Law?

You opt in by filing Modelo 149 with the Spanish tax agency (AEAT) within six months of your Social Security registration or the start of your activity. Annual filing is then done on Modelo 151. Missing the six-month window forfeits the regime for that move.

Does the Beckham Law exempt foreign income?

Largely, yes: apart from employment income, which is taxed on a worldwide basis at the flat rates, only Spanish-source income is taxed. Foreign investment income and capital gains on foreign assets generally fall outside the Spanish net, and wealth tax applies only to Spanish assets.

Is the Beckham Law worth it on a lower salary?

Not always. Spain's ordinary progressive scale starts at 19% at the bottom, so on modest incomes the flat 24% can cost more than standard taxation. Expat tax analysis typically puts the breakeven in the low 60,000s of euros in Madrid — below that, run both calculations before opting in.

Can my spouse use the Beckham Law too?

Since the 2023 reform, the spouse and children under 25 (or of any age if disabled) can opt in as associated taxpayers alongside the main beneficiary, provided they also meet the residence conditions. This was a significant expansion of the old regime.

Bottom Line

The Beckham Law is Spain's best tax deal for high-earning newcomers — a flat 24% to €600,000, six years, foreign income mostly out of scope — but it is an employment door, not a freelance one, and the six-month Modelo 149 clock starts with your Social Security registration, not your arrival. If you are a remote employee on the digital nomad visa earning above the breakeven, opt in deliberately and early; if you are freelance or modestly paid, the ordinary scale may serve you better.
