How to use this Germany visa and entry guide

Germany runs two completely separate entry systems, and mixing them up is the most common planning mistake. Short stays (tourism, business trips, family visits up to 90 days) fall under the Schengen Type C visa, applied for at a German mission or VFS centre before travel. Long stays (work, study, family reunion, the EU Blue Card, the Chancenkarte) need a national Type D visa issued by the embassy — a different form, different fee, different timeline.

The controlling authority is the Federal Foreign Office (Auswärtiges Amt) at auswaertiges-amt.de, which publishes visa information through its country-mission pages (for example india.diplo.de for India). This guide was verified against those mission pages in October 2026. Fees and processing times vary by mission — always confirm on the mission page for your country before paying.

Passport validity and document condition

As a Schengen state, Germany applies the EU-wide passport rule: your passport must have been issued within the last 10 years and remain valid for at least 3 months beyond your intended departure from the Schengen area. Travellers from India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Philippines and Bangladesh should also carry at least two blank pages; airlines enforce these rules at boarding through Timatic, and a non-compliant passport means denied boarding, not a negotiation at the gate.

For national long-stay visas, missions additionally expect the passport to cover the full initial visa period. If your passport is near the 10-year issue limit — common with older UK passports that carried extra months — renew before applying.

Identify the correct visa or entry route

Work through the routes in order. First, visa-free entry: US and UK citizens (and other visa-exempt nationalities) may enter Germany and the Schengen area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa — no application, no fee. Second, the Schengen Type C visa: for tourism, business or family visits up to 90 days. Fee €90 for adults, €45 for children aged 6–11, free for under-6s (2026, set at EU level). Third, the national Type D visa: for stays over 90 days — work, study, Ausbildung, family reunion, Chancenkarte. Fee €75, paid at the embassy or consulate (2026).

Transit and onward travel

Germany applies the EU airport-transit-visa (Type A) list: nationals of certain countries need a transit visa even for an airside-only connection at a German airport, while others may transit without one. Because the list is nationality-specific and changes, check the German mission page for your country before booking a connecting itinerary — this is the single most overlooked requirement in Germany trip planning.

If you need to leave the airport during a layover, a Type A visa is not enough: you need a full Schengen Type C visa. Travellers connecting onward to a non-Schengen destination should also confirm that their first Schengen entry point (often Frankfurt or Munich) is where passport control happens, even if Germany is not the final destination.

Evidence immigration officers may ask to see

A Schengen visa gives you the right to present yourself at the border — the Bundespolizei officer decides admission. Carry your passport with the visa sticker, travel medical insurance proof, the return or onward ticket, hotel bookings or a host invitation, and evidence of funds for the stay. Officers routinely ask about the purpose of the trip and how it is financed.

For national-visa holders arriving for work or study, carry the employment contract or university admission letter in addition to the visa. First entry on a Type D visa should be through the country that issued it where practical, and you must register your address (Anmeldung) at the local Bürgeramt within 14 days of moving in — the residence permit appointment at the Ausländerbehörde follows.

Health documents and vaccination certificates

Germany sets no general vaccination requirement for entry, and there are no pandemic-era border health checks for travellers. No health document is presented at the German border for an ordinary Schengen or national-visa trip.

The health requirement that matters is insurance, not vaccination (see below). Long-stay residents will interact with the German health system after arrival through the mandatory health insurance, but that is a residence matter handled at the Ausländerbehörde, not a border document.

Insurance as an entry or visa condition

Travel medical insurance is a Schengen visa condition: the policy must cover at least €30,000 for emergency medical care, hospitalization and repatriation, and be valid across the entire Schengen area for the whole trip. The mission will refuse the application without compliant proof — this is checked at the application stage, not at the border.

For students staying long-term, budget for German health insurance: public (statutory) student insurance runs about €140–€152 per month (roughly €1,700–€1,800 per year) for degree students under 30 (2026), while private plans start around €30–€150 per month depending on coverage. Enrolment at a German university is not possible without proof of health insurance.

Arrival permission and permitted stay

On a Schengen Type C visa, each stay is capped at 90 days in any 180-day period, counted across the whole Schengen area — days spent in France or Italy count against the same allowance. The border officer may stamp the passport; keep your own count, because overstaying even by a day can trigger fines, deportation and future visa refusals.

National Type D visa holders are admitted for the long stay stated on the visa and must convert it into a residence permit at the local Ausländerbehörde after arrival — the visa alone does not cover the full stay. Do not let the initial visa expire before the residence-permit appointment; book that appointment as early as the local office allows.

Work, study and residence are separate questions

A Schengen visa never authorizes employment — not even remote work discussions that turn into local activity. Work requires the national route. The headline work route is the EU Blue Card: from 1 January 2026 the salary thresholds are €50,700 gross per year standard, or €45,934.20 for shortage occupations and recent graduates (verified on make-it-in-germany.com, the official federal portal). The national work visa fee is €75 (2026), and the Blue Card residence permit itself costs about €100 after arrival.

Students need the national student visa (€75, 2026) plus a Sperrkonto blocked account of €11,904 per year (€992 per month) — unchanged since January 2025 — as proof of funds. Chancenkarte (Opportunity Card) job-seekers need €13,092 (€1,091 per month). Job-seeking on a Schengen visitor visa with the intent to convert is not a recognized route; apply for the correct national visa from home.

Minors, dual nationals and special documents

Each child needs their own passport and their own visa application — there are no family Schengen visas, and the reduced fees (€45 for ages 6–11, free under 6, as of 2026) apply per child. Minors travelling without both parents should carry a consent letter from the non-travelling parent(s); German missions and border officers may ask for it.

Dual nationals should enter on the passport that matches their visa or visa-free status, and keep both passports available. Travellers with refugee travel documents or alien's passports face different rules from ordinary passport holders — confirm the applicable regime on the mission page rather than assuming standard Schengen treatment.

A reliable pre-travel workflow

Schengen Type C: 1) Confirm the German mission or VFS centre handling your jurisdiction on the mission page. 2) Complete the application form and gather documents (passport, photos, itinerary, insurance proof, funds evidence, employment/student ties). 3) Book the biometric appointment — first-time applicants must appear in person. 4) Pay the €90 fee (2026). 5) Allow 15 working days, up to 45 in complex cases (2026 mission guidance).

National Type D (work/student): 1) Secure the underlying basis first — job offer with Federal Employment Agency pre-approval, or university admission. 2) Open the Sperrkonto blocked account early (€11,904 for students). 3) Book the embassy interview. 4) Pay the €75 fee (2026). 5) Allow 4 weeks for Blue Card/pre-approved employment, up to 12 weeks for other employment and for student visas depending on the mission (2026) — the local Ausländerbehörde in Germany is usually involved, which drives the timeline.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Applying for the wrong visa type. A Schengen visa cannot be converted into a work or student residence after arrival — start with the national visa.
  • Underestimating the student timeline. With Ausländerbehörde involvement, student visas can take up to 12 weeks; apply the moment you have admission.
  • Non-compliant insurance. A policy under €30,000 cover or not valid Schengen-wide means refusal.
  • Counting 90 days per country. The 90/180-day clock runs across the entire Schengen area.
  • Ignoring the Anmeldung. Address registration within 14 days of arrival is mandatory and gates everything else, including the residence permit.
  • Old passport near the 10-year issue limit. Schengen refuses passports issued more than 10 years ago, even if still technically valid.

Building a stronger visa or entry file

German missions decide Schengen applications on three questions: will you leave on time, can you fund the trip, and is the purpose genuine. Answer with paper: a dated itinerary with bookings, 3–6 months of bank statements showing regular income (not a sudden lump deposit), an employment letter with approved leave, and property or family ties.

For national visas, the file lives or dies on the underlying basis — the job contract with the correct salary threshold for a Blue Card, or the university admission plus the full Sperrkonto. A blocked account funded below €11,904 is the most common student-refusal reason and the most avoidable.

eVisas, online forms and scam avoidance

Germany offers no eVisa: the Schengen application starts with an online or paper form depending on the mission, but first-time applicants appear in person for biometrics, and the visa is a sticker in the passport. Track the application through the mission or VFS reference system.

Scam avoidance: the only legitimate channels are the German mission, VFS Global where designated, and auswaertiges-amt.de. No agent can guarantee approval or sell an earlier appointment — anyone promising either is running a scam. Never pay a 'visa fee' to a personal account; official fees are paid at the mission or VFS counter with a receipt.

Translations, certifications and document format

Bring originals plus one copy of everything: bank statements (3–6 months), employment letter, the Sperrkonto confirmation for students, the job contract for workers, travel insurance proof, and the full itinerary. Documents not in German or English need a certified translation.

Photographs must meet Schengen specifications (35×45 mm, recent, plain background) — the VFS photo booths are calibrated for this, which is the practical reason to use them. For national visas, degree certificates and professional qualifications may need recognition checks; start those early, as they run on a separate timeline from the visa.

Interviews, biometrics and identity checks

First-time Schengen applicants give fingerprints and a digital photograph at the appointment; the data stays in the Visa Information System for 59 months, so repeat applicants within that window may be exempt from appearing. A short interview about the trip's purpose, funding and ties to home is standard.

National-visa applicants attend an embassy interview as a matter of course. Answer consistently with the written file — discrepancies between the interview and the documents are a classic refusal trigger. At the border, Bundespolizei may re-verify the trip's purpose and funds; carry the same evidence you submitted.

Families and group travel

Every traveller needs a separate application and appointment — including infants — with per-child fees (€45 for ages 6–11, free under 6, as of 2026). There is no family Schengen application; each file is decided on its own merits, so a weak file for one member is not rescued by a strong file for another.

Groups should book VFS appointments together where the system allows. If one member is refused, the others' visas stand, but do not re-apply with identical weak facts — address the refusal reason first, since the refusal is recorded in the Visa Information System.

Customs, cash and restricted goods

Within the EU's external-border rules, travellers entering Germany must declare cash of €10,000 or more (per person or group travelling together) — it is not taxed, but failure to declare can lead to seizure. There is no limit on the amount itself.

Standard EU restrictions apply to animal and plant products, and medications should be in original packaging with a prescription for controlled substances. Duty-free allowances apply per adult traveller for alcohol and tobacco when arriving from outside the EU.

Departure and future travel

Keep evidence of timely departure — exit stamps and boarding passes — because a clean Schengen history is the strongest asset in your next application. Overstaying, even briefly, can mean fines, deportation and an entry ban recorded across the Schengen area.

National-visa holders: do not travel extensively outside Germany while waiting for the residence permit unless the Ausländerbehörde confirms the visa covers re-entry. A valid residence permit, once issued, allows travel across Schengen under the 90/180-day rule.

How to research a route without getting misled

Use this hierarchy: 1) the German mission page for your country (e.g. india.diplo.de) — it overrides everything else on procedure, fees and timelines. 2) auswaertiges-amt.de for the federal framework. 3) make-it-in-germany.com, the official federal portal, for skilled-worker and Blue Card routes. 4) The Ausländerbehörde website of your destination city for post-arrival steps.

Treat forums and agents' blogs as leads, not answers — Blue Card thresholds, blocked-account amounts and processing times change, and 2025 advice is already stale on several points. Any site charging far above the €90/€75 official fees is monetizing your anxiety.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Indians need a visa for Germany?

Yes. Indian passport holders need a Schengen Type C visa for visits up to 90 days (€90 fee, 2026), applied for at a German mission or VFS centre before travel — there is no visa on arrival and no eVisa. For work or study, the national Type D visa (€75, 2026) is the route, with processing of 4–12 weeks depending on the category and mission.

How much is the blocked account for Germany in 2026?

€11,904 per year (€992 per month) in a Sperrkonto blocked account — unchanged since January 2025. This is the standard proof of funds for the German student visa. Chancenkarte (Opportunity Card) applicants need €13,092 (€1,091 per month).

What is the EU Blue Card salary threshold for Germany in 2026?

From 1 January 2026: €50,700 gross per year standard, or €45,934.20 for shortage occupations and recent graduates — up about 5% from 2025. The national work visa fee is €75 (2026), and the Blue Card residence permit costs about €100 after arrival.

Can I convert a German tourist visa into a work visa after arriving?

No. A Schengen visa cannot be converted into a work or student residence permit after arrival. If you intend to work or study, apply for the national Type D visa from your home country before travelling.

How long does a German Schengen visa take in 2026?

Typically 15 working days, up to 45 for complex cases (2026 mission guidance). National visas take longer: about 4 weeks for Blue Card/pre-approved employment, up to 12 weeks for other employment and student visas, because the local Ausländerbehörde must approve. Apply as early as the mission allows.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

For Germany, pick the right system first: Schengen Type C (€90, 2026) for visits up to 90 days, national Type D (€75, 2026) for work or study — they do not convert into each other. Keep the passport within the 10-year/3-month Schengen rule, carry €30,000-compliant insurance for Schengen trips, fund the €11,904 Sperrkonto for study, and check the Blue Card's 2026 thresholds (€50,700 / €45,934.20) before signing a contract. Confirm fees and timelines on the German mission page for your country; they vary by mission.
