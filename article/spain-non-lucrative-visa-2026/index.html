Spain Non-Lucrative Visa 2026: The €2,400/Month No-Work Residence

Spain's non-lucrative visa is the most honest residence permit in Europe: it asks you to prove, in bank statements and a sworn letter, that you do not need to work — and then holds you to it. Here is what €2,400 a month actually buys you, the affidavit that surprises applicants, and the renewal rhythm that turns one careful year into permanent residence.

Spain's non-lucrative visa for 2026 requires the main applicant to show €2,400 a month (€28,800 a year) — 400% of the IPREM public income indicator — plus €600 a month for each family member. It is a consulate-only application that forbids all work, including remote work for a foreign employer. The first residence permit lasts one year, renewed in two two-year periods, with full-coverage health insurance required throughout.

Why Spain prices the no-work visa at 400% of the IPREM

Spain does not pick its visa income thresholds by feel. The non-lucrative visa's financial bar is defined as a multiple of the IPREM — the Indicador Público de Renta de Efectos Múltiples, Spain's public income indicator, set each year in the national budget. For 2026 the IPREM is €600 a month (€7,200 a year), and the main applicant must show 400% of it: €2,400 a month, or €28,800 a year. Every accompanying family member — spouse, child, dependent parent — adds another 100% of the IPREM: €600 a month, €7,200 a year each. A couple budgets €3,000 a month; a family of four budgets €4,200.

The IPREM mechanism is worth understanding because it is the reason old guides mislead. The IPREM sat at €600 a month through several budget cycles, which is why the €2,400 figure feels permanent — but it is not. When the budget moves the IPREM, the visa threshold moves with it automatically. Anyone applying late in a year should check the current figure on their own consulate's page rather than trusting a number read months earlier.

Why 400%? The logic is deliberately conservative. Spain is admitting someone who cannot legally earn a euro inside the country, so the bar is set well above subsistence — roughly four times the reference income. The consulate is not just checking that you can survive; it is checking that you can absorb a bad year without becoming a burden on the Spanish state. Treat €28,800 as the floor of your planning, not the plan itself.

The no-work rule is absolute — and it includes remote work

The official consular guidance states it without ambiguity: the visa is for residing in Spain without carrying out any gainful work or professional activity, provided the applicant has sufficient and guaranteed means to live on. It does not constitute a work permit. And then the sentence that ends a thousand internet debates: it does not allow any type of work or professional activity, including remotely (online).

This is the single most misunderstood rule in European retirement visas. The reasoning is not about where your employer's office is; it is about what you do with your days. Spain's position is that residence under this visa is incompatible with economic activity, full stop. Keeping a foreign salary flowing into your account while living in Valencia is, in the consulate's eyes, working — and saying otherwise in the file is misrepresentation.

Note: if you plan to keep working remotely, you are not a non-lucrative applicant — you are a telework (digital nomad) visa applicant. Spain created that separate route precisely for remote workers, with its own income test. Applying for the NLV while intending to work is the commonest avoidable refusal.

Savings or income: how consulates actually read your bank statements

The consulate does not ask for a salary slip. It asks for proof that you have sufficient means to cover the initial year of the residence permit, or proof of a regular income source that removes any need for paid work. In practice that evidence is bank statements from the last three months across all your accounts — checking, savings, investment — plus bank certificates for each account showing the bank's name and address, the account identification, opening dates, the balance on 31 December of the previous year, and the average balance over the previous twelve months.

Read that list as a consular officer would. The December 31 balance and the twelve-month average exist to catch the same trick: money that arrived last week. A single large deposit shortly before the appointment, with no history behind it, reads as borrowed funds. Steady balances, regular pension or dividend deposits, and a latest tax return that matches the story in the statements — that is what a strong file looks like. If you are a retiree, pension award letters work; the documents must show the income is current and will continue for at least the following twelve months.

Savings count, and a lump sum can carry the application if it clearly covers the period. But savings invite a different scrutiny: can the officer see the origin of the funds and be confident they will last? Pensioners with regular monthly income have the easier evidentiary path. Either way, original stamped documents beat screenshots, and for applicants of working age or currently employed, the consulate advises adding a termination letter from the employer — or a sabbatical acceptance letter — stating the work relationship has ended. The logic is consistent: the file must show that the working chapter is closed.

The affidavit and the insurance policy: the two documents that surprise applicants

Two requirements in the consular list catch people off guard because no other European retirement visa asks for them in quite this form. The first is the notarized affidavit: a letter, in Spanish or with a sworn translation, in which you explain your professional background, why you have decided to live in Spain, how long you plan to stay — and then commit, in writing, not to do any lucrative work by any means, on-site or remotely, while residing in Spain. You also state the Spanish address where you will live for at least the first three months. It is a sworn statement of intent, and it is taken seriously because it is the document a later violation would be measured against.

The second is the health insurance policy, and Spain's standard is stricter than most. The policy must come from an insurer authorized to operate in Spain, cover all the risks covered by Spain's public health system, and — crucially — have no deficiency, co-payment, or coverage limit: it must cover 100% of medical, hospital, and out-of-hospital expenses. It must be valid for one year and maintained for the entire stay. Travel insurance with medical assistance is explicitly not accepted, and an insurance card alone is not accepted as proof — the consulate wants the policy itself, in Spanish or with a sworn translation.

The document list is long and every consulate's version differs slightly — use the visa document checklist → to build your file against the requirements above, and double-check each item against your own consulate's page before booking the appointment.

A few supporting documents round out the file: a medical certificate issued within 90 days confirming you have no disease of serious public-health significance; a criminal background check from your country or countries of residence for the past five years, issued within the last six months, with the Hague Apostille (for US applicants, the FBI check — state checks are not accepted); and advisable extras like a minimum three-month lease or property title deed, plus school enrollment letters if you bring children of compulsory school age.

One year, then two plus two: the renewal rhythm and the tax bill that comes with it

The first residence permit is granted for one year. Renewals then run two years, then two years again — the 1+2+2 structure that carries you to the five-year mark, where long-term residence becomes available. Each renewal re-tests the financial conditions: you must still have the means, still hold the insurance, still not be working. The renewal is not a formality; it is a second application with the same philosophy as the first.

There is also a financial reality that the visa page does not advertise but every holder discovers: becoming a Spanish resident makes you a Spanish tax resident if you spend more than 183 days a year in the country, and Spain taxes residents on worldwide income at standard progressive rates. The non-lucrative visa, precisely because it involves no Spanish employment, does not qualify for the Beckham Law's flat-rate regime, which attaches to work-based moves. Budget for ordinary Spanish taxation on your pension, dividends, and rental income — and take advice on the double-tax treaty between Spain and your home country before the first tax year, not after.

On timing: Spain's new immigration regulation (Royal Decree 1155/2024) took effect on 20 May 2025, modernizing the procedures behind these applications. The consular pages still carry a notice that only the regulations in force at the time of filing apply — read the current version of your consulate's page, not a cached one.

When the non-lucrative visa is the wrong visa

The NLV is the right visa for a narrow, well-defined person: someone whose income is settled, passive, and ample — a pensioner, a landlord, a dividend investor — who wants to live in Spain and genuinely does not need to work. It is the wrong visa for almost everyone else in the retirement-adjacent space. If you plan to keep freelancing for foreign clients, take the telework visa. If you want to start a business in Spain, look at the entrepreneur route. If you want to invest your way in, the golden visa's property route ended — but other Spanish residence routes remain, and choosing among them on your actual plans rather than the income threshold is what keeps a file honest.

It is also worth being honest about cost of living. €2,400 a month is the visa's bar, not a Madrid budget. Rents in Madrid, Barcelona, and the Balearics will consume most of it; smaller cities and the interior leave real breathing room. The visa gets you legal residence. Choosing where in Spain that residence happens is what makes the numbers work in real life.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the income requirement for Spain's non-lucrative visa in 2026?

The main applicant must show €2,400 per month (€28,800 per year), which is 400% of Spain's IPREM indicator — €600 a month in 2026. Each accompanying family member adds another 100% of the IPREM: €600 per month (€7,200 per year). The money can be regular passive income or savings covering the period.

Can I work remotely on the Spain non-lucrative visa?

No. The visa does not constitute a work permit and forbids any gainful work or professional activity, including remote work performed online for a foreign employer. Remote workers should apply for Spain's telework (digital nomad) visa instead — it is a separate route with its own requirements.

Where do I apply for the Spain non-lucrative visa?

Only at the Spanish consulate with jurisdiction over your legal residence — there is no way to apply from inside Spain. You need legal residence in that consular district, and the consulate applies its own document list, so always follow your own consulate's page.

Can I use savings instead of monthly income for the NLV?

Yes. The consulate asks for proof that you have sufficient means to cover the initial year of the residence permit — either regular income without the need for paid work, or savings and assets that guarantee that income. Bank statements from the last three months and bank certificates showing balances are the core evidence.

How long is the first non-lucrative residence permit valid?

The first permit is granted for one year, and renewals follow a two-plus-two-year rhythm, reaching the five-year mark at which long-term residence becomes available. Renewals require you to still meet the financial conditions — and the tax position changes the moment you become a Spanish resident.

What health insurance does the Spain NLV require?

A public or private policy from an insurer authorized to operate in Spain, covering all risks covered by Spain's public health system with unlimited coverage and no co-payment or deductible. It must be valid for one year and maintained for the whole stay. Travel insurance with medical assistance is explicitly not accepted.

What are the 2026 fees for the non-lucrative visa?

There are two fees: a visa fee and a residence-permit fee. From January 2026 the Washington consulate lists the visa fee at $140 for US citizens (other nationalities have different rates under reciprocity) and the residence-permit fee at $13. Fees are revised quarterly with exchange rates, so confirm the current schedule before paying.

Bottom Line

Spain's non-lucrative visa is a contract: prove you can live on €2,400 a month without working, swear it in an affidavit, insure yourself fully — and Spain gives you residence that renews 1+2+2 into long-term status. The two decisions that matter are being honest about the no-work rule (remote workers need the telework visa) and budgeting for ordinary Spanish taxation from the day you become resident.
