What the Destination Thailand Visa is

The Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) is Thailand's purpose-built visa category for people who want to live in Thailand while working remotely — rather than entering as tourists and working in the shadows of a visa exemption. It is a 5-year multiple-entry visa, meaning the visa itself stays valid for five years and you may enter and exit Thailand repeatedly on it.

Each entry permits a stay of up to 180 days, extendable once. That structure — long visa validity plus generous per-entry stays — is what makes it the legal home base for digital nomads, not just another long-stay tourist product. This guide was verified against official Thai sources in October 2026.

Who qualifies: remote workers and freelancers

The DTV is designed for remote workers and freelancers — people whose work is performed for clients or employers outside Thailand — plus approved soft-power activities. The legal test is straightforward and matches Thailand's general immigration principle: immigration law cares where your body is, not where your employer sits. If you work while physically in Thailand, you need a status that authorizes it; the DTV is that status for location-independent workers.

Conventional employment with a Thai employer is a different category entirely — that is the Non-B visa plus a separate work permit from the Department of Employment, applied for after entry on the Non-B.

The ~THB 500,000 funds requirement

The headline financial test for the DTV is proof of roughly THB 500,000 in funds. This is a threshold you demonstrate with bank statements, not a payment to the government or an embassy. The classic failure is thin documentation: statements that show the money arrived days ago read very differently from statements showing funds seasoned over months.

Keep your document discipline tight across the whole file: identical name spellings everywhere, consistent dates, funds that plausibly support the stated stay, and a cover letter that ties the file together. Officers decide on the file in front of them — a tidy, well-ordered application invites the benefit of the doubt. Fee and where to apply

The base government fee for the DTV is THB 10,000 — substantially above the THB 1,000 single-entry tourist visa, reflecting the five-year multiple-entry validity. Thai embassies and consulates abroad charge the local-currency equivalent, and because the DTV is a newer category its final amount is embassy-dependent — confirm the all-in figure with the specific mission handling your application.

Apply before travel through the Thai e-Visa system at thaievisa.go.th (Ministry of Foreign Affairs — sticker visas are largely phased out) or through your local Thai mission. Your passport must be valid for at least six months beyond arrival, and the Thailand Digital Arrival Card (TDAC) is mandatory online within 72 hours before travel — free, but it must be completed against the passport you will actually carry. Pay only through official channels: legitimate portals never ask for payment via crypto, gift cards, or personal bank transfers.

DTV vs tourist entry: the remote-work trap

Thailand's 60-day visa exemption (free, for most Western nationalities plus India and China) and the THB 1,000 single-entry tourist visa look tempting for a remote worker — but tourist entries do not authorize work of any kind. Remote work is the modern trap: immigration law cares where your body is, not where your employer sits. If you will work while in Thailand, you need work authorization for Thailand — and that is exactly the problem the DTV was created to solve.

The visa-on-arrival route (THB 2,000, 15 days at designated checkpoints) is even worse for remote workers: shorter stays, a fee per entry, and still no work authorization. For the complete entry framework, see our Thailand Visa & Entry Requirements Guide.

How the DTV compares to other Thai routes

Three Thai routes look similar but answer different questions. The DTV answers: "I work remotely for non-Thai clients and want long, legal stays" — 5 years, 180 days per entry, THB 10,000 base fee. The Non-B visa answers: "a Thai employer is hiring me" — single-entry THB 2,000 (multiple-entry THB 5,000), plus a separate work permit issued by the Department of Employment after entry. The Non-ED visa answers: "I am studying at a Thai institution" — single-entry THB 2,000, with proof-of-funds expectations that vary by embassy (no fixed nationwide figure is published).

Choosing the wrong route is the expensive mistake: a tourist file with remote-work intentions, or a DTV file with a Thai employment contract, both fail at the document stage. Match the visa to the activity, not to the fee you prefer.

Common DTV application mistakes

  • Tourist-visa thinking — assuming the 60-day exemption "covers" remote work because nobody asked at the border. It does not authorize work, and intent screening at the border is real.
  • Thin funds evidence — a fresh ~THB 500,000 balance deposited last week undermines credibility; funds should be seasoned over months.
  • Name mismatches — a passport showing one name and a bank statement showing another, with no explanation attached, stalls or sinks the file.
  • Copycat payment sites — verify the domain is thaievisa.go.th before paying; the government fee is published there, and everything above it is someone's margin.
  • Last-minute applications — processing estimates assume a clean file; apply 2–4 weeks before travel so queries do not strand your plans.
  • Passport timing — the six-month validity rule is enforced at e-Visa application; the TDAC is linked to the passport number, so renewing after completing the arrival card means redoing it.

How to verify the rules before you apply

The research method that works for the DTV: start at the Thai e-Visa system (thaievisa.go.th, Ministry of Foreign Affairs), confirm the funds and fee details with the embassy that will process your application — because both are embassy-dependent — and sanity-check against a current official advisory. Three agreeing official sources beat thirty forum posts.

Keep a complete duplicate of your submitted file — if the mission queries anything, you answer from the same papers they hold. And keep the e-Visa approval and the completed TDAC accessible (printed and on your phone): both are checked before boarding and at the border. Frequently Asked Questions

Who is the Thailand DTV for?

Remote workers, freelancers whose work is for non-Thai clients or employers, and participants in approved soft-power activities. Conventional employment with a Thai company needs the Non-B visa plus a separate work permit — the DTV is not the right route for that.

How long can I stay in Thailand on the DTV?

The DTV is a 5-year multiple-entry visa, with 180 days permitted per entry (extendable once). You may enter and exit Thailand repeatedly on the same visa while it is valid.

How much money do I need to prove for the DTV?

Roughly THB 500,000 in proven funds — demonstrated with bank statements, not paid to anyone. Statements showing funds seasoned over months are far more credible than a freshly deposited balance.

What does the Thailand DTV cost?

The base government fee is THB 10,000. Thai embassies abroad charge the local-currency equivalent, and the final amount is embassy-dependent — confirm the all-in figure with the mission handling your application. See the full 2026 fee schedule for every visa type.

Can I work remotely in Thailand on a tourist visa?

No — the 60-day exemption and tourist visas do not authorize work of any kind. The DTV is the legal route for remote workers; conventional Thai employment needs the Non-B visa plus a work permit from the Department of Employment.

The practical takeaway

Bottom Line

The DTV gives remote workers a legal 5-year home base in Thailand: 180 days per entry, ~THB 500,000 in proven funds, THB 10,000 base fee, applied before travel at thaievisa.go.th or your mission. Don't confuse it with tourist routes — those don't authorize work — and confirm the embassy-dependent funds and fee details before applying.
