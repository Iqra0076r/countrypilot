Visa Fee Changes in 2026: The Bill Behind the Headline

Every few months a visa fee headline races around the internet, and almost every time it misleads in the same way: it quotes one number, strips out the surcharge, the service charge and the dependant, and presents a proposal, a court-blocked payment or a not-yet-launched authorisation as if it were already on the receipt. This roundup does the opposite. It sorts 2026 into what you actually pay today, what has been announced but is not being collected, and what has not changed at all — and it shows, country by country, why the headline figure is rarely the figure that leaves your account.

Quick answer. In 2026 the fee changes that actually raise what you pay are Britain's 8 April table (visit visa £135, Student £558, Skilled Worker £819 for up to three years, ETA £20), Australia's 1 July charges (Student subclass 500 AUD 2,500, Temporary Graduate subclass 485 AUD 5,750) and the small US ESTA rise to $40.62 on 16 October. The Schengen consular fee did not move in 2026 (still €90 for adults), Canada's headline temporary-residence fees on the current IRCC list are unchanged in character (visitor CAD $100, study permit CAD $150, work permit CAD $155), and the two loudest American and European figures — the $100,000 H-1B payment and the €20 ETIAS authorisation — are not bills you can or must pay today: the first is blocked by court orders, the second is not yet open for applications. Always total the surcharge, the dependants and the service centre before you compare destinations.

The payable test: three buckets for every 2026 fee headline

A fee headline is not a price until it passes a simple test: is it in force, is it being collected, and does it apply to a person in your position? Most of the confusion in 2026 comes from headlines that fail one of those three checks and get repeated anyway. A parliament can set a fee that an agency has not started collecting. A court can stop a payment that a proclamation has extended. A scheme can be priced years before its portal opens. Each of those is a different kind of news, and each demands a different response from an applicant.

This roundup therefore sorts every major 2026 figure into three buckets. Bucket one is payable now: the fee appears on the authority's own published schedule and is charged at lodgement or application. Bucket two is announced but not collectible: the figure exists in law, in a proclamation or in a published future fee, yet no applicant is being asked for it today, or the system that would collect it is not operating. Bucket three is unchanged: the headline stayed put while something around it — a surcharge, a service charge, an evidence threshold — did the real damage to the budget. If you remember nothing else from this article, remember to ask which bucket a figure sits in before you add it to a spreadsheet.

The second discipline is to price the household, not the headline applicant. Governments quote per-person fees, but families apply together, students bring dependants where the rules allow, and workers are sponsored by employers who pass some charges on and absorb others. A £50 rise sounds trivial until it is multiplied by four applicants and three years of surcharge. An AUD 500 rise sounds large until you learn it applies only to the main applicant while dependants are charged separately at lower rates. The tables in this article therefore show the per-person figure and then, where the official schedule allows it, the worked total for a realistic case.

Finally, a warning about sources that this article follows throughout: every fee in this roundup is taken from an official government schedule, fee table or notice, and each source is listed at the end with the date it was accessed. Where an official source does not publish a single figure — because the charge depends on sector, passport or lodgement date — this article says so instead of inventing a neat average. Where a figure could not be verified from an official source at all, it is left out, and the gap is noted rather than papered over.

Britain's April reset: the whole table moved at once

Britain is the clearest case of bucket one in 2026, because the change was not a single visa getting dearer but an entire fee table being rewritten on one date. From 8 April 2026, the Home Office's published table raised most immigration and nationality charges by roughly 6 to 7 percent. A six-month Standard Visitor visa rose from £127 to £135. The two-year long-term visit visa rose from £475 to £506, the five-year from £848 to £903, and the ten-year from £1,059 to £1,128 — remembering that each of those long-term visas still caps each stay at six months, so the extra money buys validity, not longer visits. The Electronic Travel Authorisation, the permission for visa-exempt nationals, rose from £16 to £20, a 25 percent jump that looks dramatic as a percentage and trivial as cash until a family of four pays £80 for it.

Work and study routes moved by the same table. A Skilled Worker entry clearance for a certificate of sponsorship of up to three years rose from £769 to £819, and for more than three years from £1,519 to £1,618. The lower Health and Care Worker rates rose from £304 to £324 for up to three years. A Student or Child Student application from outside the UK rose from £524 to £558, with dependants charged at the same rate. The family route to settlement rose from £1,938 to £2,064, and indefinite leave to remain rose from £3,029 to £3,226 per applicant. None of these is the largest single fee in world immigration, but together they mean that an applicant who priced a British move from a 2025 guide is now working with the wrong numbers on every line.

The surcharge is the bill

The application fee, however, is the smaller half of most British bills, and this is where otherwise careful budgets go wrong. Most applicants for visas longer than six months must also pay the Immigration Health Surcharge, which buys access to the National Health Service and is charged at £1,035 per adult per year and £776 per year for students, dependants under 18 and Youth Mobility applicants, paid in full for the entire visa at the time of application. A three-year Skilled Worker visa therefore costs £819 in application fee plus £3,105 in surcharge, a £3,924 Home Office bill for one adult before any optional priority service. A three-year Student visa costs £558 plus £2,328 of surcharge at the student rate, £2,886 in total. Visitors, by contrast, pay no surcharge at all, which is why a visit visa and a work visa cannot be compared on their application fees alone.

The practical consequence is about timing as much as totals. Because the surcharge is paid upfront for the whole grant, a family does not spread it across the visa the way it spreads rent. Two adults on three-year Skilled Worker visas pay £6,210 in surcharge alone, plus two application fees of £819 each, before a single dependant is counted. Applicants who choose a longer initial grant to avoid a second application should therefore compare the surcharge for the longer grant against the cost of applying twice, including a second application fee and a second round of surcharge, rather than assuming longer is automatically cheaper. And because the April 2026 table changed the application fees while the surcharge rates were not part of that particular rise, older guides that bundle the two together can be wrong on one line and right on the other — another reason to check the fee table and the surcharge guidance separately, on the week you apply.

Australia's July jump: the student bill leads, the graduate bill follows

Australia's 2026 story is narrower than Britain's but sharper for the people it hits, because it concentrates on the study pathway. From 1 July 2026, the standard Student visa (subclass 500) application charge rose from AUD 2,000 to AUD 2,500 for the main applicant, a 25 percent increase that followed a rise from AUD 1,600 to AUD 2,000 a year earlier. In two years the headline student charge has therefore risen by more than half, from AUD 1,600 to AUD 2,500, and because the charge is non-refundable, a refused application now costs AUD 500 more than the same refusal cost in mid-2025. That is the kind of change that alters behaviour: it makes a weak or incomplete application materially more expensive to gamble on, and it rewards applicants who wait until their evidence is genuinely ready.

The graduate route moved by the same proportion. The Temporary Graduate visa (subclass 485) rose from AUD 4,600 to AUD 5,750 for the main applicant, also a 25 percent rise, which matters because graduates are often the least liquid applicants in the system — recently finished studying, often working part-time, and facing the graduate charge precisely when their student income has ended. The Partner visa charge rose from AUD 9,365 to AUD 11,710 in the same July adjustment. Taken together, the July changes tell a coherent story about Australian policy in 2026: the price of entry for study and for post-study work rose steeply, while the policy conversation around it focused on whether the higher charges would deter the very students the system also wants to attract.

Concessions narrow the headline

Australia is also the best illustration of why a single headline figure is never the whole story. The AUD 2,500 student charge is the standard-sector rate, but ELICOS (standalone English-language) and Non-Award applicants pay AUD 2,050, and the Home Affairs pricing table sets lower charges for some Pacific-region and Timor-Leste passport holders and for eligible ASEAN passport holders. Additional applicants are charged separately — AUD 1,530 for each additional applicant aged 18 or over and AUD 500 for each child under 18 in the standard sector — so a student's total depends on family composition as well as sector. Some onshore applicants may also face a Subsequent Temporary Application Charge. The honest way to quote the Australian student fee is therefore as a range with conditions, not as a single number, and the only figure that finally matters is the one the Department's Visa Pricing Estimator returns for your passport, sector and lodgement date.

One related Australian figure deserves a clear label because it is constantly mistaken for a fee: the AUD 29,710 a year that a single student must be able to show for living costs, plus first-year tuition and travel, is an evidence threshold, not money paid to the government. It does not appear on the visa receipt, but it determines whether the application succeeds, and it has risen alongside the charges. Budgeting for Australia in 2026 therefore means holding three numbers apart in your head: the non-refundable charge you pay at lodgement, the health cover you buy for the whole stay, and the funds you must evidence without necessarily spending. Confusing any one of those with the others is how applicants arrive AUD 500 short, or AUD 29,710 short, depending on which confusion they make.

Europe's two-speed year: the Schengen fee that stood still

Europe in 2026 runs at two speeds, and the contrast is the point. For travellers who need a Schengen short-stay visa, nothing changed: the consular fee remains €90 for adults and €45 for children aged 6 to 12, with children under 6 free, exactly as set in the June 2024 increase from €80 and €40. Some facilitation-agreement nationalities pay less — €35 in most such cases and €67.50 for Cabo Verde — and some categories, including school pupils and accompanying teachers on study trips, researchers, young non-profit delegates and family members of EU citizens under free-movement rules, pay nothing. The fee is the same whichever Schengen country issues the visa, because it is set at EU level, and it is not refunded if the application is refused. For a family of two adults and a ten-year-old, the consular bill is therefore €225 before anything else is added, a figure that has been stable for more than two years.

What did change around that stable fee is the border itself, and that is the second European speed. The EU's Entry/Exit System, which records fingerprints and facial images at the external border instead of stamping passports, completed its rollout in 2026. It costs the traveller nothing directly — there is no fee to be registered at the border — but it changes the risk profile of the trip, because overstays that might once have been hidden by a missing stamp are now recorded automatically. The Schengen story of 2026 is therefore not a price story at all for visa holders; it is a story about a fee that stayed put while enforcement got sharper, and about a separate authorisation for visa-exempt travellers that is priced but still not purchasable.

ETIAS: priced, not yet purchasable

That authorisation is ETIAS, the European Travel Information and Authorisation System, and it belongs firmly in bucket two. Its fee is settled: €20 for travellers aged 18 to 70, with no fee for under-18s and over-70s, for an authorisation valid for three years or until the passport expires. But the system is not in operation, applications are not being accepted, and the late-2026 launch target that was previously published was removed from the official site during 2026 without a replacement date being announced. Observers now expect a 2027 launch, but that is an expectation, not an official announcement, and this article does not present it as one. The practical advice follows directly: do not pay anyone for an ETIAS today, do not plan a 2026 trip around holding one, and treat any website offering an approved ETIAS now as a seller of something that does not exist. When the system does launch, the EU has promised several months' notice, and the only legitimate channel will be the official portal listed in the sources below.

America's headline trap: a $100,000 figure that is not a bill today

No 2026 fee figure has been more repeated, and more misunderstood, than the American $100,000 H-1B payment. The facts, in order, are these. A presidential proclamation of 19 September 2025, effective 21 September 2025, restricted the entry of certain H-1B specialty-occupation workers unless the petition was accompanied or supplemented by a $100,000 payment, with a national-interest exception. On 18 September 2026 a further proclamation extended that restriction for twelve months, through 21 September 2027. The payment, where it applies, is the petitioning employer's to make and document, not the worker's personal fee, and it sits on top of the ordinary USCIS filing fees rather than replacing them. So far, so dramatic — and so far, incomplete, because scope and enforceability change everything.

On scope, the payment was never aimed at every H-1B case. Agency guidance clarified that it applies to certain new petitions for beneficiaries outside the United States who do not hold a valid H-1B visa, including cases proceeding through consular notification, port-of-entry notification or pre-flight inspection. It does not apply to petitions filed before the effective date, to previously issued valid H-1B visas, or to amendments, changes of status or extensions of stay for people inside the United States who are granted that amendment, change or extension. An existing H-1B worker renewing inside the United States, or transferring employers inside the United States, is therefore not in the same position as a first-time hire recruited from abroad who must seek admission to take up the job. Any summary that describes the $100,000 as the new H-1B fee, without those qualifiers, is wrong about most H-1B workers.

On enforceability, the position at publication is that the payment is not currently being collected. A federal court in Massachusetts vacated the agency guidance implementing the payment on 8 June 2026, and on 24 July 2026 the Court of Appeals for the First Circuit declined to pause that ruling while the government's appeal proceeds. A further court order in California in September 2026 also blocked collection. USCIS has said it will comply with the court order while it considers next steps, and that it intends to collect the payment if the order is lifted. The September 2026 extension therefore extends a policy whose collection is blocked; it signals intent to revive the payment if litigation resolves in the government's favour, but it does not by itself make the payment payable today. Employers planning a consular H-1B hire should budget for the possibility of the payment returning, should not pay it where no agency is collecting it, and should take advice on their filing date and the beneficiary's location, because those two facts decide whether the payment could ever apply to them.

Two more American figures that are not bills yet

The H-1B payment is not the only American figure in bucket two. A separate $103,265 fee for cap-subject H-1B petitions was proposed by the Department of Homeland Security in August 2026 through ordinary rulemaking, as a cost-recovery charge distinct from the proclamation payment. It is a proposal, not a final rule, it would apply to petitions filed in a future year if finalised, and it does not apply today. Treating it as a current cost would overstate a 2026 budget by more than $100,000. The same discipline applies to the $250 visa integrity fee created in 2025 legislation: it is a real statutory figure, but the Department of State's published fee page carries no integrity-fee line item at the time of this article's check, and earlier official-adjacent reporting described it as not yet being collected. This article therefore does not add $250 to any American total below. If collection begins, the honest response will be to update this article, not to have pre-emptively charged readers for a fee they never owed.

Against that noisy background, the ordinary American fees are almost boring, and that is their virtue. The Department of State's published schedule charges $185 for non-petition-based nonimmigrant visas — including B visitor, F student, J exchange visitor, C transit, D crew and TN professional categories — $205 for petition-based categories including H, L, O, P, Q and R, $315 for E treaty trader and investor categories, and $265 for K fiancé(e) and spouse categories. A border crossing card costs $185 for applicants aged 15 and over and $15 for younger applicants whose parent or guardian holds or is applying for one. Students and exchange visitors also pay a separate SEVIS fee before the interview, which is not included in the $185 and is not refunded with it. None of those figures changed in the 2026 headlines, and that stability is itself useful information: for most American applicants, the fee that matters in 2026 is the one that has been on the schedule for years, plus — for a narrow, contested slice of H-1B hiring — a payment that is currently blocked.

The small figure that did move: ESTA

The one American travel figure that genuinely moved in 2026, and moved twice in small steps, is the ESTA authorisation for Visa Waiver Program travellers. ESTA cost $21 until 29 September 2025, when it rose to $40 under the 2025 budget law. It was then adjusted for inflation to $40.27 from 1 January 2026, and again to $40.62 for applications submitted on or after 16 October 2026, following a Federal Register notice published on 1 October 2026. The $40.62 is made up of a $17 travel promotion fee, a $10.62 cost-recovery fee and a $13 fee per authorisation; a denied application costs $10.62. Existing approved ESTAs are not affected — holders do not pay the difference and do not need to reapply solely because the fee changed. For a family of four, the current ESTA bill is $162.48, which is worth knowing precisely because it is the rare 2026 American increase that is small, certain and already in force, unlike the six-figure figure that dominates the headlines.

Do not pay a middleman for a permission that does not exist yet. The two most profitable confusions of 2026 are ETIAS authorisations sold before the system opens and H-1B payments demanded from workers who do not owe them. ETIAS will only ever be sold through the official EU portal, and the H-1B proclamation payment, where it applies at all, is the employer's payment to document, not a fee a worker should be asked to wire to an agent. If someone charges you for either of those today, stop and check the official source first.

Canada's quiet year: the control case in this roundup

Canada is included in this roundup precisely because it did not produce a 2026 headline, and a roundup that only lists increases would mislead by omission. On Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada's current fee list, a visitor visa (including a super visa) costs CAD $100 per person, capped at CAD $500 for a family of five or more applying together at the same time and place. A study permit, including extensions, costs CAD $150 per person. A work permit costs CAD $155 per person, with an additional CAD $100 open work permit holder fee where that applies. An electronic travel authorisation costs CAD $7. Biometrics, where required, cost CAD $85 per person, capped at CAD $170 per family. Those are the figures an applicant meets in 2026, and they are notable for their modesty next to the British and Australian totals — a single Canadian study permit at CAD $150, before biometrics, against an Australian student charge of AUD 2,500.

The Canadian lesson is about what stability does not guarantee. A low headline fee does not mean a low total, because the same stacking that inflates British and Australian bills applies here in miniature: biometrics on top of the application fee, a separate fee for restoring status (CAD $246.25 for the restoration itself, plus the permit fee where a new permit is sought at the same time), and family members each charged separately subject to the family cap that applies only to visitor visas. Nor does a stable fee mean a stable system — processing, evidence and attestation requirements can tighten while the price stays still, and a refused application still loses a non-refundable fee. Canada's role in this roundup is therefore as a control: it shows what the 2026 fee landscape looks like when governments leave the price alone, and it reminds readers that the cheapest destination on application fees is not automatically the cheapest move once health cover, tuition, travel and the cost of a refusal are counted.

Your receipt, totalled: the second and third charges

Pull the country figures together and a pattern emerges that no single fee table shows. Every major destination in this roundup charges at least twice: once for the permission itself, and once or twice more for something attached to it — a health surcharge in Britain, a service-centre charge in Schengen, biometrics in Canada, a SEVIS fee in the United States, health cover in Australia. The second charge is often larger than the first, as with the British surcharge, or it is the charge that actually varies by applicant, as with Australia's sector and passport concessions. Any comparison that lines up only the first charges — £135 against €90 against CAD $100 — is comparing different things and calling it a ranking.

Worked totals make the point concrete. A single adult on a three-year British Skilled Worker visa pays £3,924 in Home Office charges (£819 plus £3,105 of surcharge). A single Australian student pays AUD 2,500 at lodgement and must evidence AUD 29,710 a year in living costs plus tuition, which is a different kind of money but a real condition of success. Before you pay: five checks that stop the expensive mistake

First, check the lodgement date rule. Britain's new table applies from 8 April 2026, Australia's charges from 1 July 2026, and America's ESTA change from 16 October 2026; in each case the fee is set by when you apply, not when you started preparing. An application lodged a day before a rise is charged at the old rate, and one lodged a day after is charged at the new one, and no authority will re-price it because your documents took longer than expected. Second, check who pays: the H-1B proclamation payment is an employer payment where it applies, the British surcharge is per applicant including dependants, and the Australian student charge varies by sector and passport, so a friend's receipt is not your price.

Third, check whether the figure is being collected at all. If a fee appears in a headline but not on the authority's published fee page — as with the $250 integrity fee — or if its system is not open — as with ETIAS — do not pay it and do not budget it as settled. Fourth, check the second charge before you celebrate a low first one: the British surcharge, the Schengen service-centre fee, Canadian biometrics and American SEVIS costs are where budgets actually break. Fifth, keep the receipt and the schedule together: screenshot or save the official fee page on the day you pay, because fee tables change in April and July with little fanfare, and the page you checked last month is not evidence of the fee you will be charged next month. If a later charge differs from the schedule you saved, that saved page is also the fastest way to query it.

One final honesty note, because this roundup is dated and fee schedules are not. Two areas were deliberately left without a figure because no official figure could be verified to the standard this article sets itself. No verified 2026 fee change was found for New Zealand or Japan in the official sources checked for this article, so neither destination is given a 2026 figure here rather than a borrowed one. And where an authority publishes a range or a conditional charge — most obviously Australia's student concessions — this article gives the range and the condition instead of a single invented average. A roundup that admits its gaps is more useful than one that fills them with plausible numbers, because the cost of a wrong fee figure is not embarrassment; it is a refused application, a rejected filing for underpayment, or a family budget that fails at the payment page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which 2026 visa fee change affects the most applicants?

Britain's 8 April 2026 table, because it moved almost every route at once: a six-month visit visa rose from £127 to £135, a Student visa from £524 to £558, and a Skilled Worker entry clearance for up to three years from £769 to £819, with the health surcharge on top.

Do I have to pay the US $100,000 H-1B fee?

Not as a settled bill for every applicant, and not today on the evidence available at publication. The payment was aimed at certain new H-1B petitions for beneficiaries outside the United States, it did not cover amendments, changes of status or extensions inside the United States, and collection is currently blocked by court orders, including a June 2026 vacatur that an appeals court left in place in July 2026. The September 2026 extension runs to 21 September 2027, but an extension does not by itself restart collection while those orders stand.

Is the US $250 visa integrity fee payable now?

Treat it as a contingent future cost, not today's bill. The fee was created in 2025 legislation, but the US Department of State's published fee page carries no integrity-fee line item at the time of this article's check, so do not add $250 to a budget as if it were already being collected. Re-check the fee page on the week you apply.

Can I buy an ETIAS authorisation today?

No. The European travel authorisation is priced at €20 for travellers aged 18 to 70, with no fee for under-18s and over-70s, but applications are not open and no new launch date has been published after the late-2026 target was dropped. Any website selling you an approved ETIAS today is not the official portal.

Why is my UK visa bill much higher than the visa fee?

Because most longer UK routes carry the Immigration Health Surcharge on top of the application fee: £1,035 per adult per year and £776 per year for students, dependants under 18 and Youth Mobility applicants, paid in full for the whole visa. A three-year Skilled Worker visa at £819 therefore carries £3,105 of surcharge, for a £3,924 Home Office bill before any priority service or centre charges.

Why did my Australian student visa cost more than a friend's?

Because Australia does not have one student charge. The standard subclass 500 charge from 1 July 2026 is AUD 2,500 for the main applicant, but ELICOS and Non-Award applicants pay AUD 2,050, and additional applicants are charged AUD 1,530 for adults and AUD 500 for children in the standard sector, with lower charges for some Pacific, Timor-Leste and ASEAN passport holders. The charge is set by the lodgement date and by sector and passport, so two students can legitimately pay different amounts.

Does a Schengen visa cost the same in every country?

The consular fee does: €90 for adults and €45 for children aged 6 to 12, with children under 6 free, and reduced €35 and €67.50 rates for some facilitation-agreement nationalities. What differs is everything around it: external service centres add their own charge, and national long-stay visa fees are set country by country, so the consular fee is the floor of the bill, not the total.

Bottom Line

Judge every 2026 fee headline by the payable test before you budget it: Britain's April table and Australia's July charges are real bills you pay at application, America's ESTA rise to $40.62 is a small real bill, Schengen's €90 consular fee and Canada's CAD $100 visitor fee are stable bills with service and biometric charges on top, and the $100,000 H-1B payment and the €20 ETIAS authorisation are not bills you pay today — the first because court orders block its collection, the second because its system is not open. Total the surcharge, the dependants and the service centre, check the official fee page on the week you apply, and never let a headline, however large, stand in for the receipt.
