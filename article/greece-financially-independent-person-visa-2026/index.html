Greece Financially Independent Person Visa 2026: The €3,500 I.8 Permit

By Nadeem Tanwari, Founder of CountryPilotReviewed by CountryPilot editorial deskPublished October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

There is no retirement visa in Greek law. What retirees actually apply for is the I.8 permit for financially independent persons — a three-year, renewable residence built on one legal test: can you support yourself from lawful resources that do not depend on working in Greece? Here is the 2026 framework, straight from the Immigration Code.

Greece's "retirement visa" is legally the I.8 residence permit for financially independent persons under article 163(8) of the Immigration Code (Law 5038/2023). It requires €3,500 a month in stable, lawful means for the principal applicant (€42,000 a year), plus 20% for a spouse and 15% per child. The permit runs three years, renews in three-year periods, confers no work rights on anyone in the household, and starts with a national type-D visa from a Greek consulate.

There is no Greek retirement visa — there is the I.8 permit

Start with the distinction that decides everything, because getting it wrong sends you to the wrong consulate queue. Greek law contains no residence category called a retirement visa. The route everyone is searching for is the residence permit for third-country nationals with sufficient means of subsistence, coded I.8 and granted under article 163(8) of the Immigration Code, Law 5038/2023. It suits retirees perfectly well — but it is equally available to a 40-year-old living on dividends, because the legal test is not age or retirement status. It is independence of means combined with the absence of economic activity in Greece.

Understanding that distinction sets the terms of the whole application. The permit is a residence permit, not an investment permit: there is no required property purchase, no capital to be placed in Greece, no transfer to the State. What the applicant must show instead is that lawful, stable, and sufficient means exist and will continue to exist. The authority that decides is the Secretary of the competent Decentralised Administration — not a consulate clerk improvising, but the office named in the Code.

This framing also explains why the I.8 permit sits apart from Greece's better-known Golden Visa. The Golden Visa is an investment route that does not require living in Greece. The I.8 permit requires no investment but is designed for people who will actually reside there and live on independent means. They answer different questions: one asks what capital you bring, the other asks what life you will live.

The €3,500 threshold: set by ministerial decision, applied exactly

The Immigration Code itself does not name a number. The amount is set by ministerial decision — and under Joint Ministerial Decision 225679/2024, the principal applicant must show at least €3,500 a month in 2026. The figure rises by 20% for a spouse (€700 a month, €4,200 for the household) and by 15% for each child (€525 a month). A principal applicant with a spouse and two children plans around €5,250 a month, or €63,000 a year.

These are eligibility thresholds, not fees, and the authorities assess them with a forensic eye. Periodic income — a pension, dividends — is assessed monthly; capital such as bank deposits is assessed against the annual figure over the life of the permit. The evidence has to be consistent, traceable to a lawful source, and capable of withstanding scrutiny of its origin. A balance that appears shortly before the application, with no explanation of where it came from, is the commonest reason files stall.

Two useful flexibilities sit inside article 163(8). First, where family members accompany the applicant, the resources requirement may be met either by each family member individually or cumulatively by the family as a whole — so one strong household income can carry the file. Second, there is a provision almost everyone overlooks: third-country nationals lawfully resident in Greece who receive a pension from a Greek public insurance fund can renew the I.8 permit whatever the amount of that pension, without meeting the resources threshold. For anyone with a Greek working history, the €3,500 test can become irrelevant at renewal.

The work ban is the suitability test, not a formality

Article 163(8)(c) is unambiguous: the permit confers no right to dependent employment or to independent economic activity of any kind in Greece — and the restriction applies to the holder, to the spouse or partner, and to every accompanying family member. This is the provision that decides whether the permit is the right one, and it should be tested before anything else is prepared.

Receiving income is not the same as working. Collecting a pension, dividends, or rent from assets held abroad is entirely consistent with the permit. Managing an operating business, sitting on a board with executive functions, consulting for clients, or providing services while resident in Greece is not — and the line between passive ownership and active involvement is fact-sensitive. Anyone who intends to keep working remotely for a foreign employer or clients from Greece should look instead at Greece's separate digital-nomad regime, a distinct title under the Code with its own conditions. Choosing the I.8 permit because the threshold is met, when the applicant in fact intends to work, creates a file that fails at renewal — when the authority may examine whether the conditions were genuinely maintained, including whether the holder engaged in prohibited work.

Note: the work ban covers the whole household. A spouse who plans to take a local job or freelance from the Greek address needs their own appropriate permit — the I.8 file cannot shelter a working partner.

The 183-day rule is a myth — but absence still matters

A great deal of published guidance states that I.8 holders must spend at least 183 days a year in Greece to keep or renew the permit. That figure does not appear in article 163(8), which governs the permit, nor in article 11, which governs its renewal. It derives from an amendment made in 2020 to the former immigration law, which the 2023 Code replaced. The 183-day threshold belongs principally to the analysis of tax residence — not to this permit.

That does not make absence irrelevant. Personal attendance is required for biometrics and may be required for interview; a real Greek address must be maintained; and long periods abroad will count against any later application for long-term residence status or Greek citizenship, both of which turn on actual, continuous residence. Anyone intending to spend most of the year outside Greece should take advice on those consequences before relying on the permit. The I.8 is designed for people who will actually reside in Greece — treat the myth as false but the underlying point as true.

The two-step process: consulate visa, then the ministry's electronic platform

The route runs in a fixed sequence. First, the applicant applies in person to the competent Greek consulate in the country of lawful residence for a national long-stay visa, type D, corresponding to the I.8 permit. The consulate may interview the applicant and request originals. A short-stay tourist visa does not convert into this permit — do not assume a scouting trip can become an application.

After entering Greece on the national visa, the applicant — or a lawyer holding the appropriate authority — files the residence-permit application electronically through the Ministry of Migration and Asylum's platform, before the visa expires. Once a complete application is accepted, a certificate of submission is issued: it evidences lawful stay while the application is examined, but it does not carry the right of free movement within the Schengen area, so travel during that period needs planning. The applicant then attends in person for biometrics, and after approval the card is issued for three years. Processing times vary considerably by consulate and regional authority, and no responsible adviser should promise a fixed end-to-end timetable.

The core file generally comprises a valid passport, the prescribed application and photographs, proof of sufficient lawful means, the criminal-record and medical certificates required for the national visa, evidence of health insurance, the civil-status documents of accompanying family members, and proof of a Greek address. Two standards are strict: the health insurance must be a full policy suited to residence-permit purposes — a short-term travel policy will not do — and foreign public documents must bear an apostille where the issuing state is party to the Hague Convention (or be legalised through the consular chain where it is not) and must be officially translated into Greek. The detailed checklist is set by Joint Ministerial Decision 95391/2024, as amended, and by the competent consulate — confirm both at the time of filing.

Renewal: what changes after three years

The permit runs for three years and may be renewed for periods of equal length — the longest initial term of any European retirement route in this guide. The renewal application must be filed within the two months before the permit expires, under article 11 of the Code. Since the 2026 amendments introduced by Law 5275/2026, a late application may still be lodged up to three months after expiry, subject to a fine of €100 for each month of delay — earlier guidance describing a shorter grace period reflects the text before that amendment.

Renewal requires fresh proof that the means, the insurance, and the other conditions continue to be met. This is the quiet discipline of the I.8: the €3,500 test is not a one-time hurdle but a continuing condition, and the authority may examine whether it was genuinely maintained across the three years. Begin renewal preparation three to four months before expiry — the filing window opens two months out, and a file assembled in a week reads like one.

On cost, the public charges are fixed and worth budgeting from day one: the national type-D visa is €180 per applicant at the consulate; the initial residence permit is €1,000 for the principal (€150 per adult family member) plus €16 per residence card; renewal costs the same €1,000 plus €16. To those must be added the third-party costs no guide can fix in advance: the health-insurance premium, certificates, apostilles and legalisation, official translations, and powers of attorney where used.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a retirement visa in Greece?

Not as a legal category. The route people call the retirement visa is the I.8 residence permit for financially independent persons, granted under article 163(8) of the Greek Immigration Code (Law 5038/2023). It is open to retirees, but it is not limited to them — the legal test is independence of means, not age.

How much income do I need for the Greece FIP permit in 2026?

At least €3,500 net per month for the principal applicant, set by Joint Ministerial Decision 225679/2024 — €42,000 a year. Add 20% for a spouse (€700/month) and 15% for each child (€525/month). The law allows the family threshold to be met either individually or cumulatively by the household as a whole.

Can I work in Greece on the financially independent person permit?

No. Article 163(8) gives the permit no right to dependent employment or to independent economic activity of any kind in Greece — and the ban covers the holder, the spouse or partner, and every accompanying family member. Collecting a pension, dividends, or rent from abroad is consistent with the permit; consulting, board roles with executive functions, or remote work are not.

Do I have to buy property in Greece for the FIP permit?

No — there is no investment requirement of any kind: no property purchase, no capital placed in Greece, no transfer to the State. You must show a genuine Greek address, but a lease is sufficient if it is real and correctly declared.

Do I have to spend 183 days a year in Greece to keep the permit?

The provisions governing the permit (article 163(8)) and its renewal (article 11) contain no day count — the 183-day figure comes from an older immigration law replaced by the 2023 Code, and it belongs principally to tax-residence analysis. That said, long absences still count against later applications for long-term residence or citizenship, which turn on actual continuous residence.

What does the Greece FIP route cost in 2026?

The national type-D visa costs €180 per applicant at the consulate. The initial residence permit costs €1,000 for the principal (€150 per adult family member) plus €16 per residence card. Renewal costs the same €1,000 plus €16, and a late renewal — now possible up to three months after expiry under the 2026 amendments — carries a fine of €100 per month of delay.

What can I do if my FIP application is refused?

Under article 16 of the Immigration Code you may apply for reconsideration to the authority that issued the decision within two months of its service, with a €50 fee, and the authority must decide within thirty days. An annulment action before the competent administrative court is also available. Time limits run from service of the decision, so act immediately.

Bottom Line

Greece's I.8 permit is the retirement route for people who read the law: no investment required, three years of renewable residence, but a €3,500-a-month means test that never goes away and a work ban that covers the whole household. The decisions that matter are proving your means are lawful, stable, and traceable; keeping the no-work promise honest (remote workers need the digital-nomad title instead); and starting renewal preparation months before the three-year card expires.
