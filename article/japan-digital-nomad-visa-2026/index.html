Japan Digital Nomad Visa 2026: ¥10M Income, 6 Months, No Renewal

Japan's digital nomad visa is the most honestly named residency product in Asia: it tells you, right in the rules, that it is a six-month guest pass for high earners, not a slow path to living in Tokyo. If you read it that way, it is genuinely useful. If you read anything else into it, it will disappoint you.

The short version: Japan's digital nomad visa (officially "Designated Activities for Digital Nomad," launched March 2024) lets qualifying remote workers stay up to 6 months. You need annual income of at least ¥10 million from non-Japanese sources, private medical travel insurance with at least ¥10 million in medical coverage, and a passport from a country holding both visa-exemption arrangements and a tax convention with Japan. The stay cannot be extended, no residence card is issued, and you may reapply only after six months outside the country.

Most digital nomad visas are written like invitations. Japan's reads like a contract with a timer on it, and that is precisely what makes it worth understanding properly. The Immigration Services Agency of Japan published the requirements in plain, unforgiving language: six months, no extension, income proven at the time of application, insurance or nothing. There is no points system to game and no renewal strategy to plan. You either clear three gates — income, insurance, nationality — or you do not apply.

This guide is built for the person actually deciding: a remote earner weighing whether six months in Japan is worth the paperwork, the insurance premium, and the discipline of a hard exit date. Every figure below comes from Japan's own immigration and foreign-affairs authorities, checked on 15 October 2026.

The ¥10 million line decides everything

Of everything in this visa, the income threshold is the part that does the most filtering, and the part applicants misunderstand most often. The official requirement is that your annual income must be at least 10 million yen at the time of application. That phrasing matters: it is a snapshot test, not a lifetime achievement award. A strong last twelve months documented in tax returns, employment contracts, or bank statements is the evidence the authorities expect.

Two subtleties hide inside that single number. First, the income must come from non-Japanese sources. The whole design philosophy of this visa is that you bring foreign money into Japan without touching the domestic labour market — if your income comes from Japanese companies or clients, you belong on a standard work visa, not this one. Freelancers, salaried remote employees, business owners, and creators all qualify in principle, provided the money originates outside Japan.

Second, the threshold is about income, not wealth. A seven-figure savings account does not substitute for the ¥10 million annual income figure. This catches out more applicants than any other detail: people with substantial savings but modest current earnings discover the visa simply was not designed for them. At 2026 exchange rates the threshold works out to roughly sixty-odd thousand US dollars, but do not convert it in your head and round — the test is in yen, and the yen's mood in the month you apply is part of the calculation. If your home-currency earnings sit close to the line, give yourself a buffer rather than a coin flip.

Note: If your income is variable — freelance projects, creator revenue, quarterly bonuses — assemble a full twelve months of documentation, not just your best quarter. Consular officers read consistency, not peaks.

Six months means six months

The Immigration Services Agency states it without decoration: the period of stay with this status cannot be extended. Your permitted stay must not exceed six months within a year. When the six months end, you leave — and you become eligible to apply for the status again only after six months outside Japan.

This single rule reshapes the entire planning exercise. Most nomad visas let you renew, convert, or at least linger while you figure out the next step. Japan's gives you a fixed window with a clean exit baked in. That makes it an excellent visa for a defined project — a sabbatical, a product launch, a half-year of deliberate exploration — and a poor visa for anyone hoping to drift into long-term life in Japan. There is no pathway from this status to a work visa or permanent residence; it is a cul-de-sac by design, and an honest one.

The practical consequence: build your exit before you build your entry. Know your departure date when you apply, keep your onward arrangements flexible enough to survive a delayed start, and treat the six-month mark as a wall rather than a suggestion. Within your six months, re-entry to Japan is possible with the appropriate re-entry permit, so a side trip to Seoul or Taipei does not burn your remaining time — but the clock does not pause while you are away.

The eligibility list is two lists stapled together

Japan did not draw up a bespoke list of friendly countries for this visa. It stapled together two lists it already had: countries and regions covered by its visa-exemption arrangements for temporary visitors, and countries party to one of Japan's tax conventions. You must hold the nationality of a place that sits on both. That is why the result looks slightly arbitrary — it is the intersection of two bureaucratic facts, not a popularity ranking.

The intersection lands at roughly fifty countries and regions, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, South Korea, and most of the European Union, plus others across Asia, the Americas, and the Middle East. The exact count shifts as treaties change, so the number matters less than the test: if your country has visa-free short-term entry to Japan and a tax treaty with Japan, you are likely in. If it has only one of the two — Taiwan, for example, sits outside the tax-convention side — the door stays closed for the main applicant.

One useful wrinkle: the eligible-country list for accompanying spouses and children is broader than the main applicant's. Family members who could not qualify on their own passport can still join you, provided each of them carries the same level of insurance. Check the Immigration Services Agency's current published list before you apply rather than relying on any blog's copy of it — this is the one detail most likely to have moved since you last looked.

No residence card, no city registration: what "temporary" costs you

Here is the detail that surprises people after the paperwork is done: no residence card is issued under this status. In Japan's system, the residence card is the key that unlocks ordinary adult life — city-hall registration, the national health insurance system, and, in practice, bank accounts, long-term rental contracts, and phone plans. Without it, you remain administratively a guest for the entire stay.

That is not a flaw in the visa; it is the visa telling you what it is. Plan accordingly: monthly or serviced apartments rather than standard leases, international banking and multi-currency cards rather than a Japanese account, a travel SIM or pocket wifi rather than a domestic phone contract. None of this is fatal — plenty of people live comfortably in Japan for six months on exactly this setup — but it is expensive to discover mid-stay. Budget for the friction: serviced accommodation in Tokyo or Osaka costs meaningfully more than a standard lease would, and that premium is part of the real price of this visa.

The upside of the temporariness is administrative lightness. There is no address to register, no pension or health-insurance enrollment to navigate, and no departure procedure beyond leaving. You arrive, you live, you leave. For a six-month chapter, that simplicity has genuine value.

The insurance bar is higher than the visa fee

The insurance requirement is the second great filter, and it is stricter than it first appears. You must hold private medical travel insurance covering death, injury, and illness for the duration of your stay — and the compensation for medical treatment for injury or illness must be at least ¥10 million. Read that again: the medical coverage alone must clear ¥10 million, roughly the same order of magnitude as the income threshold itself.

That rules out most off-the-shelf annual travel policies and credit-card travel perks, which typically cap medical cover far below that level. You are shopping in the international private medical insurance market — the same market long-term expats use — and you need the policy documents to prove it at application time, not after you land. Because Japan's National Health Insurance is not available to holders of this status, there is no public-system backstop; the private policy is the entire safety net.

Each accompanying family member needs their own cover at the same level. For a family of three or four, the insurance line in the budget can rival a month of rent, so price it before you commit to the move rather than after. The policy must cover the full intended stay — a three-month policy for a six-month application will not survive scrutiny.

Six months and the tax question

The visa's six-month ceiling interacts neatly with Japan's tax residency rules, and that is not a coincidence. A stay of under six months, without a home (domicile) in Japan, normally keeps you a non-resident for Japanese tax purposes — which means Japan does not tax your worldwide income. But "normally" is doing heavy lifting in that sentence, and the exceptions are the parts people get wrong.

The important one: pay for work performed in Japan is still Japanese-source income, even if your employer sits abroad and your salary lands in a foreign account. A tax treaty between Japan and your home country can exempt that income, but only if you meet the treaty's conditions — typically that your stay does not exceed 183 days and that the remuneration is not borne by a Japanese entity. Six months is uncomfortably close to 183 days, which is exactly why the treaty's other conditions (and your day count) deserve professional attention rather than a confident guess.

The honest advice: this visa keeps you out of Japan's worldwide tax net in the ordinary case, but the ordinary case has edges. If your situation has any edge at all — equity compensation vesting while you are there, a home you keep in Japan, split-year complications at home — pay for an hour of a cross-border tax adviser's time. It is the cheapest insurance in this entire process.

Who this visa is actually for — and who should pick a different Japan route

Strip away the marketing language and the visa has a clear ideal user: an established remote earner, clearing ¥10 million a year from foreign sources, who wants a defined, high-quality six months in Japan and is disciplined enough to leave when it ends. Sabbatical professionals, founders between chapters, creators with a Japan-shaped project, couples who can both work remotely — these are the people the visa was surveyed into existence for. The Immigration Services Agency designed the six-month length around what digital nomads themselves said they wanted: longer than the 90-day tourist ceiling, shorter than a life decision.

It is the wrong visa for almost everyone else. If you want to settle in Japan, you want a work visa, a highly skilled professional visa, or the startup route — not a status that forbids extension by name. If your income is below ¥10 million, no amount of savings or enthusiasm bridges the gap. If you need Japanese clients to make the economics work, the visa explicitly excludes that. And if you are hoping the six months will somehow turn into something permanent, read the rule once more: the period of stay cannot be extended. Japan is telling you the truth about this product. Believe it, plan around it, and it can be one of the best six months of your working life.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the Japan digital nomad visa be extended or renewed?

No. The Immigration Services Agency states plainly that the period of stay cannot be extended. The visa allows up to 6 months within a year, and you can only apply for it again after six months outside Japan.

What income do I need for the Japan digital nomad visa in 2026?

Your annual income must be at least ¥10 million at the time of application, earned from non-Japanese sources. The rule is about income, not savings: a large bank balance does not substitute for the income threshold.

Can freelancers and YouTubers apply, or only employees?

Freelancers, business owners, and creators can apply. The official status covers both remote work under a foreign employment contract and providing services or selling goods to people in foreign countries using information technology. The critical condition is that all work is for clients or employers outside Japan.

Can I bring my spouse and children?

Yes. Spouses and children can accompany you under the companion status, but each family member needs their own private medical travel insurance, and their eligible-country list is broader than the main applicant's.

Will I pay Japanese income tax on the nomad visa?

A six-month stay without a home in Japan normally keeps you a non-resident for tax purposes, but pay received for work performed in Japan is still Japanese-source income. Tax treaties can exempt it if you meet their conditions, typically a stay of 183 days or less. Get cross-border tax advice for your situation.

Can I open a bank account or sign a long-term lease?

No residence card is issued under this status, and there is no city-hall registration, which in practice makes opening a bank account, signing long-term leases, and getting a phone contract difficult. Plan for monthly rentals and international banking instead.

Can I do any work for Japanese clients while there?

No. The status expressly excludes work performed under a contract with a Japanese public or private organization. All of your income must come from outside Japan.

Bottom Line

Japan's digital nomad visa is a precision tool, not a lifestyle promise: clear ¥10 million a year from foreign income, carry ¥10 million in medical cover, hold an eligible passport, and you get six excellent months with a hard, honest exit date. Apply only if the temporariness is the feature you want — because it is the one feature you cannot negotiate.
